Question 18

Aprepitant is used in the management of chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting. What is its mechanism of action?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Aprepitant belongs to a class of antiemetic drugs that work by blocking the effects of substance P, a key neuropeptide involved in the vomiting reflex. Substance P exerts its effects by binding to the neurokinin-1 (NK1) receptor, which is found in high concentrations in the areas of the brainstem that control nausea and vomiting (the nucleus tractus solitarius and area postrema). Chemotherapy can cause the release of substance P, which then activates these NK1 receptors, leading to both acute and, particularly, delayed nausea and vomiting. Aprepitant is a selective high-affinity NK1 receptor antagonist. By blocking this receptor, it prevents substance P from binding and initiating the emetic signal. It is often used in combination with a 5-HT3 antagonist and a corticosteroid for the prevention of chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, especially with highly emetogenic regimens.

Question 128

Variation in how individuals respond to different doses of a drug is determined using which type of data?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

A quantal dose-response curve is a graph that plots the percentage of a population of individuals that responds to a specific drug effect against the log of the dose administered. This type of curve is used to examine the variation in sensitivity to a drug across a population. It allows for the determination of key parameters like the ED₅₀ (the dose that produces the desired effect in 50% of the population). The slope of the curve indicates the degree of variability; a steep slope means that most individuals respond to a narrow range of doses, while a shallow slope indicates a large variation in sensitivity, with some individuals responding to very low doses and others requiring very high doses. This information is crucial for determining the appropriate starting dose and dose range for a drug in clinical practice. The therapeutic index is a measure of drug safety, not variability.

Question 129

Which of the following features is shown by a drug that binds extensively to plasma proteins?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

When a drug enters the bloodstream, a fraction of it binds to plasma proteins, primarily albumin. This binding is reversible, and an equilibrium exists between the protein-bound drug and the free (unbound) drug. Only the free fraction is pharmacologically active and available for metabolism and excretion. Glomerular filtration in the kidney is a passive process that filters only the free, unbound drug from the plasma. The drug molecules that are bound to large proteins like albumin are too large to pass through the glomerular filtration barrier. Therefore, a drug with a high degree of plasma protein binding will have a low fraction of free drug available for filtration, resulting in a lower rate of glomerular filtration (less GFR). High protein binding also tends to reduce the volume of distribution (C) by restricting the drug to the vascular compartment and increases the potential for drug interactions (B).

Question 130

What is the mechanism of action of oseltamivir?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Oseltamivir (Tamiflu) is an antiviral drug that is active against both influenza A and influenza B viruses. Its mechanism of action is the selective inhibition of the viral enzyme neuraminidase. Neuraminidase is a glycoprotein located on the surface of the influenza virus. This enzyme plays a critical role in the final stage of the viral replication cycle. It cleaves the sialic acid residues that bind the newly formed viral particles to the surface of the infected host cell. This cleavage is necessary for the release of the progeny virions, allowing them to spread and infect other respiratory epithelial cells. By inhibiting neuraminidase, oseltamivir prevents the release of new viruses from the infected cell, thus halting the spread of the infection within the respiratory tract. To be effective, the drug must be started early in the course of the illness, typically within 48 hours of symptom onset.
