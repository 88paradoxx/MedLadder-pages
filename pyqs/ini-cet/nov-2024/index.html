Question 1

What deficit results from an injury to the posterior interosseous nerve?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Loss of finger extension The posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) is the deep, purely motor branch of the radial nerve. It innervates the muscles in the extensor compartment of the forearm. A crucial anatomical point is that the radial nerve gives off branches to the main wrist extensors (extensor carpi radialis longus and brevis) before the PIN is formed. Therefore, in an isolated PIN palsy, wrist extension is largely preserved, and a complete wrist drop (A) does not occur. However, the PIN supplies the extensor digitorum communis, extensor indicis, extensor digiti minimi, and extensor pollicis longus. Consequently, injury to this nerve results in a characteristic "finger drop," which is an inability to extend the fingers and thumb at the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints. Adduction of the fingers (D) is a function of the ulnar nerve.

Question 2

Which cranial nerve is not contained within the superior orbital fissure?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

II The superior orbital fissure is a crucial opening in the sphenoid bone that connects the middle cranial fossa with the orbit. It transmits several important neurovascular structures. The contents include the oculomotor nerve (CN III), the trochlear nerve (CN IV), the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve (CN V1), and the abducens nerve (CN VI). It also transmits the superior ophthalmic vein. In contrast, the optic nerve (CN II) and the ophthalmic artery pass through a separate, more medially located foramen called the optic canal. Therefore, the optic nerve is not a content of the superior orbital fissure. This anatomical separation is a frequently tested point in neuroanatomy, distinguishing the pathways for vision from those controlling eye movement and sensation.

Question 3

Question 4

The first migration of epiblast cells leads to the formation of which germ layer?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Endoderm During the third week of embryonic development, a process called gastrulation transforms the bilaminar embryonic disc into a trilaminar disc. This process begins at the primitive streak, where cells of the superficial layer, the epiblast, proliferate and migrate inwards. The very first wave of epiblast cells to invaginate through the primitive streak pushes aside and completely replaces the cells of the underlying hypoblast layer. This newly formed deep layer is the definitive embryonic endoderm. Shortly after, a second wave of migrating epiblast cells positions itself between the epiblast and the new endoderm, forming the middle layer, the mesoderm. The remaining cells of the epiblast that do not migrate constitute the ectoderm. Therefore, the endoderm is the first of the three primary germ layers to be formed from migrating epiblast cells.

Question 5

While receiving the second dose of the COVID vaccine, a patient developed a flabby, flattened, and uneven shoulder. A nurse observed a loss of the normal shoulder contour. Which anatomical structure is likely to have been damaged, and could this injury have been avoided?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Axillary nerve The rounded contour of the shoulder is primarily formed by the deltoid muscle. The symptoms described—a flabby, flat, and asymmetrical shoulder—are indicative of deltoid muscle atrophy or paralysis. The deltoid muscle is responsible for arm abduction and is innervated by the axillary nerve. This nerve courses around the surgical neck of the humerus, approximately 5-7 cm below the acromion. Question 8

Which of the following types of transport does not require ATP? A. Paracellular transport B. Simple diffusion C. Facilitated diffusion D. Na+/K+ ATPase E. Symport/Antiport

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

A, B, C Cellular transport can be active or passive. Active transport requires energy, typically from ATP hydrolysis, to move substances against their concentration gradient. Na+/K+ ATPase (D) is the quintessential example of primary active transport. Symport/Antiport (E) are forms of secondary active transport, which use the energy stored in an electrochemical gradient (created by primary active transport) rather than direct ATP hydrolysis. In contrast, passive transport does not require metabolic energy. Simple diffusion (B) is the movement of substances directly across the membrane down their concentration gradient. Facilitated diffusion (C) uses a channel or carrier protein to move substances down their gradient. Paracellular transport (A) is the movement of substances through the tight junctions between cells, also driven by electrochemical gradients. Thus, paracellular transport, simple diffusion, and facilitated diffusion are all passive processes that do not directly consume ATP.

Question 9

What factors contribute to the latent period in a single muscle twitch? 1 Excitation-contraction coupling 2 Time interval from the point of stimulus to the onset of contraction 3 Neuromuscular junction (NMJ) transmission 4 Transfer of calcium back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum via SERCA

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

1,2,3 The latent period is defined as the time interval from the point of stimulus to the onset of muscle contraction (2). It is a brief delay during which a series of electrochemical and mechanical events occur before tension develops. These events include the transmission of the action potential across the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) (3), including the release of acetylcholine and generation of an end-plate potential. Following this, the process of excitation-contraction coupling (1) takes place. This involves the propagation of the muscle action potential along the sarcolemma and into the T-tubules, triggering the release of calcium ions from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and their binding to troponin, which initiates the cross-bridge cycle. The transfer of calcium back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (4) is the process that causes muscle relaxation and occurs after the contraction phase, not during the latent period.

Question 10

Which of the following helps to decrease the Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Increasing hydrostatic pressure of Bowman's capsule The Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) is determined by the balance of Starling forces across the glomerular capillaries. Filtration is favored by the glomerular hydrostatic pressure and opposed by the glomerular oncotic pressure and the hydrostatic pressure in Bowman's capsule. Therefore, to decrease GFR, one must either decrease the forces favoring filtration or increase the forces opposing it. Increasing glomerular hydrostatic pressure (C) or decreasing glomerular oncotic pressure (B) would both increase GFR. Decreasing the filtration constant (A) would decrease GFR, but this is a physiological property, not a dynamic pressure. Increasing the hydrostatic pressure of Bowman's capsule (D) directly opposes the hydrostatic pressure in the glomerulus, reducing the net filtration pressure and thereby decreasing GFR. This can occur clinically in cases of urinary tract obstruction, such as from a kidney stone or enlarged prostate.

Question 11

Static 2-point discrimination is primarily assessed by which of the following receptors?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Merkel's discs Two-point discrimination is the ability to perceive two simultaneous points of contact on the skin as distinct. This sensory modality requires mechanoreceptors with small, well-defined receptive fields and high spatial resolution. Merkel's discs are slow-adapting mechanoreceptors that are densely packed in areas like the fingertips and lips. They are specialized for detecting fine touch, pressure, and texture, and their small receptive fields make them the primary receptors responsible for static two-point discrimination. Meissner's corpuscles (B) are rapidly adapting receptors with slightly larger receptive fields, involved in detecting light touch and flutter. Pacinian corpuscles (C) are also rapidly adapting but have very large receptive fields and are responsible for sensing deep pressure and high-frequency vibration, making them unsuitable for fine spatial discrimination.

Question 12

Which of the following statements accurately describes physiological dead space?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Inadequately perfused alveoli Dead space refers to the volume of inspired air that does not participate in gas exchange. Anatomical dead space is the volume of the conducting airways. Physiological dead space is the sum of the anatomical dead space and the alveolar dead space. Alveolar dead space is composed of alveoli that are ventilated but not perfused with blood, or are inadequately perfused relative to their ventilation (high V/Q ratio). In a healthy individual, alveolar dead space is negligible, so physiological dead space equals anatomical dead space. However, in lung diseases (e.g., pulmonary embolism), perfusion can be impaired, increasing alveolar dead space and making physiological dead space greater than anatomical dead space (making C incorrect). Physiological dead space is measured using Bohr's equation with expired CO2, not by simple spirometry (D).

Question 13

Peripheral chemoreceptors exhibit their greatest sensitivity in which of the following conditions?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Hypoxia The body has two sets of chemoreceptors that regulate breathing. Central chemoreceptors in the medulla are highly sensitive to changes in PaCO2 (hypercapnia) and pH in the cerebrospinal fluid. Peripheral chemoreceptors, located in the carotid and aortic bodies, are sensitive to changes in PaCO2, pH, and PaO2 in the arterial blood. However, their most critical and unique function is the detection of hypoxia (low PaO2). The central chemoreceptors do not respond directly to hypoxia. The peripheral chemoreceptors' firing rate increases significantly when arterial PaO2 drops below about 60 mmHg, providing the primary drive to breathe in response to hypoxia. While they do respond to hypercapnia (B) and acidosis (D), the central chemoreceptors provide the dominant response to these stimuli under normal conditions.

Question 18

Which of the following is used in diagnosing aneuploidy? 1 FISH 2 Cytogenetics 3 Sanger sequencing 4 PCR

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

1,2 Aneuploidy refers to an abnormal number of chromosomes, such as trisomy (an extra chromosome) or monosomy (a missing chromosome). Question 21

A diabetic patient has elevated VLDL levels. This increase results from which combination of factors?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

High Hormone-Sensitive Lipase (HSL), low Lipoprotein Lipase (LPL) In type 1 diabetes or poorly controlled type 2 diabetes, insulin deficiency or resistance has profound effects on lipid metabolism. Insulin normally inhibits Hormone-Sensitive Lipase (HSL) in adipose tissue. Lack of insulin leads to disinhibition of HSL, causing massive lipolysis and release of free fatty acids (FFAs) into the circulation. These FFAs are taken up by the liver and used to synthesize triglycerides, which are then packaged into Very Low-Density Lipoprotein (VLDL) and secreted, leading to high VLDL production. Additionally, insulin is required for the synthesis and activation of Lipoprotein Lipase (LPL), the enzyme on endothelial cells that clears triglycerides from VLDL and chylomicrons. Insulin deficiency leads to low LPL activity, impairing the clearance of VLDL. Thus, the hypertriglyceridemia seen in diabetes is due to both increased VLDL production (from high HSL) and decreased VLDL clearance (from low LPL).

Question 22

Question 26

Sickle cell anemia is caused by which of the following changes?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Glu → Val Sickle cell anemia is a genetic disorder caused by a specific point mutation in the gene encoding the beta-globin chain of hemoglobin. This mutation occurs at the sixth codon of the beta-globin gene. The normal DNA codon (GAG) codes for the amino acid glutamic acid (Glu), which is hydrophilic and negatively charged. In sickle cell disease, a single nucleotide substitution (A to T) changes the codon to GTG. This altered codon codes for the amino acid valine (Val), which is hydrophobic and uncharged. This seemingly minor change from glutamic acid to valine at the sixth position of the beta-globin chain dramatically alters the properties of the hemoglobin molecule (creating HbS), causing it to polymerize under deoxygenated conditions and leading to the sickling of red blood cells.

Question 27

Question 31

Umbilical cord bleeding with normal BT, PT, and APTT, along with a positive urea clot solubility test, suggests a deficiency of which coagulation factor?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Factor XIII This is a classic presentation for congenital Factor XIII deficiency. Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Exposure and Response Prevention The patient's symptoms—recurrent, distressing thoughts about contamination (obsessions) leading to repetitive hand-washing rituals (compulsions)—are the classic presentation of Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD). The gold-standard and most effective form of psychotherapy for OCD is a specific type of Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) known as Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP). ERP involves two core components. While CBT is the broad category, ERP is the specific, evidence-based technique for OCD.
