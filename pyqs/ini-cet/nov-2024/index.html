Question 1

What deficit results from an injury to the posterior interosseous nerve?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Loss of finger extension The posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) is the deep, purely motor branch of the radial nerve. It innervates the muscles in the extensor compartment of the forearm. A crucial anatomical point is that the radial nerve gives off branches to the main wrist extensors (extensor carpi radialis longus and brevis) before the PIN is formed. Therefore, in an isolated PIN palsy, wrist extension is largely preserved, and a complete wrist drop (A) does not occur. However, the PIN supplies the extensor digitorum communis, extensor indicis, extensor digiti minimi, and extensor pollicis longus. Consequently, injury to this nerve results in a characteristic "finger drop," which is an inability to extend the fingers and thumb at the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints. Adduction of the fingers (D) is a function of the ulnar nerve.

Question 2

Which cranial nerve is not contained within the superior orbital fissure?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

II The superior orbital fissure is a crucial opening in the sphenoid bone that connects the middle cranial fossa with the orbit. It transmits several important neurovascular structures. The contents include the oculomotor nerve (CN III), the trochlear nerve (CN IV), the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve (CN V1), and the abducens nerve (CN VI). It also transmits the superior ophthalmic vein. In contrast, the optic nerve (CN II) and the ophthalmic artery pass through a separate, more medially located foramen called the optic canal. Therefore, the optic nerve is not a content of the superior orbital fissure. This anatomical separation is a frequently tested point in neuroanatomy, distinguishing the pathways for vision from those controlling eye movement and sensation.

Question 3

A patient reports that food gets trapped between the cheek and the teeth. Weakness of which muscle is associated with this complaint?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Buccinator The buccinator is a thin, quadrilateral muscle that forms the principal muscular component of the cheek. Its primary function during mastication is to press the cheek firmly against the molar teeth. This action keeps food on the occlusal (chewing) surfaces of the teeth and prevents it from accumulating in the oral vestibule, which is the space between the teeth/gums and the cheek. Weakness or paralysis of the buccinator muscle, typically due to an injury of the facial nerve (CN VII), results in the inability to perform this function effectively. Consequently, food tends to pocket or get stuck in the oral vestibule during chewing, matching the patient's complaint precisely. The orbicularis oris (B) closes the lips, while the zygomaticus major (D) and risorius (D) are involved in smiling.

Question 4

The first migration of epiblast cells leads to the formation of which germ layer?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Endoderm During the third week of embryonic development, a process called gastrulation transforms the bilaminar embryonic disc into a trilaminar disc. This process begins at the primitive streak, where cells of the superficial layer, the epiblast, proliferate and migrate inwards. The very first wave of epiblast cells to invaginate through the primitive streak pushes aside and completely replaces the cells of the underlying hypoblast layer. This newly formed deep layer is the definitive embryonic endoderm. Shortly after, a second wave of migrating epiblast cells positions itself between the epiblast and the new endoderm, forming the middle layer, the mesoderm. The remaining cells of the epiblast that do not migrate constitute the ectoderm. Therefore, the endoderm is the first of the three primary germ layers to be formed from migrating epiblast cells.

Question 5

While receiving the second dose of the COVID vaccine, a patient developed a flabby, flattened, and uneven shoulder. A nurse observed a loss of the normal shoulder contour. Which anatomical structure is likely to have been damaged, and could this injury have been avoided?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Axillary nerve The rounded contour of the shoulder is primarily formed by the deltoid muscle. The symptoms described—a flabby, flat, and asymmetrical shoulder—are indicative of deltoid muscle atrophy or paralysis. The deltoid muscle is responsible for arm abduction and is innervated by the axillary nerve. This nerve courses around the surgical neck of the humerus, approximately 5-7 cm below the acromion. Intramuscular injections, such as vaccines, should be administered into the thickest part of the deltoid muscle, well above the course of the axillary nerve. An injection given too low on the arm can directly injure the axillary nerve, leading to deltoid paralysis, atrophy, and the observed loss of shoulder contour. This is a known iatrogenic injury that is preventable by using the correct injection landmarking technique.

Question 8

Which of the following types of transport does not require ATP? A. Paracellular transport B. Simple diffusion C. Facilitated diffusion D. Na+/K+ ATPase E. Symport/Antiport

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

A, B, C Cellular transport can be active or passive. Active transport requires energy, typically from ATP hydrolysis, to move substances against their concentration gradient. Na+/K+ ATPase (D) is the quintessential example of primary active transport. Symport/Antiport (E) are forms of secondary active transport, which use the energy stored in an electrochemical gradient (created by primary active transport) rather than direct ATP hydrolysis. In contrast, passive transport does not require metabolic energy. Simple diffusion (B) is the movement of substances directly across the membrane down their concentration gradient. Facilitated diffusion (C) uses a channel or carrier protein to move substances down their gradient. Paracellular transport (A) is the movement of substances through the tight junctions between cells, also driven by electrochemical gradients. Thus, paracellular transport, simple diffusion, and facilitated diffusion are all passive processes that do not directly consume ATP.

Question 9

What factors contribute to the latent period in a single muscle twitch? 1 Excitation-contraction coupling 2 Time interval from the point of stimulus to the onset of contraction 3 Neuromuscular junction (NMJ) transmission 4 Transfer of calcium back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum via SERCA

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

1,2,3 The latent period is defined as the time interval from the point of stimulus to the onset of muscle contraction (2). It is a brief delay during which a series of electrochemical and mechanical events occur before tension develops. These events include the transmission of the action potential across the neuromuscular junction (NMJ) (3), including the release of acetylcholine and generation of an end-plate potential. Following this, the process of excitation-contraction coupling (1) takes place. This involves the propagation of the muscle action potential along the sarcolemma and into the T-tubules, triggering the release of calcium ions from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and their binding to troponin, which initiates the cross-bridge cycle. The transfer of calcium back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (4) is the process that causes muscle relaxation and occurs after the contraction phase, not during the latent period.

Question 10

Which of the following helps to decrease the Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Increasing hydrostatic pressure of Bowman's capsule The Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) is determined by the balance of Starling forces across the glomerular capillaries. Filtration is favored by the glomerular hydrostatic pressure and opposed by the glomerular oncotic pressure and the hydrostatic pressure in Bowman's capsule. Therefore, to decrease GFR, one must either decrease the forces favoring filtration or increase the forces opposing it. Increasing glomerular hydrostatic pressure (C) or decreasing glomerular oncotic pressure (B) would both increase GFR. Decreasing the filtration constant (A) would decrease GFR, but this is a physiological property, not a dynamic pressure. Increasing the hydrostatic pressure of Bowman's capsule (D) directly opposes the hydrostatic pressure in the glomerulus, reducing the net filtration pressure and thereby decreasing GFR. This can occur clinically in cases of urinary tract obstruction, such as from a kidney stone or enlarged prostate.

Question 11

Static 2-point discrimination is primarily assessed by which of the following receptors?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Merkel's discs Two-point discrimination is the ability to perceive two simultaneous points of contact on the skin as distinct. This sensory modality requires mechanoreceptors with small, well-defined receptive fields and high spatial resolution. Merkel's discs are slow-adapting mechanoreceptors that are densely packed in areas like the fingertips and lips. They are specialized for detecting fine touch, pressure, and texture, and their small receptive fields make them the primary receptors responsible for static two-point discrimination. Meissner's corpuscles (B) are rapidly adapting receptors with slightly larger receptive fields, involved in detecting light touch and flutter. Pacinian corpuscles (C) are also rapidly adapting but have very large receptive fields and are responsible for sensing deep pressure and high-frequency vibration, making them unsuitable for fine spatial discrimination.

Question 12

Which of the following statements accurately describes physiological dead space?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Inadequately perfused alveoli Dead space refers to the volume of inspired air that does not participate in gas exchange. Anatomical dead space is the volume of the conducting airways. Physiological dead space is the sum of the anatomical dead space and the alveolar dead space. Alveolar dead space is composed of alveoli that are ventilated but not perfused with blood, or are inadequately perfused relative to their ventilation (high V/Q ratio). In a healthy individual, alveolar dead space is negligible, so physiological dead space equals anatomical dead space. However, in lung diseases (e.g., pulmonary embolism), perfusion can be impaired, increasing alveolar dead space and making physiological dead space greater than anatomical dead space (making C incorrect). Physiological dead space is measured using Bohr's equation with expired CO2, not by simple spirometry (D).

Question 13

Peripheral chemoreceptors exhibit their greatest sensitivity in which of the following conditions?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Hypoxia The body has two sets of chemoreceptors that regulate breathing. Central chemoreceptors in the medulla are highly sensitive to changes in PaCO2 (hypercapnia) and pH in the cerebrospinal fluid. Peripheral chemoreceptors, located in the carotid and aortic bodies, are sensitive to changes in PaCO2, pH, and PaO2 in the arterial blood. However, their most critical and unique function is the detection of hypoxia (low PaO2). The central chemoreceptors do not respond directly to hypoxia. The peripheral chemoreceptors' firing rate increases significantly when arterial PaO2 drops below about 60 mmHg, providing the primary drive to breathe in response to hypoxia. While they do respond to hypercapnia (B) and acidosis (D), the central chemoreceptors provide the dominant response to these stimuli under normal conditions.

Question 15

The adrenal medulla releases catecholamines in response to all of the following stimuli, except which one?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Hyperglycemia The adrenal medulla is a key component of the sympathetic nervous system and is responsible for the "fight-or-flight" response. It secretes catecholamines (epinephrine and norepinephrine) in response to various forms of physiological or psychological stress. Exercise (D) is a potent stimulus. The act of standing up from a lying position (A) activates the baroreceptor reflex and sympathetic outflow to maintain blood pressure. Question 17

Which blood product is NOT obtained through centrifugation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Cryoprecipitate Standard blood banking involves separating whole blood into its components primarily through centrifugation. A "hard spin" separates the cellular components, allowing for the collection of packed red blood cells (D). A subsequent "light spin" of the resulting plasma allows for the separation of platelet-rich plasma, from which platelets (C) are concentrated. Serum albumin (A) is a plasma protein that is purified from large pools of plasma through a process called Cohn fractionation, which involves ethanol precipitation and centrifugation. In contrast, cryoprecipitate is a concentrate of specific clotting factors prepared from Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP). It is isolated not by centrifugation, but by thawing FFP slowly at 1-6°C. The cold-insoluble proteins (including Factor VIII, fibrinogen, and von Willebrand factor) precipitate out and are then collected. BIOCHEMISTRY

Question 18

Which of the following is used in diagnosing aneuploidy? 1 FISH 2 Cytogenetics 3 Sanger sequencing 4 PCR

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

1,2 Aneuploidy refers to an abnormal number of chromosomes, such as trisomy (an extra chromosome) or monosomy (a missing chromosome). The gold standard method for diagnosing aneuploidy is cytogenetics, specifically karyotyping (2), which involves visualizing and counting all the chromosomes from a cell culture. Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH) (1) is a rapid molecular cytogenetic technique that uses fluorescent probes to identify and count specific chromosomes or chromosome regions in interphase cells, making it an excellent tool for rapid screening for common aneuploidies like trisomies 21, 18, and 13. In contrast, Sanger sequencing (3) and PCR (4) are techniques used to analyze the nucleotide sequence of DNA. They are ideal for detecting single gene mutations or small insertions/deletions, but not for identifying changes in the number of entire chromosomes.

Question 19

Which enzymes are utilized in recombinant DNA technology? 1 Isomerase 2 Phosphatase 3 Terminal transferase 4 CRISPR-Cas9

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

2,3,4 Recombinant DNA technology relies on a toolkit of specific enzymes to manipulate DNA. Key enzymes include restriction endonucleases to cut DNA and DNA ligase to join it. Alkaline phosphatase (2) is used to remove the 5' phosphate group from a vector to prevent it from re-ligating to itself. Terminal transferase (3) is an enzyme that adds nucleotides to the 3' end of a DNA strand without a template, useful for creating sticky ends. CRISPR-Cas9 (4) is a revolutionary gene-editing system consisting of a guide RNA and the Cas9 nuclease, used to create targeted double-strand breaks in DNA. In contrast, isomerase (1) is a broad class of enzymes that catalyze the conversion of a molecule into one of its isomers; they are central to metabolism but are not a standard tool in the recombinant DNA technology toolkit.

Question 20

Which of the following substances does not inhibit cytochrome activity?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Nitric oxide Cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV) is the terminal enzyme of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Several classic poisons exert their toxicity by inhibiting this enzyme. Cyanide (D) and carbon monoxide (CO) (B) are well-known inhibitors that bind to the ferrous (Fe2+) and ferric (Fe3+) iron in the heme a3 component of the enzyme, blocking electron transfer to oxygen. Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) (C) is another toxic gas that also inhibits cytochrome c oxidase in a manner similar to cyanide. In contrast, nitric oxide (NO) (A), while a potent signaling molecule that can bind to heme iron, acts as a reversible and physiological inhibitor of cytochrome c oxidase. This reversible inhibition plays a role in regulating cellular respiration and is distinct from the irreversible and toxic inhibition caused by the other substances.

Question 21

A diabetic patient has elevated VLDL levels. This increase results from which combination of factors?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

High Hormone-Sensitive Lipase (HSL), low Lipoprotein Lipase (LPL) In type 1 diabetes or poorly controlled type 2 diabetes, insulin deficiency or resistance has profound effects on lipid metabolism. Insulin normally inhibits Hormone-Sensitive Lipase (HSL) in adipose tissue. Lack of insulin leads to disinhibition of HSL, causing massive lipolysis and release of free fatty acids (FFAs) into the circulation. These FFAs are taken up by the liver and used to synthesize triglycerides, which are then packaged into Very Low-Density Lipoprotein (VLDL) and secreted, leading to high VLDL production. Additionally, insulin is required for the synthesis and activation of Lipoprotein Lipase (LPL), the enzyme on endothelial cells that clears triglycerides from VLDL and chylomicrons. Insulin deficiency leads to low LPL activity, impairing the clearance of VLDL. Thus, the hypertriglyceridemia seen in diabetes is due to both increased VLDL production (from high HSL) and decreased VLDL clearance (from low LPL).

Question 22

In the treatment of urea cycle disorders, which amino acid provides the nitrogen that combines with sodium benzoate?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Glycine Urea cycle disorders lead to the accumulation of toxic ammonia. Nitrogen-scavenging drugs are used to provide an alternative pathway for nitrogen excretion. Sodium benzoate is one such drug. It is conjugated with the amino acid glycine to form hippuric acid. This reaction consumes one molecule of glycine, and since glycine synthesis requires nitrogen, this effectively removes one mole of nitrogen from the body for every mole of benzoate given. The resulting hippuric acid is then readily excreted in the urine. Another drug, sodium phenylacetate/phenylbutyrate, is conjugated with glutamine (A) to form phenylacetylglutamine, which removes two moles of nitrogen. However, sodium benzoate specifically utilizes glycine for its nitrogen-scavenging effect.

Question 26

Sickle cell anemia is caused by which of the following changes?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Glu → Val Sickle cell anemia is a genetic disorder caused by a specific point mutation in the gene encoding the beta-globin chain of hemoglobin. This mutation occurs at the sixth codon of the beta-globin gene. The normal DNA codon (GAG) codes for the amino acid glutamic acid (Glu), which is hydrophilic and negatively charged. In sickle cell disease, a single nucleotide substitution (A to T) changes the codon to GTG. This altered codon codes for the amino acid valine (Val), which is hydrophobic and uncharged. This seemingly minor change from glutamic acid to valine at the sixth position of the beta-globin chain dramatically alters the properties of the hemoglobin molecule (creating HbS), causing it to polymerize under deoxygenated conditions and leading to the sickling of red blood cells.

Question 27

Which protein plays a role in the process of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

SNAIL & TWIST Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a fundamental biological process where epithelial cells lose their cell-cell adhesion and polarity, and acquire a migratory, mesenchymal phenotype. This process is crucial in embryonic development and wound healing, and it is co-opted by cancer cells to promote invasion and metastasis. The EMT is driven by a network of key transcription factors that repress epithelial genes (like E-cadherin) and activate mesenchymal genes. Among the most important and well-studied of these master regulators are the zinc-finger transcription factor SNAIL and the basic helix-loop-helix transcription factor TWIST. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) like MMP9 (C) and cathepsins (D) are proteases that are often upregulated during EMT and help degrade the extracellular matrix, facilitating invasion, but they are downstream effectors, not the primary transcription factors driving the transition.

Question 28

Which is the most essential fatty acid?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Linoleic acid Essential fatty acids are polyunsaturated fatty acids that humans must obtain from their diet because the body cannot synthesize them. There are two primary essential fatty acids: linoleic acid (LA), an omega-6 fatty acid, and alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), an omega-3 fatty acid. From these two precursors, the body can synthesize other important long-chain fatty acids. Arachidonic acid (C) can be synthesized from linoleic acid, making it conditionally essential. Between the two essential fatty acids, linoleic acid is often considered the most essential because its deficiency is more readily produced and has more prominent clinical signs, and the quantitative requirement for it is higher than for alpha-linolenic acid. Question 29

Which anticoagulant is most suitable for collecting blood samples for HbA1c testing?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

EDTA Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) is a measure of long-term glycemic control, reflecting the average blood glucose level over the preceding 2-3 months. For this test, a whole blood sample is required, and it is crucial to prevent the blood from clotting after collection. The anticoagulant of choice for HbA1c estimation, as well as for most hematological tests like a complete blood count, is EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid), which is found in lavender-top tubes. EDTA works by chelating calcium ions, which are essential for the coagulation cascade, thereby preventing clotting and preserving the integrity of the red blood cells that contain the hemoglobin. A fluoride tube (B) is used for glucose estimation as it inhibits glycolysis, but it is not ideal for HbA1c. Citrate (C) is used for coagulation studies, and heparin (D) is used for plasma chemistry.

Question 30

Which of the following is true regarding serum calcium estimation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Prolonged immobilization causes increased free and total calcium Several pre-analytical factors can affect the accuracy of serum calcium measurements. Fist clenching (A) and prolonged or tight tourniquet application (B) can cause a localized increase in lactate and a decrease in pH, which displaces calcium from albumin, and can cause hemoconcentration, falsely elevating the total calcium level. Question 31

Umbilical cord bleeding with normal BT, PT, and APTT, along with a positive urea clot solubility test, suggests a deficiency of which coagulation factor?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Factor XIII This is a classic presentation for congenital Factor XIII deficiency. Factor XIII, also known as fibrin-stabilizing factor, is responsible for cross-linking fibrin strands to form a strong, stable clot. Its deficiency results in a fragile clot that is easily broken down, leading to a characteristic pattern of delayed bleeding. Bleeding from the umbilical stump in the neonatal period is a hallmark sign. Because the initial formation of the fibrin clot is normal, the standard coagulation screening tests—PT, APTT, and Bleeding Time—are all normal. The diagnosis is confirmed by a specific assay, the urea clot solubility test. A normal, cross-linked clot is insoluble in 5M urea, whereas the weak, un-cross-linked clot found in Factor XIII deficiency will readily dissolve, yielding a positive test result.

Question 45

When collecting a blood sample for serum calcium estimation, which practice must be avoided to prevent incorrect or misleading calcium results? a. Collection in a sodium citrate vial b. Forearm exercise and fist clenching c. Prolonged immobilization of the patient d. Rapid removal of the tourniquet

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

a, b, c Several pre-analytical factors can significantly alter serum calcium levels, leading to erroneous results. (a) Collection in a sodium citrate vial : This must be avoided. Anticoagulants like citrate and EDTA chelate (bind) calcium ions, which will falsely decrease the measured calcium level in the sample. Serum samples should be collected in plain red-top or serum separator tubes. (b) Forearm exercise and fist clenching : This action during phlebotomy can cause localized lactic acidosis. The resulting decrease in pH causes more calcium to dissociate from its protein carrier, albumin, thereby falsely increasing the ionized (free) calcium concentration. (c) Prolonged immobilization : While a physiological state rather than a collection error, prolonged bed rest or immobilization leads to bone resorption, which genuinely increases total serum calcium levels. If a baseline calcium is desired, this confounding factor must be considered. (d) Rapid removal of the tourniquet : This is a correct procedure. It is prolonged tourniquet application (>1 minute) that should be avoided, as it causes hemoconcentration and protein leakage, falsely elevating the concentration of total calcium (since about 45% of calcium is protein-bound). Thus, factors a, b, and c must be avoided or accounted for to prevent erroneous reporting. PHARMACOLOGY
