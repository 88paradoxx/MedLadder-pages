Question 1

Reticular fibers are absent in :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Thymus Reticular fibers, which are composed of type III collagen, form a delicate, intricate meshwork that provides structural support to the parenchymal cells of most lymphoid and hematopoietic organs. This reticular stroma is a characteristic feature of the bone marrow (A), the spleen (B) (in the white and red pulp), and the lymph nodes (D), creating a scaffold for lymphocytes, macrophages, and other immune cells. The thymus (C) is the distinct exception among these organs. The structural framework of the thymus is not made of reticular fibers but is instead formed by a unique cytoreticulum composed of specialized cells called thymic epithelial cells (also known as epithelioreticular cells). These cells provide the necessary support and create the microenvironment essential for the maturation and selection of T-lymphocytes (thymocytes). This cellular, rather than fibrous, stroma is a key histological feature that distinguishes the thymus from other lymphoid tissues.

Question 2

A patient is unable to abduct the upper limb. Which nerve root is affected?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

C5 The abduction of the upper limb is a complex movement primarily performed by the deltoid and supraspinatus muscles. The initiation of abduction (the first 0-15 degrees) is by the supraspinatus muscle, which is innervated by the suprascapular nerve (with root values C5 and C6). The main power for abduction from 15 to 90 degrees comes from the deltoid muscle, which is innervated by the axillary nerve. The axillary nerve is derived from the posterior cord of the brachial plexus and receives its fibers from the C5 and C6 nerve roots. The C5 root is considered the primary contributor to both the axillary and suprascapular nerves and is therefore the most critical nerve root for shoulder abduction. A lesion affecting the C5 root, such as in an upper brachial plexus injury (Erb's palsy), characteristically results in a profound inability to abduct the arm. The C6 root also contributes but is more associated with elbow flexion.

Question 3

Which of the following muscles does not take part in abducting the arm overhead?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Pectoralis major Abduction of the arm to an overhead position (180 degrees) is a coordinated movement involving both the glenohumeral joint and the scapulothoracic articulation, a process known as scapulohumeral rhythm. The deltoid muscle (B) is the prime mover of abduction at the glenohumeral joint up to 90 degrees. To achieve abduction beyond 90 degrees, the scapula must upwardly rotate on the thoracic wall. This crucial upward rotation is accomplished by a force couple created by the upper and lower fibers of the trapezius muscle (C) and the powerful serratus anterior muscle (A). The serratus anterior protracts the scapula and is essential for its upward rotation. In contrast, the primary actions of the pectoralis major muscle (D) are adduction and medial rotation of the humerus. It acts as an antagonist to abduction and therefore is not involved in, and in fact opposes, the action of overhead abduction.

Question 4

The slow waves (basic electrical rhythm or BER frequency) are lowest in which of the following?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Stomach Slow waves, also known as the basic electrical rhythm (BER), are spontaneous, rhythmic fluctuations in the membrane potential of gastrointestinal smooth muscle cells. They are generated by specialized pacemaker cells called the interstitial cells of Cajal. While these waves do not cause contractions themselves, they determine the maximum frequency at which contractions can occur. The frequency of the BER follows a gradient along the GI tract. It is highest in the proximal small intestine, with the duodenum (B) having a rate of about 12 cycles per minute. The frequency then decreases distally through the jejunum (C) and ileum (D) (to about 8-9 cycles per minute). The stomach (A), however, has a much lower intrinsic BER frequency, with only about 3 slow waves per minute. Therefore, among the options provided, the frequency of slow waves is least in the stomach.

Question 5

All of the following substances show an increase in concentration within the Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT), except which one? :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Amino acid In the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT), a large proportion of filtered substances are reabsorbed back into the bloodstream. However, the concentration of a substance within the tubular fluid depends on whether it is reabsorbed more rapidly than water. Amino acids and glucose are almost completely reabsorbed early in the PCT by active transport (via sodium-dependent cotransporters). Because they are reabsorbed rapidly and nearly completely, their concentration in tubular fluid decreases, not increases. Bicarbonate is also significantly reabsorbed in the PCT (about 80–90%). Its concentration generally falls as it is reclaimed efficiently. In contrast, chloride initially shows a relative increase in concentration in the early PCT. This occurs because water and bicarbonate are reabsorbed faster than chloride, leaving chloride behind and increasing its tubular concentration temporarily before it is later reabsorbed. Thus, the substance that does not show an increase in concentration in the PCT is amino acid, making option (A) correct.

Question 6

What happens when a person moves from a lying (supine) position to standing?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Decrease firing in baroreceptor afferents When a person stands up from a supine position, gravity causes blood to pool in the veins of the lower extremities. This leads to a transient decrease in venous return to the heart, a decrease in cardiac output, and consequently, a drop in arterial blood pressure. This fall in blood pressure is sensed by the high-pressure baroreceptors located in the carotid sinus and the aortic arch. These receptors are stretch receptors, and the reduced stretch due to the lower blood pressure causes a decrease in the firing rate of the baroreceptor afferent nerves that travel to the brainstem. This decreased afferent signal triggers a compensatory reflex : sympathetic output increases (causing vasoconstriction and increased heart rate), and vagal (parasympathetic) output to the heart decreases (C is incorrect), all aimed at restoring the blood pressure to its normal level.

Question 9

In membrane receptors, the transmembrane region is made up of which type of amino acids?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Hydrophobic Transmembrane proteins, including membrane receptors, must span the lipid bilayer of the cell membrane. The core of the lipid bilayer is composed of the fatty acid tails of phospholipids, creating a highly nonpolar, oily environment. To be stably embedded within this environment, the segment of the protein that passes through the membrane must be composed of amino acids with nonpolar, hydrophobic side chains. These amino acids (such as leucine, isoleucine, valine, and phenylalanine) interact favorably with the lipid tails, anchoring the protein in the membrane. Typically, this transmembrane domain is arranged in an alpha-helical structure. In contrast, the extracellular and intracellular domains of the receptor, which are exposed to the aqueous environments outside and inside the cell, are primarily composed of hydrophilic (B) and charged (C) amino acids.

Question 10

High anion gap acidosis is seen in all except :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Respiratory disorders A high anion gap metabolic acidosis is caused by the accumulation of unmeasured organic acids in the blood. In diabetic ketoacidosis (A), the acids are acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate. In lactic acidosis (B), the acid is lactate. Certain types of renal failure (uremia) can also lead to the accumulation of acids like sulfates and phosphates, causing a high anion gap. The term "renal aciduria" (C) is ambiguous but likely refers to this or a renal tubular acidosis that can sometimes be associated with a high anion gap. In contrast, respiratory disorders (D) cause respiratory acidosis or alkalosis, not metabolic acidosis. Respiratory acidosis is caused by the retention of CO 2 . While this lowers the pH, it does not involve the addition of an unmeasured anion and therefore does not cause a high anion gap. It is a distinct acid-base disturbance with a different underlying pathophysiology.

Question 11

The macula densa is located in :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT) The macula densa is a specialized plaque of densely packed epithelial cells that is a key component of the juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA). The JGA is a structure that regulates renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate. Anatomically, the macula densa is located at the very beginning of the distal convoluted tubule (DCT), at the precise point where the tubule passes between the afferent and efferent arterioles of its own glomerulus. Some texts describe its location as the terminal part of the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, right at the transition to the DCT. These cells function as chemoreceptors, sensing the concentration of sodium chloride in the tubular fluid. This information is then used to signal the afferent arteriole to constrict or dilate, a process known as tubuloglomerular feedback, which helps to autoregulate the filtration rate of the individual nephron.

Question 13

Which of the following statements about skeletal muscle contraction are correct?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

A & C both The process of excitation-contraction coupling in skeletal muscle has specific characteristics. The action potential travels down the T-tubules, where it activates voltage-sensitive DHP receptors. These receptors are mechanically linked to ryanodine receptors on the membrane of the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR). This mechanical coupling triggers the opening of the ryanodine receptors, causing a massive release of stored calcium ions (Ca ++ ) from the SR into the cytoplasm (C is true). This rise in intracellular calcium initiates the cross-bridge cycle. Unlike cardiac muscle, skeletal muscle contraction is not directly dependent on the influx of extracellular calcium for triggering this release; the process is purely mechanical. Therefore, extracellular Ca ++ is not important for initiating the contraction (A is true). Calcium is released from the SR, not the mitochondria (B is incorrect). Since both statements A and C are true regarding skeletal muscle, option D is the correct answer. BIOCHEMISTRY

Question 14

DNA methylation is not associated with :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

RNA splicing DNA methylation is a fundamental epigenetic mechanism that plays a role in several key cellular processes. It is a primary mechanism of gene silencing (C), as the methylation of CpG islands in a gene's promoter region typically prevents the binding of transcription factors and recruits repressive proteins. This process is intimately linked with chromatin remodeling (A), as methylated DNA promotes the formation of condensed, transcriptionally silent heterochromatin. In prokaryotes, it is also crucial for methyl-directed mismatch repair (D), a system that uses the methylation status of the parental DNA strand to identify and correct errors made during DNA replication on the newly synthesized strand. However, RNA splicing (B) is a post-transcriptional process. It involves the removal of introns from a pre-mRNA molecule to form mature mRNA. This process is carried out by the spliceosome and is regulated by splicing factors, but it is not directly controlled by the methylation status of the DNA from which the RNA was transcribed.

Question 17

Which of the following statements about telomerase is incorrect?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

It maintains high fidelity during DNA replication Telomerase is a unique enzyme responsible for maintaining the length of chromosomes (D) by adding repetitive nucleotide sequences to the ends, or telomeres. Question 20

The steps of PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction) are :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Denaturation → Annealing → Elongation The Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) is a technique used to amplify a specific segment of DNA exponentially. It consists of a series of repeated cycles, with each cycle comprising three distinct temperature-controlled steps : 1. Denaturation : The reaction mixture is heated to a high temperature (typically 94-98°C). This breaks the hydrogen bonds holding the double-stranded DNA template together, separating it into two single strands. 2. Annealing : The temperature is lowered (typically 50-65°C). This allows short, single-stranded DNA primers, which are complementary to the sequences flanking the target region, to bind (anneal) to their respective sites on the single-stranded DNA templates. 3. Elongation (or Extension) : The temperature is raised again (typically to 72°C), which is the optimal temperature for the thermostable DNA polymerase (like Taq polymerase). The polymerase then synthesizes a new complementary DNA strand, starting from the primers and extending along the template strand. This three-step cycle is repeated 25-35 times, leading to an exponential amplification of the target DNA sequence.

Question 21

Compare the normality of 2 mM HCl and 1 mM H 2 SO 4 .

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Same Normality (N) is a measure of concentration defined as the molarity (M) of a solution multiplied by its equivalence factor (or n-factor). For an acid, the n-factor is the number of moles of hydrogen ions (H + ) that one mole of the acid can donate. For hydrochloric acid (HCl), it is a monoprotic acid, meaning it donates one H + ion per molecule. Therefore, its n-factor is 1. 1. Normality of 2 mM HCl = 2 mM × 1 = 2 mN (millinormal). 2. For sulfuric acid (H 2 SO 4 ), it is a diprotic acid, meaning it can donate two H + ions per molecule. Therefore, its n-factor is 2. 3. Normality of 1 mM H 2 SO 4 = 1 mM × 2 = 2 mN. Since both solutions have a normality of 2 mN, their normalities are the same.

Question 22

The thermogenic effect of food is highest for :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Proteins This is a duplicate of a previous question. The thermic effect of food (TEF), also called specific dynamic action, is the energy expenditure required for the digestion, absorption, and metabolism of nutrients after a meal. This value varies significantly between the different macronutrients. Proteins have the highest TEF, with about 20-30% of their ingested energy being used for their processing and assimilation. This is due to the energy-intensive processes of deamination and urea synthesis. Carbohydrates (B) have an intermediate TEF, typically in the range of 5-10%. Lipids (A), or fats, have the lowest TEF, at only about 0-3%, meaning they are the most metabolically efficient macronutrient to store. Therefore, protein consumption leads to the greatest increase in metabolic rate compared to the other macronutrients, a principle often utilized in diets for weight management. PATHOLOGY

Question 24

Which of the following increases in COPD? 1 FRC (Functional Residual Capacity) 2 TLC (Total Lung Capacity) 3 VC (Vital Capacity) 4 FEV1/FVC (Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 second / Forced Vital Capacity)

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

1 & 2 Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) is characterized by persistent airflow limitation. This obstruction leads to air trapping, where air gets trapped in the lungs at the end of expiration. This has several effects on lung volumes measured by spirometry and plethysmography. The trapped air leads to an increase in the volume of air remaining in the lungs after a normal expiration, which is the Functional Residual Capacity (FRC) (1). It also leads to an increase in the volume of air remaining after a maximal expiration (Residual Volume, RV). As a consequence of the increased RV, the Total Lung Capacity (TLC) (2), which is the sum of RV and Vital Capacity, is also typically increased or normal. In contrast, the Vital Capacity (VC) (3) (the maximum volume of air that can be exhaled) is often decreased. The hallmark of COPD is a reduced FEV1/FVC ratio (4), which is decreased, not increased.

Question 132

Subcortical dementia seen in? A. Parkinson's disease B. Wilson's disease C. Huntington's disease D. Pick's disease

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

ABC only Dementias can be broadly classified as cortical or subcortical, based on the primary site of pathology. Subcortical dementias are characterized by prominent deficits in executive function, psychomotor slowing, and mood changes, with memory impairment being less severe initially. The pathology primarily involves structures deep within the brain, such as the basal ganglia, thalamus, and brainstem nuclei. Parkinson's disease (A), Wilson's disease (B), and Huntington's disease (C) are all classic examples of conditions that primarily affect subcortical structures and can lead to a subcortical dementia syndrome. In contrast, Pick's disease (D) (a type of frontotemporal dementia) and Alzheimer's disease are classic examples of cortical dementias, where the pathology predominantly affects the cerebral cortex, leading to prominent early memory loss (amnesia), language deficits (aphasia), and visuospatial problems (agnosia).

Question 135

How will you differentiate delirium from dementia in Alzheimer's disease?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Acute onset and level of consciousness Delirium and dementia can present with overlapping symptoms like confusion and memory impairment, and delirium is often superimposed on a baseline dementia. However, there are key features that help to differentiate them. The most crucial distinguishing feature is the onset and course. Dementia, like Alzheimer's disease, has an insidious onset and a progressive, chronic course. Delirium, on the other hand, is characterized by an acute onset (developing over hours to days) and a fluctuating course. The second cardinal feature that distinguishes delirium is a disturbance in the level of consciousness, awareness, and attention. Patients with delirium have a clouded sensorium and are inattentive, whereas in dementia, the level of consciousness is typically preserved until the very late stages. While symptoms like agitation (A, D) and hallucinations (B) are common in delirium, they can also occur in dementia, making them less reliable differentiating factors than the acute onset and altered level of consciousness.
