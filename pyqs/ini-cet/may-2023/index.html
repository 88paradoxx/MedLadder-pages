Question 1

What is the correct order of structures in the auditory pathway?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Eight nerve cochlear nuclei superior olivary nucleus lateral lemniscus inferior colliculus → medial geniculate body → auditory cortex The central auditory pathway is a complex, polysynaptic route that transmits sound information from the inner ear to the cerebral cortex for conscious perception. The pathway begins with the spiral ganglion neurons, whose axons form the cochlear division of the vestibulocochlear nerve (cranial nerve VIII). These fibers make their first synapse in the cochlear nuclei located in the brainstem. From here, auditory information ascends bilaterally, with a major synapse in the superior olivary nucleus, which is crucial for sound localization. The pathway continues upward through a major ascending tract known as the lateral lemniscus. The next significant relay station is the inferior colliculus in the midbrain, another key auditory processing center. Subsequently, fibers project to the medial geniculate body of the thalamus, the final subcortical relay nucleus. Finally, thalamocortical radiations carry the processed auditory information to the primary auditory cortex located in the temporal lobe.

Question 2

Which nerve provides innervation to the gluteus maximus muscle?

Correct answer: C

Inferior Gluteal Nerve The gluteus maximus is the largest and most superficial of the three gluteal muscles, and it serves as the primary extensor of the hip joint. Its motor innervation is provided solely by the inferior gluteal nerve. This nerve arises from the posterior divisions of the ventral rami of the L5, S1, and S2 spinal nerves within the lumbosacral plexus. It exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, inferior to the piriformis muscle, and travels a short course to innervate the overlying gluteus maximus muscle. It's important to differentiate this from the innervation of the other gluteal muscles; the gluteus medius and minimus, which are responsible for hip abduction and stabilization, are innervated by the superior gluteal nerve. The femoral nerve supplies the anterior thigh muscles, while the sciatic nerve innervates the posterior thigh and the leg and foot, passing deep to the gluteus maximus without supplying it.

Question 6

All vessels demonstrate the Windkessel effect except:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Radial artery The Windkessel effect describes the phenomenon where the pulsatile flow of blood ejected from the heart is converted into a more continuous, smoother flow in the distal vasculature. This is achieved by the elastic recoil of large arteries. During systole, the walls of large elastic arteries like the aorta and its major branches (e.g., abdominal aorta, subclavian artery) stretch to accommodate the stroke volume, storing potential energy. During diastole, these elastic walls recoil, pushing the stored blood forward and maintaining diastolic pressure and flow. As arteries travel further from the heart, their composition changes from predominantly elastic to more muscular. The radial artery, a muscular artery in the forearm, has significantly less elastin in its walls compared to the aorta. Therefore, it exhibits a much-reduced Windkessel effect and demonstrates a more pulsatile pressure waveform. The renal artery is still a large branch off the aorta and retains significant elastic properties.

Question 7

Arrange the following sarcomere components from the most peripheral region to the most central region. 1. Z line 2. M line 3. A band 4. H zone

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

1,3,4,2 The sarcomere is the fundamental contractile unit of striated muscle, extending between two adjacent Z lines. When arranging its components from the outermost boundary towards the absolute center, the correct sequence is based on their defined anatomical positions. The outermost boundary of a single sarcomere is the Z line (1), which anchors the thin (actin) filaments. Moving inward from the Z line, one encounters the A band. The A band (3) represents the entire length of the thick (myosin) filaments. Within the central region of the A band, there is a lighter area where the thin filaments do not overlap the thick filaments; this is known as the H zone (4). Finally, at the very center of the H zone, and thus the absolute center of the sarcomere, is the M line (2), which serves to anchor the thick filaments. Therefore, the correct sequence from the outer boundary to the innermost point is Z line → A band → H zone → M line.

Question 9

Which factors are responsible for the latent period observed in a single muscle twitch? 1. Excitation-contraction coupling 2. Time interval from the point of stimulus to the onset of contraction 3. Neuromuscular junction (NMJ) transmission 4. Transfer of calcium back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum via SERCA

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

1,2,3 The latent period of a muscle twitch is defined as the time interval from the application of a stimulus to the beginning of the actual muscle contraction. It is a brief delay during which a series of critical electrochemical events must occur before tension can be generated. These events include the transmission of the action potential across the neuromuscular junction (NMJ), which involves the release of acetylcholine, binding to receptors, and depolarization of the sarcolemma. Following this, the process of excitation-contraction coupling takes place. This involves the propagation of the muscle action potential along the T-tubules, triggering the release of calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, and the binding of calcium to troponin to initiate cross-bridge cycling. The reuptake of calcium into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by the SERCA pump (4) is the process that causes muscle relaxation and occurs after the contraction phase, not during the latent period.

Question 10

What is the correct sequence of band arrangement within a sarcomere?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Z-I-A-H-M This question asks for the correct spatial arrangement of the bands and lines within a sarcomere, the fundamental contractile unit of a muscle fiber. A sarcomere is defined as the segment between two successive Z lines. Moving from one Z line towards the center of the sarcomere, the first region encountered is the I band, which is a light band containing only thin (actin) filaments. The Z line itself bisects the I band. Next is the A band, a dark band that represents the entire length of the thick (myosin) filaments. Within the center of the A band, there is a paler region where the thin and thick filaments do not overlap; this is the H zone. Finally, at the very center of the H zone and the sarcomere is the M line, which anchors the thick filaments. Therefore, the correct sequence of structures from the edge to the center is Z-I-A-H-M. Which option results in the greatest thermogenic effect?

Correct answer: B

Protein The thermic effect of food (TEF), also known as specific dynamic action, refers to the increase in metabolic rate (energy expenditure) that occurs after ingesting food. This energy is required for the digestion, absorption, processing, and storage of the nutrients. The magnitude of the TEF varies significantly depending on the macronutrient composition of the meal. Protein has the highest thermic effect of all macronutrients. The body expends approximately 20-30% of the energy content of the ingested protein to process it. This high energy cost for protein metabolism is due to the complex processes of deamination and the synthesis of urea, among other metabolic pathways involved in handling amino acids.

Correct answer: A

It is caused by a deficiency of the enzyme lysosomal acid alpha-glucosidase, also known as acid maltase. In a healthy individual, this enzyme is responsible for breaking down glycogen into glucose within the lysosomes. Which vitamin deficiency is linked to xanthurenic aciduria?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Vitamin B6 Xanthurenic aciduria refers to the excessive excretion of xanthurenic acid in the urine. This is a characteristic biochemical finding in vitamin B6 deficiency. Vitamin B6, in its active form pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), is an essential cofactor for numerous enzymes involved in amino acid metabolism. One such enzyme is kynureninase, which is part of the metabolic pathway that converts tryptophan to niacin. When there is a deficiency of vitamin B6, the activity of kynureninase is impaired. Question 16

The liver is capable of producing ketone bodies but cannot utilize them because it lacks which enzyme?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Thiophorase During periods of fasting, starvation, or uncontrolled diabetes, the liver engages in ketogenesis, producing ketone bodies (acetoacetate and β -hydroxybutyrate) from fatty acid oxidation. These ketone bodies are then exported into the bloodstream to be used as an energy source by extrahepatic tissues like the brain, heart, and skeletal muscle. For these peripheral tissues to utilize ketone bodies, they must first convert acetoacetate back into acetyl-CoA for entry into the TCA cycle. This activation step is catalyzed by the mitochondrial enzyme succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid-CoA transferase, more commonly known as thiophorase. The liver is the site of ketone body synthesis but notably lacks this thiophorase enzyme. This enzymatic deficiency prevents the liver from utilizing the ketones it produces, ensuring that they are available for export to other tissues that critically need them for energy.

Question 20

What occurs when misfolded proteins accumulate abnormally?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease A number of diseases, known as proteinopathies or protein misfolding diseases, are caused by the accumulation of abnormally folded proteins that aggregate and cause cellular damage. Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD) is the classic example of a human prion disease, which falls into this category. It is caused by the conversion of the normal cellular prion protein (PrPC) into an abnormal, misfolded, and infectious isoform (PrPSc). Question 23

How can IDA be interpreted? a. Low serum ferritin b. Low transferrin saturation c. Low serum iron d. Increased TIBC

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

abcd Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA) has a characteristic pattern on iron studies that reflects the body's depleted iron stores and its attempt to compensate. Serum ferritin is a measure of stored iron, and a low level is the most specific and earliest indicator of iron deficiency (a). Serum iron measures the amount of iron circulating in the blood bound to transferrin, and it is also low in IDA (c). The Total Iron-Binding Capacity (TIBC) is an indirect measure of transferrin, the protein that transports iron. In an effort to acquire more iron, the liver increases the production of transferrin, leading to an increased TIBC (d). Finally, the transferrin saturation, calculated as (serum iron / TIBC) x 100, is low because there is very little iron available to saturate the increased amount of transferrin (b). All four of these findings together form the classic biochemical profile of iron deficiency anemia.

Question 28

What does IDA stand for and how is it interpreted in the medical field?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

All of the above IDA stands for Iron Deficiency Anemia. Its diagnosis is confirmed by a characteristic pattern on a panel of blood tests known as iron studies. This pattern reflects both the depletion of the body's iron stores and its compensatory physiological responses. Low serum ferritin is the most specific indicator, as ferritin is the primary iron storage protein, and its levels directly correlate with total body iron stores. Therapeutic index is LD50/ED50 The therapeutic index (TI) is a quantitative measure of the relative safety of a drug. It is a ratio that compares the dose of a drug that produces a toxic effect to the dose that produces a therapeutic or desired effect. Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Nihilistic delusion The patient's statements are a classic example of nihilistic delusions, which are a core feature of Cotard's syndrome (also known as the delusion of negation). A nihilistic delusion is a false and fixed belief centered on themes of non-existence, death, or the futility of everything. The patient may believe that they are already dead, that parts of their body (like their brain) are missing or have ceased to function, or that the world itself has ended. The statements "my brain is gone" and "I am already deceased" directly reflect this profound sense of negation and non-existence. These delusions are most commonly seen in the context of severe psychotic depression but can also occur in other psychotic disorders. A hypochondriacal delusion (D) is a belief of having a serious illness, but not of being dead or non-existent.
