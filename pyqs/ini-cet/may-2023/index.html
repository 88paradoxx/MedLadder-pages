Question 1

What is the correct order of structures in the auditory pathway?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Eight nerve cochlear nuclei superior olivary nucleus lateral lemniscus inferior colliculus → medial geniculate body → auditory cortex The central auditory pathway is a complex, polysynaptic route that transmits sound information from the inner ear to the cerebral cortex for conscious perception. The pathway begins with the spiral ganglion neurons, whose axons form the cochlear division of the vestibulocochlear nerve (cranial nerve VIII). These fibers make their first synapse in the cochlear nuclei located in the brainstem. From here, auditory information ascends bilaterally, with a major synapse in the superior olivary nucleus, which is crucial for sound localization. The pathway continues upward through a major ascending tract known as the lateral lemniscus. The next significant relay station is the inferior colliculus in the midbrain, another key auditory processing center. Subsequently, fibers project to the medial geniculate body of the thalamus, the final subcortical relay nucleus. Finally, thalamocortical radiations carry the processed auditory information to the primary auditory cortex located in the temporal lobe.

Question 2

Which nerve provides innervation to the gluteus maximus muscle?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Inferior Gluteal Nerve The gluteus maximus is the largest and most superficial of the three gluteal muscles, and it serves as the primary extensor of the hip joint. Its motor innervation is provided solely by the inferior gluteal nerve. This nerve arises from the posterior divisions of the ventral rami of the L5, S1, and S2 spinal nerves within the lumbosacral plexus. It exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, inferior to the piriformis muscle, and travels a short course to innervate the overlying gluteus maximus muscle. It's important to differentiate this from the innervation of the other gluteal muscles; the gluteus medius and minimus, which are responsible for hip abduction and stabilization, are innervated by the superior gluteal nerve. The femoral nerve supplies the anterior thigh muscles, while the sciatic nerve innervates the posterior thigh and the leg and foot, passing deep to the gluteus maximus without supplying it.

Question 4

Arrange the neural columns in medial-to-lateral order 1. Special somatic afferent 2. Special visceral efferent 3. General somatic efferent 4. General somatic afferent

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

GSE, SVE, GSA, SSA During the development of the brainstem, the cranial nerve nuclei are organized into longitudinal functional columns arranged in a specific medial-to-lateral sequence. The sulcus limitans divides the plate into a medial motor (basal) plate and a lateral sensory (alar) plate. The motor columns are thus medial to the sensory columns. Within the motor plate, the most medial column is the General Somatic Efferent (GSE), innervating skeletal muscles derived from somites. Lateral to it is the Special Visceral Efferent (SVE) column, supplying muscles from the pharyngeal arches. In the sensory plate, the General Somatic Afferent (GSA) column is medial to the Special Somatic Afferent (SSA) column, which is the most lateral of all the columns. Therefore, the correct medial-to-lateral arrangement of the specified columns is GSE, SVE, GSA, and SSA. PHYSIOLOGY

Question 6

All vessels demonstrate the Windkessel effect except:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Radial artery The Windkessel effect describes the phenomenon where the pulsatile flow of blood ejected from the heart is converted into a more continuous, smoother flow in the distal vasculature. This is achieved by the elastic recoil of large arteries. During systole, the walls of large elastic arteries like the aorta and its major branches (e.g., abdominal aorta, subclavian artery) stretch to accommodate the stroke volume, storing potential energy. During diastole, these elastic walls recoil, pushing the stored blood forward and maintaining diastolic pressure and flow. As arteries travel further from the heart, their composition changes from predominantly elastic to more muscular. The radial artery, a muscular artery in the forearm, has significantly less elastin in its walls compared to the aorta. Therefore, it exhibits a much-reduced Windkessel effect and demonstrates a more pulsatile pressure waveform. The renal artery is still a large branch off the aorta and retains significant elastic properties.

Question 7

Arrange the following sarcomere components from the most peripheral region to the most central region. 1. Z line 2. M line 3. A band 4. H zone

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

1,3,4,2 The sarcomere is the fundamental contractile unit of striated muscle, extending between two adjacent Z lines. When arranging its components from the outermost boundary towards the absolute center, the correct sequence is based on their defined anatomical positions. The outermost boundary of a single sarcomere is the Z line (1), which anchors the thin (actin) filaments. Moving inward from the Z line, one encounters the A band. The A band (3) represents the entire length of the thick (myosin) filaments. Within the central region of the A band, there is a lighter area where the thin filaments do not overlap the thick filaments; this is known as the H zone (4). Finally, at the very center of the H zone, and thus the absolute center of the sarcomere, is the M line (2), which serves to anchor the thick filaments. Therefore, the correct sequence from the outer boundary to the innermost point is Z line → A band → H zone → M line.

Question 9

Which factors are responsible for the latent period observed in a single muscle twitch? 1. Excitation-contraction coupling 2. Time interval from the point of stimulus to the onset of contraction 3. Neuromuscular junction (NMJ) transmission 4. Transfer of calcium back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum via SERCA

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

1,2,3 The latent period of a muscle twitch is defined as the time interval from the application of a stimulus to the beginning of the actual muscle contraction. It is a brief delay during which a series of critical electrochemical events must occur before tension can be generated. These events include the transmission of the action potential across the neuromuscular junction (NMJ), which involves the release of acetylcholine, binding to receptors, and depolarization of the sarcolemma. Following this, the process of excitation-contraction coupling takes place. This involves the propagation of the muscle action potential along the T-tubules, triggering the release of calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, and the binding of calcium to troponin to initiate cross-bridge cycling. The reuptake of calcium into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by the SERCA pump (4) is the process that causes muscle relaxation and occurs after the contraction phase, not during the latent period.

Question 10

What is the correct sequence of band arrangement within a sarcomere?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Z-I-A-H-M This question asks for the correct spatial arrangement of the bands and lines within a sarcomere, the fundamental contractile unit of a muscle fiber. A sarcomere is defined as the segment between two successive Z lines. Moving from one Z line towards the center of the sarcomere, the first region encountered is the I band, which is a light band containing only thin (actin) filaments. The Z line itself bisects the I band. Next is the A band, a dark band that represents the entire length of the thick (myosin) filaments. Within the center of the A band, there is a paler region where the thin and thick filaments do not overlap; this is the H zone. Finally, at the very center of the H zone and the sarcomere is the M line, which anchors the thick filaments. Therefore, the correct sequence of structures from the edge to the center is Z-I-A-H-M. Question 11

Which option results in the greatest thermogenic effect?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Protein The thermic effect of food (TEF), also known as specific dynamic action, refers to the increase in metabolic rate (energy expenditure) that occurs after ingesting food. This energy is required for the digestion, absorption, processing, and storage of the nutrients. The magnitude of the TEF varies significantly depending on the macronutrient composition of the meal. Protein has the highest thermic effect of all macronutrients. The body expends approximately 20-30% of the energy content of the ingested protein to process it. This is substantially higher than the TEF for carbohydrates , which is around 5-10%, and for fats, which have the lowest TEF at only 0-3%. This high energy cost for protein metabolism is due to the complex processes of deamination and the synthesis of urea, among other metabolic pathways involved in handling amino acids.

Question 12

What is the cause of Pompe disease?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Lysosomal acid alpha-glucosidase Pompe disease, also known as Glycogen Storage Disease Type II, is an autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder. It is caused by a deficiency of the enzyme lysosomal acid alpha-glucosidase, also known as acid maltase. In a healthy individual, this enzyme is responsible for breaking down glycogen into glucose within the lysosomes. When the enzyme is deficient, glycogen accumulates progressively within the lysosomes of various tissues, leading to cellular damage and organ dysfunction. The most severely affected tissues are cardiac and skeletal muscle, resulting in profound muscle weakness (hypotonia), respiratory insufficiency, and, in the classic infantile form, a massive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy that typically leads to death within the first year of life. The other enzymes listed are deficient in different lysosomal storage diseases : B-Hexosaminidase-A in Tay-Sachs disease , B-Galactocerebrosidase in Krabbe disease , and B-Glucocerebrosidase in Gaucher disease.

Question 13

Which vitamin deficiency is linked to xanthurenic aciduria?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Vitamin B6 Xanthurenic aciduria refers to the excessive excretion of xanthurenic acid in the urine. This is a characteristic biochemical finding in vitamin B6 deficiency. Vitamin B6, in its active form pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), is an essential cofactor for numerous enzymes involved in amino acid metabolism. One such enzyme is kynureninase, which is part of the metabolic pathway that converts tryptophan to niacin. When there is a deficiency of vitamin B6, the activity of kynureninase is impaired. This creates a metabolic block, causing the upstream intermediate, 3-hydroxykynurenine, to be shunted into an alternative pathway where it is converted to xanthurenic acid. This abnormal metabolite then accumulates and is excreted in the urine. Therefore, measuring urinary xanthurenic acid after a tryptophan load is a functional test used to assess vitamin B6 status.

Question 16

The liver is capable of producing ketone bodies but cannot utilize them because it lacks which enzyme?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Thiophorase During periods of fasting, starvation, or uncontrolled diabetes, the liver engages in ketogenesis, producing ketone bodies (acetoacetate and β -hydroxybutyrate) from fatty acid oxidation. These ketone bodies are then exported into the bloodstream to be used as an energy source by extrahepatic tissues like the brain, heart, and skeletal muscle. For these peripheral tissues to utilize ketone bodies, they must first convert acetoacetate back into acetyl-CoA for entry into the TCA cycle. This activation step is catalyzed by the mitochondrial enzyme succinyl-CoA:3-ketoacid-CoA transferase, more commonly known as thiophorase. The liver is the site of ketone body synthesis but notably lacks this thiophorase enzyme. This enzymatic deficiency prevents the liver from utilizing the ketones it produces, ensuring that they are available for export to other tissues that critically need them for energy.

Question 17

Which enzyme deficiency is responsible for Pompe’s disease?

Lysosomal acid alpha glucosidase Pompe disease is also known as Glycogen Storage Disease Type II. Question 19

What immunologic defect is seen in B cells when CD40 is absent?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Decreased IgG and increase in IgM The interaction between the CD40 ligand (CD40L or CD154) on activated helper T cells and the CD40 receptor on B cells is absolutely essential for B cell activation, proliferation, and differentiation. Specifically, this T-cell help mediated by CD40-CD40L interaction is required for the process of immunoglobulin class switching. Without this signal, B cells are unable to switch from producing their initial IgM antibodies to producing other isotypes like IgG, IgA, or IgE. Question 20

What occurs when misfolded proteins accumulate abnormally?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease A number of diseases, known as proteinopathies or protein misfolding diseases, are caused by the accumulation of abnormally folded proteins that aggregate and cause cellular damage. Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD) is the classic example of a human prion disease, which falls into this category. It is caused by the conversion of the normal cellular prion protein (PrPC) into an abnormal, misfolded, and infectious isoform (PrPSc). This misfolded protein is resistant to degradation and acts as a template to convert more normal PrPC into the abnormal form, leading to its exponential accumulation as insoluble aggregates in the brain. This process results in spongiform encephalopathy and a rapidly progressive, fatal neurodegenerative disease. In contrast, sickle cell anemia is caused by a mutation leading to hemoglobin polymerization, and megaloblastic anemia is due to impaired DNA synthesis, not protein misfolding.

Question 21

In gonadal sex reversal syndrome with an XX karyotype, what is the most common underlying cause?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Translocation 46,XX testicular disorder of sex development (also known as XX male syndrome) is a condition where an individual has a female karyotype (46,XX) but develops a male phenotype, including testes. The primary determinant of male development is the SRY (Sex-determining Region Y) gene, which is normally located on the Y chromosome. The most common cause of 46,XX male syndrome, accounting for about 80% of cases, is an unequal crossing-over event during paternal meiosis. This results in the translocation of the SRY gene from the Y chromosome onto the X chromosome. The resulting X chromosome now carries the SRY gene. When an embryo inherits this SRY-positive X chromosome along with a normal X chromosome from the mother, it will have a 46,XX karyotype but will develop as a male due to the presence of the SRY gene, which initiates testis development.

Question 22

Which of the following genes functions as an inhibitor of apoptosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

MCL-1 The intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway of apoptosis is tightly regulated by the BCL-2 family of proteins, which includes both pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic members. The pro-apoptotic members include the "effectors" like BAX and BAK , which form pores in the mitochondrial membrane, and the "BH3-only" sensors like BID and NOXA, which activate the effectors or inhibit the anti-apoptotic members. The anti-apoptotic (pro-survival) members act as inhibitors of apoptosis by binding to and sequestering the pro-apoptotic proteins. This group includes BCL-2, BCL-XL, and MCL-1 (Myeloid Cell Leukemia-1). Therefore, MCL-1 is a key inhibitor of apoptosis, and its overexpression is a common mechanism for cancer cells to evade cell death. BAK, BID, and NOXA are all pro-apoptotic proteins that promote cell death.

Question 23

How can IDA be interpreted? a. Low serum ferritin b. Low transferrin saturation c. Low serum iron d. Increased TIBC

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

abcd Iron Deficiency Anemia (IDA) has a characteristic pattern on iron studies that reflects the body's depleted iron stores and its attempt to compensate. Serum ferritin is a measure of stored iron, and a low level is the most specific and earliest indicator of iron deficiency (a). Serum iron measures the amount of iron circulating in the blood bound to transferrin, and it is also low in IDA (c). The Total Iron-Binding Capacity (TIBC) is an indirect measure of transferrin, the protein that transports iron. In an effort to acquire more iron, the liver increases the production of transferrin, leading to an increased TIBC (d). Finally, the transferrin saturation, calculated as (serum iron / TIBC) x 100, is low because there is very little iron available to saturate the increased amount of transferrin (b). All four of these findings together form the classic biochemical profile of iron deficiency anemia.

Question 24

What does the absence of CD40 indicate in an individual?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

IgM increase The interaction between the CD40 receptor, expressed on B cells and other antigen-presenting cells, and its ligand, CD40L (CD154), expressed on activated T helper cells, is a critical step in humoral immunity. Question 25

To prevent contamination, blood should be drawn into vacutainers in the following order:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Blue, Red, Violet, Grey When drawing multiple tubes of blood from a single venipuncture, a specific "order of draw" must be followed to prevent cross-contamination of additives between tubes, which could interfere with test results. The standardized order is based on the type of additive in the tube stopper. While the exact order can have minor variations, the generally accepted sequence is: First, blood culture bottles (if needed). Therefore, the correct sequence among the given options is Blue, Red, Violet, Grey.

In a 60-year-old man with generalized lymphadenopathy, elevated white blood cell count, and smudge cells on peripheral smear, which further diagnostic test should be performed?

Flow cytometry from peripheral blood The clinical presentation of an older adult with lymphadenopathy, lymphocytosis, and the presence of smudge cells (fragile lymphocytes that are crushed during slide preparation) on a peripheral blood smear is highly suggestive of Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL). Question 27

Which of the following methods is not used to detect abnormal gene sequences?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Flow cytometry The detection of abnormal gene sequences involves various molecular biology techniques. Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism (RFLP) analysis is a classic technique that can detect sequence variations that alter restriction enzyme cutting sites. Pyrosequencing is a method of DNA sequencing that can identify the exact nucleotide sequence of a gene. Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH) uses fluorescent probes to detect the presence, absence, or location of specific DNA sequences on chromosomes, and is used for detecting larger scale abnormalities like deletions or translocations. It is primarily used to measure cell surface protein expression (immunophenotyping), cell size, and granularity, and for cell sorting. Question 28

What does IDA stand for and how is it interpreted in the medical field?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

All of the above IDA stands for Iron Deficiency Anemia. Its diagnosis is confirmed by a characteristic pattern on a panel of blood tests known as iron studies. This pattern reflects both the depletion of the body's iron stores and its compensatory physiological responses. Low serum ferritin is the most specific indicator, as ferritin is the primary iron storage protein, and its levels directly correlate with total body iron stores. The body responds to the deficiency by increasing the liver's production of transferrin, the protein that transports iron. This is measured as an increased Total Iron-Binding Capacity (TIBC). Since there is very little iron to be transported, the transferrin saturation, which reflects the percentage of transferrin that is actually carrying iron, is consequently low. Therefore, the combination of low ferritin, low serum iron, increased TIBC, and low transferrin saturation is the classic profile for interpreting a diagnosis of iron deficiency anemia.

Question 29

In Cystic Fibrosis, which membrane channel is primarily impacted?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Chloride Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an autosomal recessive genetic disorder caused by mutations in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene. The protein encoded by this gene functions as an ATP-gated ion channel located in the apical membrane of epithelial cells in various organs, including the lungs, pancreas, intestines, and sweat glands. The primary function of the CFTR protein is to transport chloride ions across the cell membrane. Defective CFTR protein results in impaired chloride transport, which in turn leads to abnormal transport of sodium and water across the epithelial surface. This disruption of ion and water flux is the fundamental defect that causes the production of abnormally thick, viscous mucus in the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts and abnormally salty sweat, which are the hallmarks of the disease.

Question 30

Question 31

A diabetic woman’s HbA1c decreased from 7.6 to 6.7 after receiving a medication that raised GIP and GLP-1 levels and lowered glucagon secretion. Which drug was most likely given?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Sitagliptin The physiological effects described—increased levels of the incretin hormones GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide) and GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1), and a subsequent decrease in glucagon levels—are characteristic of the mechanism of action of dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors. Sitagliptin is a member of this class of oral antidiabetic drugs. The DPP-4 enzyme is responsible for the rapid degradation of endogenous incretin hormones. By inhibiting this enzyme, sitagliptin increases the circulating half-life and concentration of active GIP and GLP-1. These elevated incretin levels then potentiate glucose-dependent insulin secretion and suppress glucagon release, leading to improved glycemic control, as evidenced by the patient's reduction in HbA1c. Liraglutide (D) is a GLP-1 receptor agonist, which mimics the action of GLP-1 but does not increase GIP levels.

Which monoclonal antibody, recently approved, is used in the treatment of Rett syndrome?

Trofinetide Rett syndrome is a rare, severe neurodevelopmental disorder primarily affecting females. Question 33

Which of the following statements about the clinical therapeutic index is correct?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Therapeutic index is LD50/ED50 The therapeutic index (TI) is a quantitative measure of the relative safety of a drug. It is a ratio that compares the dose of a drug that produces a toxic effect to the dose that produces a therapeutic or desired effect. The classical definition of the therapeutic index, derived from preclinical animal studies, is the ratio of the LD50 (the dose that is lethal to 50% of the population) to the ED50 (the dose that is therapeutically effective in 50% of the population). A higher therapeutic index indicates a wider margin of safety, meaning a much larger dose is needed to cause toxicity than is needed for the desired effect. Conversely, a drug with a low or narrow therapeutic index requires careful monitoring as the effective and toxic doses are close together. The other options describe concepts related to therapeutic drug monitoring or risk-benefit analysis rather than the formal definition of the TI.

Question 41

What is the type of hypoxia caused by carbon monoxide poisoning?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Anemic hypoxia Hypoxia is classified into four main types based on its underlying cause. Anemic hypoxia is defined as a condition where the partial pressure of oxygen in the arterial blood (PaO2) is normal, but the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood is reduced. This is precisely the mechanism of carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning. CO has an affinity for hemoglobin that is over 200 times greater than that of oxygen. When inhaled, CO avidly binds to hemoglobin to form carboxyhemoglobin (COHb), which is incapable of transporting oxygen. This effectively reduces the amount of functional hemoglobin available to carry oxygen to the tissues, thus decreasing the blood's total oxygen content, despite a normal PaO2. This is functionally equivalent to having a severe anemia. Histotoxic hypoxia (D) is caused by cyanide, which prevents cells from using oxygen. MICROBIOLOGY

Question 43

Correct answer: C

Oocyst This question describes the transmission of Toxoplasma gondii, the parasite that causes toxoplasmosis. Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Standard Deviation The formula provided, √[ Σ (xi - x ̄ )² / (N-1)], is the mathematical definition of the sample standard deviation. Let's break down the components of this important statistical measure. The term (xi - x ̄ ) represents the deviation of each individual data point (xi) from the sample mean (x ̄ ). These deviations are then squared, (xi - x ̄ )², to eliminate negative values. The sum of these squared deviations, Σ (xi - x ̄ )², is then divided by (N-1), which is the number of data points minus one, to calculate the sample variance. The use of (N-1) in the denominator provides an unbiased estimate of the population variance. Finally, the square root of the entire expression is taken to bring the measure back to the original units of the data. The standard deviation is a crucial measure of the dispersion or spread of data points around their mean in a given sample.
