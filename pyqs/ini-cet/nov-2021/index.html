Question 6

What is a characteristic feature observed in physiological jaundice?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Increased indirect bilirubin, increased urobilinogen, absent bilirubin in urine Physiological jaundice in newborns is a common, transient condition caused by the immaturity of the liver's bilirubin-conjugating system (low UGT enzyme activity), coupled with an increased bilirubin load from the breakdown of fetal red blood cells. This leads to an increase in unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin in the bloodstream. Unconjugated bilirubin is lipid-soluble and bound to albumin, so it cannot be filtered by the kidneys, resulting in absent bilirubin in the urine. The bilirubin that is successfully conjugated and excreted into the gut gets converted by bacteria to urobilinogen. A portion of this urobilinogen is reabsorbed and excreted in the urine, so urobilinogen is present (and may be increased due to the higher overall bilirubin turnover). This pattern contrasts with obstructive jaundice, which would feature increased direct bilirubin and bilirubin in the urine.

Question 7

Which of the following is not associated with renal auto-regulation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Renal sympathetic system fibers Renal autoregulation refers to the intrinsic mechanisms within the kidney that maintain a stable renal blood flow (RBF) and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) despite fluctuations in systemic blood pressure. The two primary intrinsic mechanisms are the myogenic reflex (B), where the afferent arteriole constricts in response to stretch, and tubuloglomerular feedback (TGF) (A), mediated by the macula densa. While the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) (C) is often considered an extrinsic system, angiotensin II plays a role in autoregulation by constricting the efferent arteriole, helping to maintain GFR when renal perfusion pressure drops. In contrast, the renal sympathetic nervous system (D) is a purely extrinsic control mechanism. Strong sympathetic stimulation (e.g., during shock or intense exercise) causes profound vasoconstriction of the afferent arterioles, which can override the intrinsic autoregulatory mechanisms to reduce GFR and divert blood flow away from the kidneys.

Question 8

The stroke volume depends on end-diastolic volume. This is best represented as?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Length-tension relationship The statement describes the Frank-Starling law of the heart. This fundamental principle states that the force of ventricular contraction (and thus stroke volume) is directly proportional to the initial length of the muscle fibers at the end of diastole. The end-diastolic volume (EDV), or preload, determines this initial fiber length. The underlying cellular mechanism is the length-tension relationship of the cardiac muscle (myocardium). As the myocytes are stretched by an increased EDV, the overlap between actin and myosin filaments becomes more optimal, and the sensitivity of the myofilaments to calcium increases. This results in a more forceful contraction (greater tension development), which ejects a larger stroke volume. While "length-force" (A) is functionally similar, "length-tension" is the more precise physiological term used to describe this property of muscle tissue.

Question 15

Which of the following lipase enzymes is inhibited by insulin?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Hormone-sensitive lipase Hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) is a key intracellular enzyme found in adipocytes (fat cells). Its function is to hydrolyze stored triglycerides into free fatty acids and glycerol, a process known as lipolysis, releasing these energy substrates into the bloodstream. The activity of HSL is tightly regulated by hormones. It is stimulated by counter-regulatory hormones like epinephrine and glucagon (via cAMP and PKA). Conversely, it is strongly inhibited by insulin. Insulin, which is released in the fed state, promotes energy storage. By inhibiting HSL, insulin suppresses the breakdown of stored fat. In contrast, insulin activates lipoprotein lipase (A), which is located on capillary walls and helps to clear triglycerides from the blood for storage in fat cells. Pancreatic (B) and gastric (C) lipases are digestive enzymes.

Question 16

What is the main substrate and metabolic fuel used by red blood cells (RBCs) during the fasting period?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Glucose Red blood cells (RBCs) have a unique metabolism because they lack mitochondria. The absence of mitochondria means they are incapable of aerobic respiration (i.e., they cannot use the Krebs cycle or oxidative phosphorylation). Therefore, they are entirely dependent on anaerobic glycolysis for all of their ATP production. The sole fuel source for glycolysis is glucose. This dependence is absolute and does not change with the body's metabolic state. Whether in the fed state or a prolonged fasting period, RBCs must continuously extract glucose from the bloodstream to meet their energy needs. They cannot utilize free fatty acids (C), ketone bodies (A), or amino acids (D) as fuel because the metabolic pathways for these substrates require functional mitochondria.

Question 17

Which of the following macronutrients requires the most energy for assimilation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Proteins The energy required to digest, absorb, and metabolize nutrients is known as the thermic effect of food (TEF) or specific dynamic action. This effect varies significantly among macronutrients. Proteins have the highest TEF, requiring the most energy for their assimilation. Approximately 20-30% of the energy content of the ingested protein is expended during its metabolism (e.g., deamination, urea synthesis). Carbohydrates (B) have a lower TEF, typically around 5-10%. Fats, including both mono-unsaturated (C) and polyunsaturated (D) fatty acids, have the lowest TEF, usually in the range of 0-3%. This means that the body is most efficient at storing energy from fat and least efficient at storing energy from protein. This high thermic effect of protein is one reason why high-protein diets are often recommended for weight management.

Scurvy Scurvy is the disease caused by a severe deficiency of Vitamin C (ascorbic acid). Question 19

Which enzyme is affected in a patient with thiamine deficiency who develops lactic acidosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Pyruvate dehydrogenase Thiamine (Vitamin B1), in its active form thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), is a critical cofactor for several key enzymes in carbohydrate metabolism. One of the most important of these is the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex. The PDH complex catalyzes the irreversible conversion of pyruvate (the end product of glycolysis) to acetyl-CoA, linking glycolysis to the Krebs cycle. In thiamine deficiency, the activity of the PDH complex is severely impaired. This creates a metabolic block, preventing pyruvate from entering the Krebs cycle for aerobic respiration. The accumulating pyruvate is then shunted to an alternative pathway, where it is converted to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase. This overproduction of lactate leads to lactic acidosis. Transketolase (D) is also a thiamine-dependent enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway, but its deficiency does not cause lactic acidosis.

Question 25

Vitamin B6 Sideroblastic anemia is a group of blood disorders characterized by the presence of ring sideroblasts in the bone marrow. It results from a defect in the synthesis of heme. Question 30

Which statement accurately describes the effect of iron regulatory protein (IRP) binding to iron- responsive elements (IRE) on the translation of ferritin and transferrin receptor 1 in a patient with iron deficiency anemia?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Ferritin translation is decreased by binding IRP to IRE In a state of iron deficiency, intracellular iron levels are low. This causes the iron regulatory protein (IRP) to be active and bind to specific stem-loop structures on mRNAs called iron-responsive elements (IREs). The location of the IRE determines the effect of IRP binding. The IRE for ferritin (the iron storage protein) is located in the 5' untranslated region (5' UTR) of its mRNA. Question 34

Which repair mechanism is linked with CRISPR-Cash?

Which imaging technique is most suitable for diagnosing primary hyperparathyroidism in a woman presenting with confusion, constipation, headache, and elevated serum calcium (13.1 mg/ dL) along with elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Sestamibi Scan The patient's symptoms and laboratory findings (hypercalcemia with elevated ALP) are highly suggestive of primary hyperparathyroidism, which is most commonly caused by a single, benign parathyroid adenoma. The goal of preoperative imaging is to localize this adenoma. The most common and effective functional imaging technique for this purpose is a Technetium-99m Sestamibi Parathyroid Scan. This allows for the precise localization of the adenoma before surgery.

Question 145

Which phase of the cell cycle displays the highest resistance to radiation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

S phase The sensitivity of a cell to the damaging effects of ionizing radiation varies significantly throughout the cell cycle. Cells are most sensitive to radiation during the M (mitosis) phase and the G2 phase. They are less sensitive during the G1 phase. The phase of the cell cycle where cells are the most resistant to radiation is the late S (synthesis) phase. During the S phase, the cell is actively replicating its DNA. The presence of homologous DNA sister chromatids and the upregulation of DNA repair enzymes (like those involved in homologous recombination) during this phase are thought to confer a higher capacity to repair radiation-induced DNA double-strand breaks, thus making the cell more radioresistant. This principle is important in radiation oncology, as it influences the effectiveness of fractionated radiotherapy schedules.
