Question 1

Which of the following branches originate from the anterior division of the internal iliac artery? 1. Superior gluteal artery 2. Inferior gluteal artery 3. Superior vesical artery 4. Middle rectal artery 5. Inferior vesical artery 6. Vaginal artery

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

2,3,4,5,6 The internal iliac artery bifurcates into an anterior and a posterior division. The posterior division supplies the pelvic wall and gluteal region, giving off the iliolumbar, lateral sacral, and superior gluteal arteries (1). All the other arteries listed are branches of the anterior division, which primarily supplies the pelvic viscera, perineum, and medial thigh. These visceral and parietal branches include the umbilical artery (from which the superior vesical artery (3) arises), obturator artery, inferior vesical artery (5) (in males), uterine artery (from which the vaginal artery (6) arises in females), middle rectal artery (4), internal pudendal artery, and the inferior gluteal artery (2). Therefore, all the arteries listed, with the exception of the superior gluteal artery, originate from the anterior division of the internal iliac artery.

Question 6

What is a characteristic feature observed in physiological jaundice?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Increased indirect bilirubin, increased urobilinogen, absent bilirubin in urine Physiological jaundice in newborns is a common, transient condition caused by the immaturity of the liver's bilirubin-conjugating system (low UGT enzyme activity), coupled with an increased bilirubin load from the breakdown of fetal red blood cells. This leads to an increase in unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin in the bloodstream. Unconjugated bilirubin is lipid-soluble and bound to albumin, so it cannot be filtered by the kidneys, resulting in absent bilirubin in the urine. The bilirubin that is successfully conjugated and excreted into the gut gets converted by bacteria to urobilinogen. A portion of this urobilinogen is reabsorbed and excreted in the urine, so urobilinogen is present (and may be increased due to the higher overall bilirubin turnover). This pattern contrasts with obstructive jaundice, which would feature increased direct bilirubin and bilirubin in the urine.

Question 7

Which of the following is not associated with renal auto-regulation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Renal sympathetic system fibers Renal autoregulation refers to the intrinsic mechanisms within the kidney that maintain a stable renal blood flow (RBF) and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) despite fluctuations in systemic blood pressure. The two primary intrinsic mechanisms are the myogenic reflex (B), where the afferent arteriole constricts in response to stretch, and tubuloglomerular feedback (TGF) (A), mediated by the macula densa. While the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) (C) is often considered an extrinsic system, angiotensin II plays a role in autoregulation by constricting the efferent arteriole, helping to maintain GFR when renal perfusion pressure drops. In contrast, the renal sympathetic nervous system (D) is a purely extrinsic control mechanism. Strong sympathetic stimulation (e.g., during shock or intense exercise) causes profound vasoconstriction of the afferent arterioles, which can override the intrinsic autoregulatory mechanisms to reduce GFR and divert blood flow away from the kidneys.

Question 8

The stroke volume depends on end-diastolic volume. This is best represented as?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Length-tension relationship The statement describes the Frank-Starling law of the heart. This fundamental principle states that the force of ventricular contraction (and thus stroke volume) is directly proportional to the initial length of the muscle fibers at the end of diastole. The end-diastolic volume (EDV), or preload, determines this initial fiber length. The underlying cellular mechanism is the length-tension relationship of the cardiac muscle (myocardium). As the myocytes are stretched by an increased EDV, the overlap between actin and myosin filaments becomes more optimal, and the sensitivity of the myofilaments to calcium increases. This results in a more forceful contraction (greater tension development), which ejects a larger stroke volume. While "length-force" (A) is functionally similar, "length-tension" is the more precise physiological term used to describe this property of muscle tissue.

Question 9

Which sensory receptors are responsible for tactile discrimination?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Both A & B Tactile discrimination, which includes the ability to perceive fine details like texture, shape, and two-point discrimination, relies on sensory receptors with small, well-defined receptive fields, located superficially in the skin. Both Merkel discs and Meissner's corpuscles fit this description. Merkel discs (A) are slow-adapting mechanoreceptors that are sensitive to sustained pressure and texture, making them crucial for reading Braille and sensing fine details. Meissner's corpuscles (B) are rapidly adapting receptors that are sensitive to light touch, flutter, and grip control. Both are highly concentrated in the fingertips and other sensitive areas. In contrast, Pacinian corpuscles (C) are located deeper in the dermis, have large receptive fields, and are rapidly adapting receptors that detect deep pressure and high-frequency vibration, contributing less to fine tactile discrimination. Therefore, both Merkel discs and Meissner's corpuscles are key players.

Question 14

Which of the following poisonous agents does not inhibit cytochrome oxidase?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Methane Cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV) is the terminal enzyme of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Its inhibition blocks aerobic respiration, leading to cytotoxic hypoxia. Cyanide (D) is the classic inhibitor, binding to the ferric iron ($Fe^{3+}$) within the enzyme. Hydrogen sulfide (A) acts through a similar inhibitory mechanism. Carbon monoxide (B) can also inhibit cytochrome oxidase, although its primary toxic effect is its high-affinity binding to hemoglobin. In contrast, methane ($CH_4$) is a simple asphyxiant. It is a physiologically inert gas that is not directly toxic to cells. It causes hypoxia simply by displacing oxygen in the inhaled air, leading to a reduced fraction of inspired oxygen ($FiO_2$) and subsequent hypoxemia. It does not have any direct inhibitory effect on cellular enzymes like cytochrome oxidase.

Question 15

Which of the following lipase enzymes is inhibited by insulin?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Hormone-sensitive lipase Hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) is a key intracellular enzyme found in adipocytes (fat cells). Its function is to hydrolyze stored triglycerides into free fatty acids and glycerol, a process known as lipolysis, releasing these energy substrates into the bloodstream. The activity of HSL is tightly regulated by hormones. It is stimulated by counter-regulatory hormones like epinephrine and glucagon (via cAMP and PKA). Conversely, it is strongly inhibited by insulin. Insulin, which is released in the fed state, promotes energy storage. By inhibiting HSL, insulin suppresses the breakdown of stored fat. In contrast, insulin activates lipoprotein lipase (A), which is located on capillary walls and helps to clear triglycerides from the blood for storage in fat cells. Pancreatic (B) and gastric (C) lipases are digestive enzymes.

Question 16

What is the main substrate and metabolic fuel used by red blood cells (RBCs) during the fasting period?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Glucose Red blood cells (RBCs) have a unique metabolism because they lack mitochondria. The absence of mitochondria means they are incapable of aerobic respiration (i.e., they cannot use the Krebs cycle or oxidative phosphorylation). Therefore, they are entirely dependent on anaerobic glycolysis for all of their ATP production. The sole fuel source for glycolysis is glucose. This dependence is absolute and does not change with the body's metabolic state. Whether in the fed state or a prolonged fasting period, RBCs must continuously extract glucose from the bloodstream to meet their energy needs. They cannot utilize free fatty acids (C), ketone bodies (A), or amino acids (D) as fuel because the metabolic pathways for these substrates require functional mitochondria.

Question 17

Which of the following macronutrients requires the most energy for assimilation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Proteins The energy required to digest, absorb, and metabolize nutrients is known as the thermic effect of food (TEF) or specific dynamic action. This effect varies significantly among macronutrients. Proteins have the highest TEF, requiring the most energy for their assimilation. Approximately 20-30% of the energy content of the ingested protein is expended during its metabolism (e.g., deamination, urea synthesis). Carbohydrates (B) have a lower TEF, typically around 5-10%. Fats, including both mono-unsaturated (C) and polyunsaturated (D) fatty acids, have the lowest TEF, usually in the range of 0-3%. This means that the body is most efficient at storing energy from fat and least efficient at storing energy from protein. This high thermic effect of protein is one reason why high-protein diets are often recommended for weight management.

Question 18

Which of the following is associated with a defect in collagen maturity?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Scurvy Scurvy is the disease caused by a severe deficiency of Vitamin C (ascorbic acid). Vitamin C is an essential cofactor for two key enzymes, prolyl hydroxylase and lysyl hydroxylase, which are involved in the post-translational modification of collagen. These enzymes are responsible for hydroxylating proline and lysine residues within the procollagen chains. This hydroxylation step is crucial for the formation of stable cross-links between collagen molecules, which gives the final collagen triple helix its strength and stability. In the absence of Vitamin C, this process is impaired, leading to the production of weak, unstable, and immature collagen. This results in the characteristic symptoms of scurvy, such as poor wound healing, bleeding gums, and perifollicular hemorrhages. Rickets (B) is a defect in bone mineralization due to Vitamin D deficiency.

Question 19

Which enzyme is affected in a patient with thiamine deficiency who develops lactic acidosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Pyruvate dehydrogenase Thiamine (Vitamin B1), in its active form thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), is a critical cofactor for several key enzymes in carbohydrate metabolism. One of the most important of these is the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex. The PDH complex catalyzes the irreversible conversion of pyruvate (the end product of glycolysis) to acetyl-CoA, linking glycolysis to the Krebs cycle. In thiamine deficiency, the activity of the PDH complex is severely impaired. This creates a metabolic block, preventing pyruvate from entering the Krebs cycle for aerobic respiration. The accumulating pyruvate is then shunted to an alternative pathway, where it is converted to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase. This overproduction of lactate leads to lactic acidosis. Transketolase (D) is also a thiamine-dependent enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway, but its deficiency does not cause lactic acidosis.

Question 20

Which of the following statements accurately describe the impact of Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency? 1. Decrease in reduced glutathione 2. Decrease NADPH 3. Decreased lipid peroxidation 4. Decreased RBC membrane damage

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

1 & 2 are correct Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) is the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). The primary function of the PPP in red blood cells is to produce NADPH (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate). Therefore, a deficiency in G6PD leads to a decrease in NADPH (2 is correct). NADPH is essential for maintaining a supply of reduced glutathione (GSH), which is catalyzed by the enzyme glutathione reductase. Reduced glutathione is a critical antioxidant that detoxifies reactive oxygen species. A decrease in NADPH leads to a decrease in reduced glutathione (1 is correct). In the absence of sufficient GSH, RBCs are highly susceptible to oxidative stress from certain drugs or infections. This oxidative stress leads to increased lipid peroxidation (3 is incorrect) and increased RBC membrane damage (4 is incorrect), ultimately causing hemolysis.

Question 23

Question 24

Which mediators are involved in the initial stage of type 1 hypersensitivity? 1. Mast cell 2. Histamine 3. Neutrophils 4. Prostaglandins 5. Leukotrienes

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

1,2,4,5 Type I hypersensitivity is the immediate, IgE-mediated allergic reaction. The process is initiated when an allergen cross-links IgE antibodies bound to the surface of mast cells (1) (and basophils). This cross-linking triggers the degranulation of the mast cell, which releases a flood of pre-formed and newly synthesized mediators. The initial or immediate phase is dominated by the release of pre-formed mediators from granules, the most important of which is histamine (2). Almost immediately, the mast cell also begins to synthesize lipid-derived mediators from its membrane phospholipids via the arachidonic acid pathway. These include prostaglandins (4) (e.g., PGD2) and leukotrienes (5) (e.g., LTC4, LTD4, LTE4). These mediators are responsible for the classic allergic symptoms. Neutrophils (3) are recruited later in the late-phase reaction, not the initial stage.

Question 25

Which of the following is utilized for the treatment of sideroblastic anemia?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Vitamin B6 Sideroblastic anemia is a group of blood disorders characterized by the presence of ring sideroblasts in the bone marrow. It results from a defect in the synthesis of heme. The first and rate-limiting step in heme synthesis is the condensation of succinyl-CoA and glycine to form delta-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), a reaction catalyzed by the enzyme ALA synthase. This enzyme requires pyridoxal 5'-phosphate (PLP) as a cofactor. PLP is the active form of Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine). In some forms of sideroblastic anemia, particularly the hereditary X-linked type, there is a mutation in the ALA synthase enzyme that can be partially overcome by administering high doses of its cofactor, Vitamin B6. Therefore, a trial of pyridoxine is a cornerstone of therapy for certain types of sideroblastic anemia.

Question 30

Which statement accurately describes the effect of iron regulatory protein (IRP) binding to iron- responsive elements (IRE) on the translation of ferritin and transferrin receptor 1 in a patient with iron deficiency anemia?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Ferritin translation is decreased by binding IRP to IRE In a state of iron deficiency, intracellular iron levels are low. This causes the iron regulatory protein (IRP) to be active and bind to specific stem-loop structures on mRNAs called iron-responsive elements (IREs). The location of the IRE determines the effect of IRP binding. The IRE for ferritin (the iron storage protein) is located in the 5' untranslated region (5' UTR) of its mRNA. When IRP binds here, it sterically hinders the assembly of the ribosomal complex, thereby blocking and decreasing the translation of ferritin. This makes physiological sense, as the cell does not want to store iron when it is deficient. The IRE for the transferrin receptor 1 (which imports iron into the cell) is in the 3' UTR. IRP binding here stabilizes the mRNA and prevents its degradation, thus increasing its translation. This allows the cell to make more receptors to take up more iron.

Question 31

In respiratory distress syndrome, which structure is affected?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Increased HbF A rightward shift of the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve signifies a decrease in hemoglobin's affinity for oxygen, which facilitates oxygen unloading to the tissues. Factors that cause a right shift include increased 2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (BPG) (B), acidosis (decreased pH, the Bohr effect) (C), and increased partial pressure of carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) (D), as well as increased temperature. These conditions are typically found in metabolically active tissues. In contrast, fetal hemoglobin (HbF) (A) has a higher intrinsic affinity for oxygen than adult hemoglobin (HbA). This is because HbF binds 2,3-BPG less avidly. The higher oxygen affinity of HbF facilitates the transfer of oxygen from the maternal circulation across the placenta to the fetus. Therefore, an increased proportion of HbF causes a leftward shift of the dissociation curve, not a rightward shift.

Question 34

Which repair mechanism is linked with CRISPR-Cash?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Non-homologous end joining The CRISPR-Cash system is a genome editing tool that works by inducing a targeted double-strand break (DSB) in the DNA at a specific location guided by an RNA molecule. Once the DSB is created, the cell's own DNA repair machinery takes over. The cell has two main pathways to repair DSBs. The most common and default pathway is non-homologous end joining (NHEJ). This pathway is error-prone and often introduces small insertions or deletions (indels) at the break site as it ligates the broken ends back together. This is the mechanism that is typically exploited to create gene "knockouts." The other, less frequent pathway is homology-directed repair (HDR), which can be used to "knock in" a specific DNA sequence if a template is provided. The other options (B, C, D) are repair mechanisms for different types of DNA damage, not DSBs. PHARMACOLOGY

Question 35

Which thiazide diuretic should be given to a 34-year-old male with hypertension and stage 4 chronic kidney disease, whose estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is below 30 mL/min?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Metolazone Thiazide and thiazide-like diuretics, such as hydrochlorothiazide (D), chlorthalidone (B), and chlorothiazide (A), exert their antihypertensive and diuretic effects by inhibiting the Na-Cl cotransporter in the distal convoluted tubule. However, their efficacy is significantly diminished when the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) falls below 30 mL/min, as insufficient drug is filtered to reach its site of action. A major exception to this rule is metolazone. Metolazone is a thiazide-like diuretic that, unlike others in its class, retains its efficacy even in patients with advanced chronic kidney disease and a low GFR. It is often used in combination with a loop diuretic in patients with refractory edema due to renal failure or heart failure. Therefore, it is the most appropriate choice in this clinical scenario.

Question 38

For which medical condition is inhaled nitric oxide approved for treatment?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Pulmonary hypertension Inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) is a potent and selective pulmonary vasodilator. When inhaled, it diffuses across the alveolar-capillary membrane and relaxes the smooth muscle in the adjacent pulmonary vessels, leading to a decrease in pulmonary vascular resistance and improved ventilation-perfusion matching. Its primary FDA-approved indication is for the treatment of term and near-term neonates (>34 weeks gestation) with hypoxic respiratory failure associated with clinical or echo cardiographic evidence of pulmonary hypertension. In this setting, iNO improves oxygenation and significantly reduces the need for more invasive therapies like extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). It is not used for systemic hypertension (A), migraine (C), or Parkinson's disease (D), as its effects are localized to the pulmonary circulation when administered via inhalation.

Question 39

Which of the following is not a cause of the paradoxical bronchoconstrictor associated with ipratropium bromide?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Hypertonic saline Paradoxical bronchoconstrictor is a rare but reported adverse effect of nebulized ipratropium bromide solution. This reaction is generally attributed to the drug's formulation or its pharmacology. The nebulizer solution contains excipients, such as the preservative benzalkonium chloride (C) and the chelating agent disodium edetate (EDTA) (B), both of which are known to have bronchoconstrictor properties in some susceptible individuals. Question 41

Category A, B, C, D, X in drug classification are indicative of which of the following?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Safety in pregnancy The A, B, C, D, and X categories were part of the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's (FDA) classification system to indicate the potential of a drug to cause birth defects if used during pregnancy. This system provided a letter grade to summarize the level of risk to a fetus. Category A represented drugs for which adequate studies in pregnant women have shown no risk. Category B, C, and D represented increasing levels of potential risk based on animal and human data. Category X represented drugs for which studies have demonstrated fetal abnormalities, and the risk of use in pregnant women clearly outweighs any possible benefit. Although this system has been replaced in the United States by the Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR), it is still widely recognized and tested as an indicator of a drug's safety in pregnancy.

Question 42

At what dosage does dopamine function on beta-1 receptors?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

2-10 mcg/kg/min Dopamine is a catecholamine with dose-dependent receptor effects, which is crucial for its clinical use as an inotrope and vasopressor. 1. Low dose (renal dose) : At infusion rates of approximately <2-5 mcg/kg/min, dopamine primarily stimulates D1 receptors, causing vasodilation in the renal, mesenteric, and coronary circulations. 2. Moderate dose (cardiac dose) : At infusion rates of approximately 2-10 mcg/kg/min, dopamine primarily stimulates beta-1 adrenergic receptors on the heart. This results in increased myocardial contractility (positive inotropy) and heart rate (positive chronotropy), leading to an increased cardiac output. 3. High dose (vasopressor dose) : At infusion rates >10 mcg/kg/min, the effects on alpha-1 adrenergic receptors predominate, causing systemic vasoconstriction and an increase in blood pressure.

Question 43

Which of the following medications would increase the bioavailability of saquinavir when given concurrently?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Ritonavir Saquinavir is an antiretroviral drug from the protease inhibitor class. When used alone, it has very poor oral bioavailability due to extensive metabolism by the cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) enzyme in the gut wall and liver. Ritonavir is another protease inhibitor that is also a very potent inhibitor of the CYP3A4 enzyme. This boosting strategy is a common principle in HIV therapy. The other substances listed do not have this specific and potent CYP3A4 inhibitory effect.

Question 44

How does cyclosporine work in terms of its mechanism of action?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Inhibition of calcineurin Cyclosporine is a powerful immunosuppressive agent widely used in organ transplantation to prevent rejection. Its mechanism of action involves the inhibition of calcineurin. Inside a T-lymphocyte, cyclosporine first binds to an intracellular protein called cyclophilin. This cyclosporine-cyclophilin complex then binds to and inhibits calcineurin, which is a calcium- and calmodulin-dependent phosphatase enzyme. The inhibition of calcineurin prevents it from dephosphorylating the Nuclear Factor of Activated T-cells (NFAT). As a result, NFAT cannot translocate to the nucleus to act as a transcription factor. This blocks the transcription of genes for several crucial cytokines, most importantly Interleukin-2 (IL-2). By preventing IL-2 production, cyclosporine effectively suppresses the proliferation and activation of T-cells, a central step in the immune response leading to organ rejection.

Question 45

This combination is thought to provide antidepressant effects with potentially fewer side effects, particularly sexual dysfunction, compared to traditional SSRIs. Lurasidone (A), Asenapine (C), and Blonanserin (D) are all atypical antipsychotics. While lurasidone is also approved for treating bipolar depression, vilazodone's unique mechanism as a primary antidepressant makes it the most fitting answer for a "new and innovative" medication for depression among the choices.
