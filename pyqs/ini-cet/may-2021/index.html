Question 1

Pelvic diaphragm is formed by the following muscles:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

All of the above The pelvic diaphragm is a crucial funnel-shaped muscular structure that forms the floor of the pelvic cavity, supporting the pelvic organs. It is composed of the levator ani and coccygeus muscles. The levator ani muscle is not a single muscle but a complex sheet made up of several interconnected parts. The main components of the levator ani are the iliococcygeus and the pubococcygeus muscles. The pubococcygeus is further subdivided based on its fiber attachments. In females, the most medial fibers of the pubococcygeus that flank the vagina are specifically referred to as the pubovaginalis muscle. This part is critical for vaginal support and sphincter function. Since the pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus, and pubovaginalis are all integral components of the levator ani complex, and the levator ani is the main constituent of the pelvic diaphragm, all the listed options are correct.

Question 2

When the outer sheath and nerve fiber remain intact but the inner axons are damaged, this condition is known as what?:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Axonotmesis This question refers to Seddon's classification of peripheral nerve injuries, which has three main types. Neurapraxia (A) is the mildest form, involving a temporary block of nerve conduction, usually due to focal demyelination, without any axonal damage. The nerve sheaths and axon are intact, and recovery is complete. Neurotmesis (D) is the most severe form, where the entire nerve trunk, including the axons and all surrounding connective tissue sheaths (endoneurium, perineurium, and epineurium), is completely transected. Recovery does not occur without surgical repair. Axonotmesis is the intermediate injury, defined by the disruption of the inner axons and their myelin sheaths, but with the connective tissue framework of the nerve, including the endoneurial tube ("outer sheath"), remaining intact. This leads to Wallerian degeneration distal to the injury, but the intact sheath provides a scaffold for potential axonal regeneration, making recovery possible but slow.

Question 3

Which of the following does not enhance the production of gastric acid?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Serotonin The secretion of gastric acid from parietal cells in the stomach is stimulated by three main physiological agonists. Gastrin (A), a hormone released from G cells in the stomach antrum, stimulates acid secretion both directly and indirectly by promoting histamine release. Histamine (B), released from enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells, is a powerful paracrine stimulator of parietal cells via H2 receptors. Acetylcholine (C), released from vagal nerve endings, stimulates parietal cells directly via M3 muscarinic receptors. These three substances work synergistically to promote acid production. Serotonin (5-HT) (D), while abundant in the gastrointestinal tract where it primarily regulates motility and secretion in the intestines, is not a primary stimulant for gastric acid production. In fact, some subtypes of serotonin receptors have been shown to have an inhibitory effect on acid secretion. Therefore, among the options provided, serotonin is the one that does not enhance gastric acid production.

Question 4

Which of the following options demonstrates the highest airway resistance?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Bronchi Airway resistance is determined by the caliber and arrangement of the airways. While one might intuitively think that the smallest airways would have the highest resistance, this is incorrect. The total cross-sectional area of the airways is the key factor. Although an individual small bronchiole has a very high resistance, the airways branch extensively, and there are millions of small bronchioles (D) and respiratory bronchioles (C) arranged in parallel. This parallel arrangement results in a massive total cross-sectional area, leading to very low overall resistance in the peripheral airways. In contrast, the medium-sized bronchi (specifically, the segmental bronchi, from the 3rd to 7th generation of branching) represent a point where the total cross-sectional area is at its minimum. This bottleneck in the airway tree is the site of the greatest level of airway resistance in the entire respiratory system. The trachea and main bronchi have a larger diameter, resulting in lower resistance.

Question 5

In a situation where cardiac contractility decreases, which of the following changes is most likely to occur?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Shift of the end-systolic volume and pressure curves to the right Reduced cardiac contractility, or negative inotropy, means that for any given preload (end-diastolic volume), the heart muscle contracts less forcefully. This is depicted on a ventricular pressure-volume loop analysis. The end-systolic pressure-volume relationship (ESPVR) is a line that represents the maximum pressure the ventricle can generate at any given volume, and its slope is a measure of contractility. When contractility is reduced, the ventricle is weaker, so for any given systolic pressure, the end-systolic volume will be larger. This causes the ESPVR curve to become less steep and shift downwards and to the right. Consequently, for a given preload and afterload, the stroke volume (C) will decrease, not increase, and the end-systolic volume will be larger. The end-diastolic volume (A) might increase as a compensatory mechanism, but it does not decrease.

Question 6

Which of the following ions does not show a reduced concentration in the lumen of the proximal convoluted tubule?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Chloride The proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) is responsible for reabsorbing the majority (about 65-70%) of the glomerular filtrate. This includes the near-complete reabsorption of valuable organic solutes like glucose (B) and amino acids (A) via sodium-coupled secondary active transport. A large amount of bicarbonate (D) is also reabsorbed in the PCT, a process linked to sodium-hydrogen exchange and carbonic anhydrase activity. As these solutes and a significant amount of sodium are reabsorbed, their concentration in the tubular fluid decreases progressively along the PCT. Water follows these solutes osmotically. However, chloride (C) is reabsorbed more slowly in the early PCT compared to sodium and water. As water is reabsorbed, the concentration of the less readily reabsorbed chloride ions in the remaining tubular fluid actually increases along the length of the PCT. This creates a favorable concentration gradient for its passive paracellular reabsorption in the later parts of the PCT.

Question 8

Methyl-tetrahydro-folic acid gets accumulated in deficiency of which of the following?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

B12 This question refers to the "folate trap" hypothesis, which explains the biochemical link between Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. The enzyme methionine synthase is responsible for converting homocysteine to methionine. This reaction requires two things : Vitamin B12 (in the form of methylcobalamin) as a cofactor, and N5-methyl-tetrahydrofolate (methyl-THF) as a methyl group donor. In the process, methyl-THF is converted back to tetrahydrofolate (THF), which can then be used to synthesize other folate derivatives needed for DNA synthesis. In Vitamin B12 deficiency, the methionine synthase enzyme cannot function properly. As a result, folate becomes "trapped" in its N5-methyl-THF form, as this is an irreversible reaction. This accumulation of methyl-THF prevents the regeneration of THF, leading to a functional folate deficiency and impairing DNA synthesis, which results in megaloblastic anemia.

Question 9

Which of the following statements is not correct about a patient with advanced liver disease?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Oxidative decarboxylation of alpha-ketoglutarate In advanced liver disease, the liver's capacity to detoxify ammonia via the urea cycle is severely impaired, leading to hyperammonemia (A). This excess ammonia is shunted to other tissues, particularly the brain and skeletal muscle. In the brain's astrocytes, ammonia is detoxified by combining with glutamate to form glutamine, a reaction catalyzed by glutamine synthetase. To replenish the glutamate used in this reaction, alpha-ketoglutarate (an intermediate of the Krebs cycle) is reductively aminated to glutamate. This leads to an increased synthesis of glutamine from alpha-ketoglutarate (D). This depletion of alpha-ketoglutarate from the Krebs cycle impairs aerobic respiration and leads to decreased ATP synthesis (B) in astrocytes, contributing to hepatic encephalopathy. The statement that oxidative decarboxylation of alpha-ketoglutarate (C) occurs is incorrect in this context; rather, it is the depletion of alpha-ketoglutarate via reductive amination that is the key pathological step.

Question 10

Which of the following options is not a substrate used in the synthesis of glucose?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Acetyl coenzyme A Gluconeogenesis is the metabolic pathway that synthesizes glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors. The major substrates for gluconeogenesis are lactate (D) (from anaerobic glycolysis, via the Cori cycle), glucogenic amino acids like alanine (C) (from muscle protein breakdown, via the glucose-alanine cycle), and glycerol (B) (from the breakdown of triglycerides in adipose tissue). These substrates can all be converted into pyruvate or other intermediates of the Krebs cycle, which can then be used to form glucose. However, acetyl-CoA (A), which is derived from the beta-oxidation of fatty acids, cannot be used for the net synthesis of glucose in humans. This is because the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is an irreversible reaction. There is no pathway in humans to convert acetyl-CoA back to pyruvate or oxaloacetate for entry into gluconeogenesis.

Question 11

1,2,3 and 4 The inferior thyroid artery is a major branch of the thyrocervical trunk, which arises from the first part of the subclavian artery. Question 12

What repair mechanism is linked to CRISPR- Cas9?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Non-homologous end joining The CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing system functions by creating a targeted double-strand break (DSB) in the DNA. Once this break is made, the cell utilizes its own endogenous DNA repair pathways to fix it. The cell has two major pathways for repairing DSBs. The predominant, default, and most frequently used pathway is non-homologous end joining (NHEJ). This pathway is efficient but error-prone; it often introduces small, random insertions or deletions (indels) at the site of the break as it ligates the two ends back together. This is the mechanism that researchers exploit to create gene knockouts, as the resulting indel often causes a frameshift mutation that inactivates the gene. The other repair pathways listed—base excision repair (B), nucleotide excision repair (C), and mismatch repair (D)—are responsible for fixing different types of DNA damage, such as single-base errors or bulky adducts, not double-strand breaks.

Question 13

Which of the following substances is not characterized by amphipathicity?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Triglycerides An amphipathic (or amphiphilic) molecule is one that possesses both a hydrophilic ("water-loving") polar head and a hydrophobic ("water-fearing") nonpolar tail. This dual nature allows these molecules to form structures like micelles and lipid bilayers in aqueous environments. Phosphoglycerols (B) (a major class of phospholipids), sphingolipids (A), and glycolipids (D) are all major components of cell membranes and are all amphipathic. They each have a polar head group (containing phosphate, choline, or a carbohydrate moiety) attached to nonpolar fatty acid tails. In contrast, triglycerides (also called triacylglycerols) are the main form of stored fat. They are composed of a glycerol backbone esterified to three fatty acid chains. This structure is almost entirely nonpolar and hydrophobic. Since they lack a distinct polar head group, triglycerides are not amphipathic and are stored as oily droplets within cells.

Question 14

Which process explains how organophosphorus compounds inhibit the acetylcholinesterase enzyme?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Suicidal inhibition Organophosphorus compounds inhibit acetylcholinesterase (AChE) by forming a covalent bond with the enzyme at its active site. Normally, acetylcholinesterase breaks down acetylcholine into acetate and choline, thereby terminating synaptic transmission. However, organophosphates phosphorylate the serine hydroxyl group present in the active site of the enzyme. This phosphorylation initially forms a stable enzyme–inhibitor complex. Over time, a process called “aging” occurs, in which the bond becomes even more stable and resistant to hydrolysis. As a result, the enzyme becomes permanently inactivated and cannot regenerate under normal physiological conditions. This mechanism is referred to as suicidal inhibition (mechanism-based inhibition) because the inhibitor binds to the active site and is processed as if it were a substrate, but instead permanently inactivates the enzyme. It differs from reversible competitive inhibition because the bond formed is covalent and essentially irreversible. The accumulation of acetylcholine at synapses leads to continuous stimulation of muscarinic and nicotinic receptors, producing the characteristic cholinergic toxicity seen in organophosphate poisoning.

Question 15

In the formula for urea clearance $(U\times V)/ P$ what does U represent?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Urine urea in mg/100 ml The formula presented is the standard formula for calculating the renal clearance of any substance : Clearance = (U × V) / P. In this formula, each component represents a specific measurement, and it is crucial that the units are consistent. V represents the urine flow rate (urine volume per unit time), typically expressed in mL/min. P represents the plasma concentration of the substance, usually expressed in mg/dL or mg/100 mL. U represents the urine concentration of the substance. To ensure the units cancel out correctly to give a final clearance value in mL/min, the urine concentration (U) must be expressed in the same units as the plasma concentration (P). Therefore, U represents the concentration of urea in the urine, expressed in mg/100 mL (or mg/dL). Expressing it in different units like g/24h (C) would require a conversion before it could be used in this formula.

Question 16

To inhibit translation, which region of the mRNA does miRNA bind to?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

$3^{\prime}$ UTR MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small, non-coding RNA molecules that play a crucial role in the post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression. They function by binding to complementary sequences on target messenger RNA (mRNA) molecules. This binding is mediated by a protein complex called the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). In animals, the miRNA binding sites are most commonly located in the 3' untranslated region (3' UTR) of the target mRNA. The binding of the miRNA-RISC complex to the 3' UTR typically does not involve perfect complementarity. This imperfect pairing leads to the inhibition of the translation of the mRNA into protein and often promotes the deadenylation and eventual degradation of the mRNA molecule. Binding to the gene promoter (B) or gene body (A) relates to transcriptional regulation at the DNA level, not post-transcriptional regulation of mRNA.

Question 17

Which of the following methods is not appropriate for causing protein precipitation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Moving pH away from isoelectric pH Protein precipitation is a process where proteins are made to come out of solution. A protein's solubility is at its minimum at its isoelectric point (pI). The pI is the specific pH at which the net electrical charge of the protein is zero. At this pH, the repulsive electrostatic forces between protein molecules are minimal, allowing them to aggregate and precipitate more easily. Therefore, moving the pH towards the pI is a method used to precipitate proteins. Moving the pH away from the isoelectric pH (C) will increase the net charge on the protein molecules, leading to increased electrostatic repulsion between them and thus increasing their solubility, which is the opposite of precipitation. The other methods listed are all common techniques for precipitating proteins : adding organic solvents like alcohol and acetone (A), using heavy metal ions like mercury or lead (B), and adding strong acids like trichloroacetic acid (D) all work by disrupting the protein's hydration shell or neutralizing its charge, leading to precipitation.

Question 18

Select the incorrect statement concerning suicide inhibition.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The inhibitor can bind with any site resulting in suicidal inhibition This statement is incorrect. Suicide inhibition is a highly specific process. A suicide inhibitor, also known as a mechanism-based inactivator, is an unreactive molecule that is structurally similar to the substrate of a specific enzyme. It binds to the enzyme's active site just like a normal substrate. The enzyme then proceeds with its normal catalytic mechanism, but in doing so, it converts the inhibitor into a highly reactive intermediate. This reactive intermediate then binds covalently and irreversibly to a residue within the active site, permanently inactivating the enzyme. Therefore, the inhibitor must bind specifically to the active site; it cannot bind to "any site" to cause this effect. The other statements are correct : the binding is ultimately irreversible (B), the high specificity makes it a valuable tool for rational drug design (C), and the enzyme's own action creates the reactive product that inhibits it (D). Question 19

Which factor is responsible for triggering an aplastic crisis in patients with hereditary spherocytosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Parvovirus Patients with chronic hemolytic anemias, such as hereditary spherocytosis or sickle cell disease, rely on a highly accelerated rate of red blood cell production (erythropoiesis) in their bone marrow to compensate for the shortened lifespan of their red cells. An aplastic crisis is a sudden, transient cessation of this erythropoiesis, leading to a rapid and severe drop in hemoglobin levels without the normal compensatory reticulocytosis. The most common infectious agent that triggers an aplastic crisis in these patients is Parvovirus B19. This virus has a specific tropism for and infects erythroid precursor cells (pronormoblasts) in the bone marrow, causing them to stop dividing. In a person with normal red cell survival, this temporary halt in production is clinically insignificant. However, in a patient with a chronic hemolytic anemia, it leads to a rapid and life-threatening anemia. Epstein-Barr virus (B) is associated with infectious mononucleosis.

Question 20

Which specific cell plays a role in type IV (delayed-type) hypersensitivity?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Cytotoxic T cell Type IV hypersensitivity, also known as delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH), is a T-cell mediated immune response that takes 24-72 hours to develop. The classic example is the tuberculin skin test. The reaction is initiated by antigen-specific Th1 helper T-cells. When these sensitized Th1 cells are re-exposed to an antigen presented by antigen-presenting cells, they become activated and release a profile of pro-inflammatory cytokines, most notably Interferon-gamma (IFN- γ ). The primary function of IFN- γ is to be a potent activator of macrophages. These activated macrophages are the principal effector cells of the DTH reaction. They become enlarged, highly phagocytic, and release lytic enzymes and other inflammatory mediators that are responsible for the tissue damage, induration, and inflammation characteristic of the DTH lesion. While cytotoxic T cells (A) are involved in a different subtype of Type IV hypersensitivity, the classic DTH reaction is mediated by cytokine release from Th1 cells that activates macrophages.

Question 21

Which of the following is not a product of heme catabolism?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Aminolevulinic acid Heme catabolism is the process of breaking down the heme component of hemoglobin from old red blood cells. The process begins in macrophages. The first step is catalyzed by the enzyme heme oxygenase, which opens the heme ring. This single reaction produces three products : 1. Biliverdin (C), a green pigment. 2. Carbon monoxide (CO) (D), which is exhaled. 3. Ferrous iron ($Fe^{2+}$) (A), which is released and recycled, transported in the blood by transferrin. Biliverdin is then subsequently reduced to bilirubin. In contrast, delta-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) (B) is not a product of heme breakdown; it is a precursor molecule in the synthesis of heme. It is formed in the first step of the heme synthesis pathway by the condensation of glycine and succinyl-CoA, a reaction catalyzed by ALA synthase.

Question 22

A male with hyperpigmentation, Tanner stage 5, hypertension, and signs of precocious puberty is evaluated. What is the most likely underlying defect?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

11-Beta hydroxylase deficiency This patient's presentation of precocious puberty (early virilization), hypertension, and hyperpigmentation points to a specific form of congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH). In 11-beta-hydroxylase deficiency, the final step in cortisol synthesis is blocked. This leads to low cortisol levels, which results in a loss of negative feedback to the pituitary and a subsequent overproduction of ACTH. The excess ACTH causes adrenal hyperplasia and the shunting of steroid precursors towards the androgen synthesis pathway, leading to high levels of androgens and causing precocious puberty. The ACTH precursor, POMC, also contains the MSH sequence, leading to hyperpigmentation. The enzymatic block also leads to the accumulation of the precursor 11-deoxycorticosterone (DOC), a potent mineralocorticoid. The excess DOC causes sodium and water retention, leading to hypertension and hypokalemia. In contrast, 21-hydroxylase deficiency (A) is associated with salt wasting and hypotension. 17-alpha-hydroxylase deficiency (D) impairs both cortisol and sex steroid synthesis, preventing puberty.

Question 23

Question 24

What condition can be avoided by administering irradiated red blood cells?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Graft-versus-host disease Irradiation of cellular blood products (red blood cells and platelets) is a specific procedure performed to prevent a rare but often fatal complication of transfusion known as Transfusion-Associated Graft-versus-Host Disease (TA-GVHD). This condition occurs when viable donor T-lymphocytes present in the transfused blood product engraft in an immuno compromised or immunologically similar recipient. These donor lymphocytes then recognize the recipient's tissues as foreign and mount an immune attack against them, leading to severe damage to the skin, liver, and gastrointestinal tract. Gamma irradiation or X-irradiation of the blood product damages the DNA of the donor lymphocytes, rendering them incapable of proliferating and thus preventing their ability to cause TA-GVHD. Irradiation does not prevent HLA alloimmunization (B), immuno modulation (C), or TRALI (D), which have different pathophysiological mechanisms.

Question 25

What distinctive feature would be seen on electron microscopy in a patient with suspected Langerhans cell histiocytosis who presents with skull lytic lesions?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Birbeck granules Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare disease characterized by the abnormal proliferation of Langerhans cells, a type of dendritic cell. Patients often present with lytic bone lesions, particularly in the skull. While light microscopy of a biopsy will show an infiltrate of Langerhans cells (histiocytes) mixed with eosinophils and other inflammatory cells, the definitive and pathognomonic finding is seen on electron microscopy. Langerhans cells in LCH contain unique, rod-shaped or "tennis racket"-shaped cytoplasmic organelles called Birbeck granules. The presence of these specific granules within the neoplastic cells is diagnostic of LCH. While histiocytes (A), eosinophils (C), and giant cells (D) are all seen on a standard H&E stained slide, the Birbeck granule is the specific ultrastructural marker that defines the cell type involved in this disease.

Question 26

Which immunoglobulin is mainly responsible for causing facial swelling, itching, and hypotension in a person who presents to the emergency department after a bee sting?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

IgE The patient is experiencing a systemic allergic reaction, or anaphylaxis, triggered by a bee sting. This is a classic example of a Type I (immediate) hypersensitivity reaction. This type of reaction is mediated by Immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies. In a previously sensitized individual, specific IgE antibodies against bee venom antigens are bound to the surface of mast cells and basophils. Upon re-exposure to the venom (the bee sting), the antigens cross-link these surface-bound IgE molecules. This cross-linking triggers the rapid degranulation of the mast cells, releasing a massive amount of pre-formed and newly synthesized inflammatory mediators, such as histamine and leukotrienes. These mediators are responsible for the clinical manifestations of anaphylaxis, including vasodilation (causing hypotension), increased vascular permeability (causing facial swelling or angioedema), and smooth muscle contraction.

Question 27

What is the underlying pathophysiological process in a 42-year-old woman who presents with cough, low-grade fever, and hemoptysis, and whose biopsy from a cavitary lesion in the right lung apex reveals caseous necrosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Type IV hypersensitivity reaction The clinical and pathological findings—a cavitary lesion in the lung apex with caseous necrosis on biopsy—are pathognomonic for post-primary (reactivation) tuberculosis. The tissue damage and granuloma formation seen in tuberculosis are not caused directly by the Mycobacterium tuberculosis bacillus itself, which has low intrinsic virulence. Instead, the pathology is the result of the host's own immune response to the infection. Specifically, it is a cell-mediated immune response, which is a classic example of a Type IV or delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH) reaction. Sensitized CD4+ Th1 lymphocytes recognize mycobacterial antigens and release cytokines, particularly interferon-gamma. This activates macrophages, transforming them into epithelioid histiocytes, which form the granulomas. The prolonged, intense DTH reaction leads to the characteristic cheesy, friable necrosis in the center of the granuloma, known as caseous necrosis, which is responsible for the tissue destruction and cavity formation.

Question 28

Which further diagnostic investigations should be pursued for a 65-year-old man who presents with persistent fatigue, lymphadenopathy, and smudge cells noted on a peripheral blood smear?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Flow cytometry The patient's presentation with lymphadenopathy, fatigue, and smudge cells on the peripheral smear is highly suggestive of Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL). While the blood smear is characteristic, the definitive diagnosis and confirmation are made by immunophenotyping of the circulating lymphocytes using flow cytometry. This technique uses fluorescently-labeled antibodies to identify a specific profile of cell surface markers (CD antigens) on the leukemic cells. The classic immunophenotype for CLL is the co-expression of the B-cell markers CD19 and CD20, along with the aberrant expression of the T-cell marker CD5. The cells also typically express CD23 and have dim surface immunoglobulin expression. This specific marker profile confirmed by flow cytometry is the gold standard for diagnosing CLL and distinguishing it from other B-cell lymphoproliferative disorders. FISH (B) and cytogenetics (D) are important for prognostication (e.g., detecting del17p) but not for the primary diagnosis.

Question 29

What genetic abnormality is most likely in a male patient who presents with fever, night sweats, and neck swelling, and whose biopsy reveals a starry-sky pattern?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

MYC The combination of constitutional "B" symptoms (fever, night sweats), a rapidly growing mass (neck swelling), and a biopsy with a "starry sky" appearance is classic for Burkitt lymphoma. Burkitt lymphoma is a highly aggressive B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. The "starry sky" histology is created by a diffuse infiltrate of uniform, medium-sized tumor cells (the "dark sky") interspersed with numerous benign tangible body macrophages (the "stars") that are phagocytosing apoptotic tumor cells, reflecting the tumor's extremely high proliferation rate. The defining genetic abnormality in virtually all cases of Burkitt lymphoma is a translocation involving the MYC proto-oncogene on chromosome 8. The most common translocation, t(8;14), places the MYC gene under the control of the strong immunoglobulin heavy chain gene enhancer, leading to its massive overexpression and driving uncontrolled cell proliferation.

Question 30

Which of the following symptoms is least likely to occur in a patient with Paroxysmal Nocturnal Hemoglobinuria (PNH)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Leukemia Paroxysmal Nocturnal Hemoglobinuria (PNH) is a rare, acquired clonal stem cell disorder caused by a somatic mutation in the PIGA gene. This leads to a deficiency of GPI-anchored proteins (like CD55 and CD59) on the surface of blood cells, making them susceptible to complement-mediated destruction. The clinical triad of PNH includes : Intravascular hemolysis (D), leading to anemia and hemoglobinuria. A very high risk of venous and arterial thrombosis (C), often in unusual sites like the hepatic or portal veins, which is a major cause of mortality. Variable degrees of bone marrow failure, which can manifest as or evolve into aplastic anemia (A). While PNH is a clonal disorder and carries a small long-term risk of transformation to myelodysplastic syndrome or acute myeloid leukemia, leukemia (B) is by far the least common manifestation of the disease compared to the cardinal features of hemolysis, thrombosis, and bone marrow failure.

Question 31

For which interpretation of HER2/neu status is Fluorescence In-Situ Hybridization (FISH) indicated?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

2+ The HER2/neu status of a breast cancer is a critical biomarker for determining eligibility for anti-HER2 targeted therapies like trastuzumab. The initial assessment is typically done by immunohistochemistry (IHC), which stains for the HER2 protein on the surface of tumor cells. The result is scored from 0 to 3+. A score of 0 or 1+ is considered HER2 negative. A score of 3+ (strong, complete membrane staining in >10% of tumor cells) is considered HER2 positive. A score of 2+ is considered equivocal or borderline. According to ASCO/CAP guidelines, when the IHC result is equivocal (2+), reflex testing with Fluorescence In-Situ Hybridization (FISH) is required to make a definitive determination. FISH is a molecular test that directly counts the number of copies of the HER2 gene within the tumor cell nuclei to assess for gene amplification. A positive FISH result will then classify the 2+ case as HER2 positive.

Question 32

In evaluating a patient for resistant hypertension, their blood pressure remains uncontrolled despite taking three antihypertensive drugs. Which specific class of antihypertensive medication must be included in this regimen for the diagnosis to apply?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Diuretic Resistant hypertension is defined as blood pressure that remains above the target goal despite the concurrent use of three antihypertensive agents from different classes, taken at optimal doses. A crucial part of this definition is that one of the three agents must be a diuretic. Many cases of difficult-to-control hypertension are driven by volume expansion, making diuretics a cornerstone of therapy. Therefore, a patient cannot be diagnosed with true resistant hypertension unless their regimen includes a diuretic (typically a thiazide-like diuretic like chlorthalidone) and they are still not at goal. If a patient is on three non-diuretic medications, the first step is to add a diuretic before labeling them as resistant. This ensures that a major contributing factor (volume overload) has been addressed before proceeding to more complex investigations or fourth-line agents.

Question 33

In a 35-year-old woman with class II pulmonary hypertension who shows a negative response on vasoreactivity testing, what is the next management step?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Ambrisentan The management of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is guided by vasoreactivity testing. A small subset of patients who show a positive response (a significant drop in pulmonary artery pressure) to inhaled nitric oxide during right heart catheterization are candidates for treatment with high-dose calcium channel blockers like nifedipine (C). However, this patient had a negative vasoreactive test, meaning she is not a candidate for calcium channel blocker therapy. For patients with WHO Functional Class II symptoms who are vasoreactivity-negative, the guidelines recommend initiating therapy with an approved PAH-specific agent. The first-line options for Class II patients typically include oral medications like an endothelin receptor antagonist (ERA), such as ambrisentan or bosentan, or a phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE-5) inhibitor, such as sildenafil. Prostacyclin analogues like iloprost (A) and epoprostenol (D) are generally reserved for more severe disease (Functional Class III or IV).

Question 34

Which medication is considered the treatment of choice for primary progressive multiple sclerosis (PPMS)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Ocrelizumab Primary progressive multiple sclerosis (PPMS) is a form of MS characterized by a steady worsening of neurological function from the onset, without distinct relapses or remissions. For many years, there were no approved treatments that could slow the progression of PPMS. This changed with the approval of ocrelizumab. Ocrelizumab is a monoclonal antibody that targets and depletes CD20-positive B-lymphocytes. In its pivotal clinical trial (ORATORIO), ocrelizumab was the first and, to date, the only disease-modifying therapy to demonstrate a significant reduction in the progression of clinical disability in patients with PPMS. The other medications listed—natalizumab (A), fingolimod (C), and alemtuzumab (D)—are highly effective treatments for relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS) but have not shown efficacy in treating PPMS. Therefore, ocrelizumab is the preferred and only approved choice for managing this specific form of the disease.

Question 35

Which of the following statements about the selection of essential drugs is not correct?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Fixed drug combination is preferred over single drugs The World Health Organization's concept of an essential medicines list is based on several key principles for selecting drugs that should be available in a health system at all times. The selection criteria include public health relevance, meaning the drug should address a condition with significant disease prevalence (D). The drug must have demonstrated efficacy and an adequate safety profile from clinical trials (A). The cost-to-benefit ratio (B) is also a major consideration to ensure affordability and accessibility. However, the statement that fixed-dose combinations (FDCs) are preferred over single drugs (C) is incorrect. The general principle is that most essential medicines should be formulated as single compounds. FDCs are only included if they have a proven advantage in terms of therapeutic effect, safety, adherence, or reducing drug resistance (e.g., in TB or HIV treatment). They are the exception, not the preferred rule.

Question 36

What adjustment should be made to a patient’s retigabine regimen when phenytoin is added to their epilepsy treatment?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Increase the dose This question involves a drug-drug interaction based on hepatic enzyme metabolism. Retigabine (also known as ezogabine) is an antiepileptic drug that is primarily metabolized in the liver, partly through glucuronidation by UGT enzymes. Phenytoin is a classic and potent inducer of hepatic microsomal enzymes, including various cytochrome P450 isoforms and UGT enzymes. When an enzyme-inducing drug like phenytoin is added to a patient's regimen, it will accelerate the metabolism of other drugs that are substrates for those enzymes. This leads to a faster clearance and lower plasma concentration of the affected drug. In this case, phenytoin will induce the metabolism of retigabine, leading to lower-than-expected blood levels and potentially a loss of seizure control. Therefore, to maintain a therapeutic effect, it is necessary to increase the dose of retigabine to compensate for its accelerated clearance.

Question 37

What might explain why clopidogrel failed to work in a patient who suffered a second stroke six months after starting the medication?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Downregulation of CYP2C19 Clopidogrel is an antiplatelet agent that functions as a prodrug, meaning it must be converted into its active metabolite to exert its therapeutic effect. This two-step bioactivation process occurs in the liver and is primarily mediated by the cytochrome P450 enzyme, CYP2C19. Some individuals possess genetic polymorphisms in the CYP2C19 gene that result in a reduced-function or non-functional enzyme. These individuals are known as "poor metabolizers." In such patients, the conversion of clopidogrel to its active form is significantly impaired, which can be described as a functional downregulation of CYP2C19 activity. This leads to lower plasma levels of the active metabolite, resulting in an inadequate antiplatelet response and a higher risk of major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, such as stroke or stent thrombosis, despite adherence to standard dosing. Upregulation of the enzyme (B) would lead to an enhanced effect, while the other enzymes listed are not the primary activators of clopidogrel.

Question 39

Nivolumab is used as a checkpoint inhibitor in which of the following conditions?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Hodgkin's lymphoma Nivolumab is an immune checkpoint inhibitor, a type of immunotherapy that works by blocking the programmed death-1 (PD-1) receptor on T-cells. This action prevents cancer cells from inactivating the T-cells, thereby unleashing the body's own immune system to attack the tumor. Nivolumab has been approved for the treatment of a variety of cancers. One of its key and highly effective indications is for classical Hodgkin's lymphoma that has relapsed or progressed after other treatments, such as chemotherapy and stem cell transplant. The malignant Reed-Sternberg cells in Hodgkin's lymphoma frequently exhibit genetic alterations that lead to the overexpression of the PD-1 ligands (PD-L1 and PD-L2), which makes this cancer particularly vulnerable to PD-1 blockade. Nivolumab is not a standard therapy for the other pediatric malignancies listed, such as retinoblastoma (A), medulloblastoma (B), or pleuropulmonary blastoma (C).

Question 40

Which of the following conditions does not involve the use of antimicrobial combinations?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Uncomplicated Gonorrhea The use of combination antimicrobial therapy is standard practice for several conditions to enhance efficacy and prevent resistance. Tuberculosis (B) is the classic example, where a multi-drug regimen (e.g., RIPE) is mandatory to prevent the emergence of resistant strains. Malaria (D), particularly P. falciparum, is treated with Artemisinin-based Combination Therapy (ACT). Intra-abdominal infections (C) are typically polymicrobial, involving both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria, thus requiring broad-spectrum combination therapy. For many years, uncomplicated gonorrhea was treated with dual therapy (ceftriaxone plus azithromycin). However, due to rising azithromycin resistance and to promote antimicrobial stewardship, recent guidelines (e.g., CDC 2021) have shifted. The current primary recommendation for uncomplicated urogenital gonorrhea is monotherapy with a single higher dose of ceftriaxone, provided that co-infection with Chlamydia has been excluded. This makes gonorrhea the condition among the choices where combination therapy is not always required.

Question 41

If the dose of a drug that follows first-order kinetics is doubled, what happens to its half-life and plasma concentration?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Half-life remains the same and plasma concentration doubles This question assesses a fundamental concept in pharmacokinetics. For a drug that follows first-order kinetics, a constant fraction of the drug is eliminated per unit of time. A key characteristic of this process is that the elimination half-life (t1/2) is constant and is independent of the drug's plasma concentration or the dose administered. Therefore, doubling the dose will have no effect on the half-life. The steady-state plasma concentration (Css), on the other hand, is directly proportional to the dosing rate. If the dose is doubled while the dosing interval remains the same, the dosing rate is effectively doubled. Consequently, the average steady-state plasma concentration will also double. Thus, the correct answer is that the half-life remains the same while the plasma concentration doubles. This linear relationship between dose and concentration is the defining feature of first-order kinetics.

Question 42

A patient on phenytoin for seizures is also prescribed sucralfate four times daily for peptic ulcers. To prevent drug interaction and allow proper absorption of both medications, what is the minimum time gap that should be maintained between administering phenytoin and sucralfate?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

120 minutes Sucralfate is a medication used to treat ulcers by forming a protective, viscous barrier over the ulcerated surface in the stomach and duodenum. This physical coating, while beneficial for ulcer healing, can also interfere with the absorption of other orally administered medications by binding to them or preventing them from coming into contact with the gastrointestinal mucosa. Phenytoin is an anticonvulsant with a narrow therapeutic index, and its absorption is known to be significantly reduced when taken concurrently with sucralfate, potentially leading to subtherapeutic levels and loss of seizure control. To prevent this pharmacokinetic interaction, it is a standard recommendation to separate the administration of the two drugs. Other medications should be taken at least 2 hours (120 minutes) before or after taking sucralfate. This time interval allows for the adequate absorption of the other drug before the GI tract is coated by the sucralfate.

Question 43

How does remdesivir work in terms of its mechanism of action?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

RNA-dependent RNA polymerase inhibition Remdesivir is a nucleotide analogue prodrug that is converted inside the cell into its active triphosphate form. This active metabolite mimics adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and competes for incorporation into viral RNA. RNA viruses such as SARS-CoV-2 replicate using the enzyme RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp). When remdesivir triphosphate is incorporated into the growing viral RNA strand by RdRp, it causes premature termination of RNA synthesis. This halts viral genome replication and reduces viral proliferation. Option (A) refers to RNA-dependent DNA polymerase (reverse transcriptase), which is used by retroviruses like HIV. Remdesivir does not act on reverse transcriptase. Options (C) and (D) involve blocking viral entry or fusion, which is not remdesivir’s mechanism. Therefore, remdesivir works by inhibiting RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, making option (B) correct.

Question 44

Which drug, when given concurrently, would increase the bioavailability of saquinavir?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Ritonavir This is a duplicate of a question from the November 2021 paper. Saquinavir is an HIV protease inhibitor that is extensively metabolized by the cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP3A4, resulting in poor oral bioavailability. Ritonavir, another protease inhibitor, is a potent inhibitor of CYP3A4. This property is utilized in HIV therapy by co-administering a low dose of ritonavir with other protease inhibitors like saquinavir. This practice is known as "pharmacokinetic boosting." The ritonavir effectively shuts down the metabolic pathway for saquinavir, leading to a substantial increase in its plasma concentration and duration of action, thereby enhancing its antiviral activity and allowing for lower or less frequent dosing. This boosting strategy is a fundamental concept in modern antiretroviral therapy. The other options do not have this potent and specific interaction.

Question 45

How does cyclosporine exert its mechanism of action?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Inhibition of calcineurin This is a duplicate of a question from the November 2021 paper. Cyclosporine is a potent calcineurin inhibitor used as an immunosuppressant to prevent organ transplant rejection and treat autoimmune diseases. Inside a T-cell, cyclosporine binds to its intracellular receptor, cyclophilin. The resulting cyclosporine-cyclophilin complex then binds to and inhibits calcineurin, a calcium-dependent phosphatase enzyme. The primary substrate of calcineurin is the Nuclear Factor of Activated T-cells (NFAT). By inhibiting calcineurin, cyclosporine prevents the dephosphorylation of NFAT. The phosphorylated NFAT cannot enter the nucleus, which in turn prevents the transcription of the gene for Interleukin-2 (IL-2). Since IL-2 is the main cytokine responsible for T-cell proliferation and activation, its suppression effectively halts the T-cell mediated immune response. This targeted action on T-cell activation is the basis of cyclosporine's powerful immunosuppressive effects.

Question 46

Which of the following options refers to a newly developed, innovative medication specifically intended for the treatment of depression?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Vilazodone Vilazodone is an antidepressant with a novel dual mechanism of action, classifying it as a Serotonin Partial Agonist/Reuptake Inhibitor (SPARI). It combines the mechanism of a conventional selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) with partial agonism at the 5-HT1A serotonin receptor. This innovative approach is intended to provide effective antidepressant action while potentially mitigating some of the side effects associated with traditional SSRIs, such as sexual dysfunction. In contrast, lurasidone (A), asenapine (C), and blonanserin (D) are all classified as atypical antipsychotics. While some antipsychotics are used as adjuncts in treating depression or for bipolar depression, vilazodone was specifically designed and is primarily used as a novel monotherapy for major depressive disorder.

Which drug listed in the options is the most effective for assisting with smoking cessation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Varenicline This is a duplicate of a question from the November 2021 paper. Among the available first-line pharmacological treatments for smoking cessation, varenicline is consistently shown in clinical trials to be the most effective. It acts as a partial agonist at the α 4 β 2 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor, the primary receptor mediating nicotine dependence. Question 49

Match the following findings observed during postmortem examination with their corresponding time of appearance. a. Adipocere formation 1. 24 hrs b. Relaxation of urethral sphincter 2. 2-3 days c. Marbling of veins 3. 4-5 days d. Liquefaction of tissues 4. 8-10 days

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

a-4, b-1, c-2, d-3 Adipocere formation corresponds to 8–10 days (4). Adipocere, also called “grave wax,” results from the hydrolysis and hydrogenation of body fats in moist environments. It is a relatively late postmortem change and typically becomes evident after about a week or more, especially under warm and humid conditions. Relaxation of the urethral sphincter corresponds to 24 hours (1). Shortly after death, muscles lose tone due to cessation of ATP production. This leads to sphincter relaxation and possible passage of urine and feces. It occurs early in the postmortem period. Marbling of veins corresponds to 2–3 days (2). Marbling is caused by hemolysis and bacterial production of hydrogen sulfide, which reacts with hemoglobin to form greenish discoloration along superficial veins. It is an early sign of putrefaction. Liquefaction of tissues corresponds to 4–6 days (3). As decomposition progresses, enzymatic and bacterial activity cause softening and liquefaction of internal organs. Thus, the correct matching is a-4, b-1, c-2, d-3.

Question 50

In the context of a forensic examination following an alleged sexual assault, Barberio's test is performed on stains found on the victim’s clothing. Which specific substance does this test identify?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Spermine In the forensic investigation of sexual assault, various tests are used to identify the presence of seminal fluid. Barberio's test is a specific microchemical or crystal test for the definitive identification of semen. The test relies on the detection of spermine, a polyamine that is present in very high concentrations in seminal fluid. The procedure involves adding a solution of picric acid to an extract of the suspected stain. If spermine is present, it will react with the picric acid to form characteristic, yellow, needle-shaped crystals of spermine picrate, which can be identified under a microscope. While other tests are used—the acid phosphatase test (B) is a presumptive screening test, and fructose (D) and choline (C) can also be tested for—the Barberio's test is specifically for the detection of spermine to confirm the presence of seminal fluid.
