Question 1

Pelvic diaphragm is formed by the following muscles:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

All of the above The pelvic diaphragm is a crucial funnel-shaped muscular structure that forms the floor of the pelvic cavity, supporting the pelvic organs. It is composed of the levator ani and coccygeus muscles. The levator ani muscle is not a single muscle but a complex sheet made up of several interconnected parts. The main components of the levator ani are the iliococcygeus and the pubococcygeus muscles. The pubococcygeus is further subdivided based on its fiber attachments. In females, the most medial fibers of the pubococcygeus that flank the vagina are specifically referred to as the pubovaginalis muscle. This part is critical for vaginal support and sphincter function. Since the pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus, and pubovaginalis are all integral components of the levator ani complex, and the levator ani is the main constituent of the pelvic diaphragm, all the listed options are correct.

Question 2

When the outer sheath and nerve fiber remain intact but the inner axons are damaged, this condition is known as what?:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Axonotmesis This question refers to Seddon's classification of peripheral nerve injuries, which has three main types. Neurapraxia (A) is the mildest form, involving a temporary block of nerve conduction, usually due to focal demyelination, without any axonal damage. The nerve sheaths and axon are intact, and recovery is complete. Neurotmesis (D) is the most severe form, where the entire nerve trunk, including the axons and all surrounding connective tissue sheaths (endoneurium, perineurium, and epineurium), is completely transected. Recovery does not occur without surgical repair. Axonotmesis is the intermediate injury, defined by the disruption of the inner axons and their myelin sheaths, but with the connective tissue framework of the nerve, including the endoneurial tube ("outer sheath"), remaining intact. This leads to Wallerian degeneration distal to the injury, but the intact sheath provides a scaffold for potential axonal regeneration, making recovery possible but slow.

Question 3

Which of the following does not enhance the production of gastric acid?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Serotonin The secretion of gastric acid from parietal cells in the stomach is stimulated by three main physiological agonists. Gastrin (A), a hormone released from G cells in the stomach antrum, stimulates acid secretion both directly and indirectly by promoting histamine release. Histamine (B), released from enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells, is a powerful paracrine stimulator of parietal cells via H2 receptors. Acetylcholine (C), released from vagal nerve endings, stimulates parietal cells directly via M3 muscarinic receptors. These three substances work synergistically to promote acid production. Serotonin (5-HT) (D), while abundant in the gastrointestinal tract where it primarily regulates motility and secretion in the intestines, is not a primary stimulant for gastric acid production. In fact, some subtypes of serotonin receptors have been shown to have an inhibitory effect on acid secretion. Therefore, among the options provided, serotonin is the one that does not enhance gastric acid production.

Question 4

Which of the following options demonstrates the highest airway resistance?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Bronchi Airway resistance is determined by the caliber and arrangement of the airways. While one might intuitively think that the smallest airways would have the highest resistance, this is incorrect. The total cross-sectional area of the airways is the key factor. Although an individual small bronchiole has a very high resistance, the airways branch extensively, and there are millions of small bronchioles (D) and respiratory bronchioles (C) arranged in parallel. This parallel arrangement results in a massive total cross-sectional area, leading to very low overall resistance in the peripheral airways. In contrast, the medium-sized bronchi (specifically, the segmental bronchi, from the 3rd to 7th generation of branching) represent a point where the total cross-sectional area is at its minimum. This bottleneck in the airway tree is the site of the greatest level of airway resistance in the entire respiratory system. The trachea and main bronchi have a larger diameter, resulting in lower resistance.

Question 5

In a situation where cardiac contractility decreases, which of the following changes is most likely to occur?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Shift of the end-systolic volume and pressure curves to the right Reduced cardiac contractility, or negative inotropy, means that for any given preload (end-diastolic volume), the heart muscle contracts less forcefully. This is depicted on a ventricular pressure-volume loop analysis. The end-systolic pressure-volume relationship (ESPVR) is a line that represents the maximum pressure the ventricle can generate at any given volume, and its slope is a measure of contractility. When contractility is reduced, the ventricle is weaker, so for any given systolic pressure, the end-systolic volume will be larger. This causes the ESPVR curve to become less steep and shift downwards and to the right. Consequently, for a given preload and afterload, the stroke volume (C) will decrease, not increase, and the end-systolic volume will be larger. The end-diastolic volume (A) might increase as a compensatory mechanism, but it does not decrease.

Question 6

Which of the following ions does not show a reduced concentration in the lumen of the proximal convoluted tubule?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Chloride The proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) is responsible for reabsorbing the majority (about 65-70%) of the glomerular filtrate. This includes the near-complete reabsorption of valuable organic solutes like glucose (B) and amino acids (A) via sodium-coupled secondary active transport. A large amount of bicarbonate (D) is also reabsorbed in the PCT, a process linked to sodium-hydrogen exchange and carbonic anhydrase activity. As these solutes and a significant amount of sodium are reabsorbed, their concentration in the tubular fluid decreases progressively along the PCT. Water follows these solutes osmotically. However, chloride (C) is reabsorbed more slowly in the early PCT compared to sodium and water. As water is reabsorbed, the concentration of the less readily reabsorbed chloride ions in the remaining tubular fluid actually increases along the length of the PCT. This creates a favorable concentration gradient for its passive paracellular reabsorption in the later parts of the PCT.

Question 8

Methyl-tetrahydro-folic acid gets accumulated in deficiency of which of the following?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

B12 This question refers to the "folate trap" hypothesis, which explains the biochemical link between Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. The enzyme methionine synthase is responsible for converting homocysteine to methionine. This reaction requires two things : Vitamin B12 (in the form of methylcobalamin) as a cofactor, and N5-methyl-tetrahydrofolate (methyl-THF) as a methyl group donor. In the process, methyl-THF is converted back to tetrahydrofolate (THF), which can then be used to synthesize other folate derivatives needed for DNA synthesis. In Vitamin B12 deficiency, the methionine synthase enzyme cannot function properly. As a result, folate becomes "trapped" in its N5-methyl-THF form, as this is an irreversible reaction. This accumulation of methyl-THF prevents the regeneration of THF, leading to a functional folate deficiency and impairing DNA synthesis, which results in megaloblastic anemia.

Question 9

Which of the following statements is not correct about a patient with advanced liver disease?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Oxidative decarboxylation of alpha-ketoglutarate In advanced liver disease, the liver's capacity to detoxify ammonia via the urea cycle is severely impaired, leading to hyperammonemia (A). This excess ammonia is shunted to other tissues, particularly the brain and skeletal muscle. In the brain's astrocytes, ammonia is detoxified by combining with glutamate to form glutamine, a reaction catalyzed by glutamine synthetase. To replenish the glutamate used in this reaction, alpha-ketoglutarate (an intermediate of the Krebs cycle) is reductively aminated to glutamate. This leads to an increased synthesis of glutamine from alpha-ketoglutarate (D). This depletion of alpha-ketoglutarate from the Krebs cycle impairs aerobic respiration and leads to decreased ATP synthesis (B) in astrocytes, contributing to hepatic encephalopathy. The statement that oxidative decarboxylation of alpha-ketoglutarate (C) occurs is incorrect in this context; rather, it is the depletion of alpha-ketoglutarate via reductive amination that is the key pathological step.

Question 10

Which of the following options is not a substrate used in the synthesis of glucose?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Acetyl coenzyme A Gluconeogenesis is the metabolic pathway that synthesizes glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors. The major substrates for gluconeogenesis are lactate (D) (from anaerobic glycolysis, via the Cori cycle), glucogenic amino acids like alanine (C) (from muscle protein breakdown, via the glucose-alanine cycle), and glycerol (B) (from the breakdown of triglycerides in adipose tissue). These substrates can all be converted into pyruvate or other intermediates of the Krebs cycle, which can then be used to form glucose. However, acetyl-CoA (A), which is derived from the beta-oxidation of fatty acids, cannot be used for the net synthesis of glucose in humans. This is because the conversion of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex is an irreversible reaction. There is no pathway in humans to convert acetyl-CoA back to pyruvate or oxaloacetate for entry into gluconeogenesis.

Question 12

What repair mechanism is linked to CRISPR- Cas9?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Non-homologous end joining The CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing system functions by creating a targeted double-strand break (DSB) in the DNA. Once this break is made, the cell utilizes its own endogenous DNA repair pathways to fix it. The cell has two major pathways for repairing DSBs. The predominant, default, and most frequently used pathway is non-homologous end joining (NHEJ). This pathway is efficient but error-prone; it often introduces small, random insertions or deletions (indels) at the site of the break as it ligates the two ends back together. This is the mechanism that researchers exploit to create gene knockouts, as the resulting indel often causes a frameshift mutation that inactivates the gene. The other repair pathways listed—base excision repair (B), nucleotide excision repair (C), and mismatch repair (D)—are responsible for fixing different types of DNA damage, such as single-base errors or bulky adducts, not double-strand breaks.

Question 13

Which of the following substances is not characterized by amphipathicity?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Triglycerides An amphipathic (or amphiphilic) molecule is one that possesses both a hydrophilic ("water-loving") polar head and a hydrophobic ("water-fearing") nonpolar tail. This dual nature allows these molecules to form structures like micelles and lipid bilayers in aqueous environments. Phosphoglycerols (B) (a major class of phospholipids), sphingolipids (A), and glycolipids (D) are all major components of cell membranes and are all amphipathic. They each have a polar head group (containing phosphate, choline, or a carbohydrate moiety) attached to nonpolar fatty acid tails. In contrast, triglycerides (also called triacylglycerols) are the main form of stored fat. They are composed of a glycerol backbone esterified to three fatty acid chains. This structure is almost entirely nonpolar and hydrophobic. Since they lack a distinct polar head group, triglycerides are not amphipathic and are stored as oily droplets within cells.

Question 14

Which process explains how organophosphorus compounds inhibit the acetylcholinesterase enzyme?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Suicidal inhibition Organophosphorus compounds inhibit acetylcholinesterase (AChE) by forming a covalent bond with the enzyme at its active site. Normally, acetylcholinesterase breaks down acetylcholine into acetate and choline, thereby terminating synaptic transmission. However, organophosphates phosphorylate the serine hydroxyl group present in the active site of the enzyme. This phosphorylation initially forms a stable enzyme–inhibitor complex. Over time, a process called “aging” occurs, in which the bond becomes even more stable and resistant to hydrolysis. As a result, the enzyme becomes permanently inactivated and cannot regenerate under normal physiological conditions. This mechanism is referred to as suicidal inhibition (mechanism-based inhibition) because the inhibitor binds to the active site and is processed as if it were a substrate, but instead permanently inactivates the enzyme. It differs from reversible competitive inhibition because the bond formed is covalent and essentially irreversible. The accumulation of acetylcholine at synapses leads to continuous stimulation of muscarinic and nicotinic receptors, producing the characteristic cholinergic toxicity seen in organophosphate poisoning.

Question 15

In the formula for urea clearance $(U\times V)/ P$ what does U represent?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Urine urea in mg/100 ml The formula presented is the standard formula for calculating the renal clearance of any substance : Clearance = (U × V) / P. In this formula, each component represents a specific measurement, and it is crucial that the units are consistent. V represents the urine flow rate (urine volume per unit time), typically expressed in mL/min. P represents the plasma concentration of the substance, usually expressed in mg/dL or mg/100 mL. U represents the urine concentration of the substance. To ensure the units cancel out correctly to give a final clearance value in mL/min, the urine concentration (U) must be expressed in the same units as the plasma concentration (P). Therefore, U represents the concentration of urea in the urine, expressed in mg/100 mL (or mg/dL). Expressing it in different units like g/24h (C) would require a conversion before it could be used in this formula.

Question 16

To inhibit translation, which region of the mRNA does miRNA bind to?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

$3^{\prime}$ UTR MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small, non-coding RNA molecules that play a crucial role in the post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression. They function by binding to complementary sequences on target messenger RNA (mRNA) molecules. This binding is mediated by a protein complex called the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). In animals, the miRNA binding sites are most commonly located in the 3' untranslated region (3' UTR) of the target mRNA. The binding of the miRNA-RISC complex to the 3' UTR typically does not involve perfect complementarity. This imperfect pairing leads to the inhibition of the translation of the mRNA into protein and often promotes the deadenylation and eventual degradation of the mRNA molecule. Binding to the gene promoter (B) or gene body (A) relates to transcriptional regulation at the DNA level, not post-transcriptional regulation of mRNA.

Question 17

Which of the following methods is not appropriate for causing protein precipitation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Moving pH away from isoelectric pH Protein precipitation is a process where proteins are made to come out of solution. A protein's solubility is at its minimum at its isoelectric point (pI). The pI is the specific pH at which the net electrical charge of the protein is zero. At this pH, the repulsive electrostatic forces between protein molecules are minimal, allowing them to aggregate and precipitate more easily. Therefore, moving the pH towards the pI is a method used to precipitate proteins. Moving the pH away from the isoelectric pH (C) will increase the net charge on the protein molecules, leading to increased electrostatic repulsion between them and thus increasing their solubility, which is the opposite of precipitation. The other methods listed are all common techniques for precipitating proteins : adding organic solvents like alcohol and acetone (A), using heavy metal ions like mercury or lead (B), and adding strong acids like trichloroacetic acid (D) all work by disrupting the protein's hydration shell or neutralizing its charge, leading to precipitation.

Question 18

Select the incorrect statement concerning suicide inhibition.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The inhibitor can bind with any site resulting in suicidal inhibition This statement is incorrect. Suicide inhibition is a highly specific process. A suicide inhibitor, also known as a mechanism-based inactivator, is an unreactive molecule that is structurally similar to the substrate of a specific enzyme. It binds to the enzyme's active site just like a normal substrate. The enzyme then proceeds with its normal catalytic mechanism, but in doing so, it converts the inhibitor into a highly reactive intermediate. This reactive intermediate then binds covalently and irreversibly to a residue within the active site, permanently inactivating the enzyme. Therefore, the inhibitor must bind specifically to the active site; it cannot bind to "any site" to cause this effect. The other statements are correct : the binding is ultimately irreversible (B), the high specificity makes it a valuable tool for rational drug design (C), and the enzyme's own action creates the reactive product that inhibits it (D). Parvovirus Patients with chronic hemolytic anemias, such as hereditary spherocytosis or sickle cell disease, rely on a highly accelerated rate of red blood cell production (erythropoiesis) in their bone marrow to compensate for the shortened lifespan of their red cells. Question 20

Which specific cell plays a role in type IV (delayed-type) hypersensitivity?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Cytotoxic T cell Type IV hypersensitivity, also known as delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH), is a T-cell mediated immune response that takes 24-72 hours to develop. The classic example is the tuberculin skin test. The reaction is initiated by antigen-specific Th1 helper T-cells. When these sensitized Th1 cells are re-exposed to an antigen presented by antigen-presenting cells, they become activated and release a profile of pro-inflammatory cytokines, most notably Interferon-gamma (IFN- γ ). The primary function of IFN- γ is to be a potent activator of macrophages. These activated macrophages are the principal effector cells of the DTH reaction. They become enlarged, highly phagocytic, and release lytic enzymes and other inflammatory mediators that are responsible for the tissue damage, induration, and inflammation characteristic of the DTH lesion. While cytotoxic T cells (A) are involved in a different subtype of Type IV hypersensitivity, the classic DTH reaction is mediated by cytokine release from Th1 cells that activates macrophages.

Question 24

What condition can be avoided by administering irradiated red blood cells?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Graft-versus-host disease Irradiation of cellular blood products (red blood cells and platelets) is a specific procedure performed to prevent a rare but often fatal complication of transfusion known as Transfusion-Associated Graft-versus-Host Disease (TA-GVHD). This condition occurs when viable donor T-lymphocytes present in the transfused blood product engraft in an immuno compromised or immunologically similar recipient. These donor lymphocytes then recognize the recipient's tissues as foreign and mount an immune attack against them, leading to severe damage to the skin, liver, and gastrointestinal tract. Gamma irradiation or X-irradiation of the blood product damages the DNA of the donor lymphocytes, rendering them incapable of proliferating and thus preventing their ability to cause TA-GVHD. Irradiation does not prevent HLA alloimmunization (B), immuno modulation (C), or TRALI (D), which have different pathophysiological mechanisms.

Question 25

What distinctive feature would be seen on electron microscopy in a patient with suspected Langerhans cell histiocytosis who presents with skull lytic lesions?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Birbeck granules Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare disease characterized by the abnormal proliferation of Langerhans cells, a type of dendritic cell. Patients often present with lytic bone lesions, particularly in the skull. While light microscopy of a biopsy will show an infiltrate of Langerhans cells (histiocytes) mixed with eosinophils and other inflammatory cells, the definitive and pathognomonic finding is seen on electron microscopy. Question 26

Which immunoglobulin is mainly responsible for causing facial swelling, itching, and hypotension in a person who presents to the emergency department after a bee sting?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

IgE The patient is experiencing a systemic allergic reaction, or anaphylaxis, triggered by a bee sting. This is a classic example of a Type I (immediate) hypersensitivity reaction. This type of reaction is mediated by Immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies. In a previously sensitized individual, specific IgE antibodies against bee venom antigens are bound to the surface of mast cells and basophils. Upon re-exposure to the venom (the bee sting), the antigens cross-link these surface-bound IgE molecules. This cross-linking triggers the rapid degranulation of the mast cells, releasing a massive amount of pre-formed and newly synthesized inflammatory mediators, such as histamine and leukotrienes. These mediators are responsible for the clinical manifestations of anaphylaxis, including vasodilation (causing hypotension), increased vascular permeability (causing facial swelling or angioedema), and smooth muscle contraction.

Question 27

What is the underlying pathophysiological process in a 42-year-old woman who presents with cough, low-grade fever, and hemoptysis, and whose biopsy from a cavitary lesion in the right lung apex reveals caseous necrosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Type IV hypersensitivity reaction The clinical and pathological findings—a cavitary lesion in the lung apex with caseous necrosis on biopsy—are pathognomonic for post-primary (reactivation) tuberculosis. The tissue damage and granuloma formation seen in tuberculosis are not caused directly by the Mycobacterium tuberculosis bacillus itself, which has low intrinsic virulence. Instead, the pathology is the result of the host's own immune response to the infection. Specifically, it is a cell-mediated immune response, which is a classic example of a Type IV or delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH) reaction. Sensitized CD4+ Th1 lymphocytes recognize mycobacterial antigens and release cytokines, particularly interferon-gamma. This activates macrophages, transforming them into epithelioid histiocytes, which form the granulomas. The prolonged, intense DTH reaction leads to the characteristic cheesy, friable necrosis in the center of the granuloma, known as caseous necrosis, which is responsible for the tissue destruction and cavity formation.

Question 28

Flow cytometry The patient's presentation with lymphadenopathy, fatigue, and smudge cells on the peripheral smear is highly suggestive of Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL). Question 29

What genetic abnormality is most likely in a male patient who presents with fever, night sweats, and neck swelling, and whose biopsy reveals a starry-sky pattern?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

MYC The combination of constitutional "B" symptoms (fever, night sweats), a rapidly growing mass (neck swelling), and a biopsy with a "starry sky" appearance is classic for Burkitt lymphoma. Burkitt lymphoma is a highly aggressive B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. The "starry sky" histology is created by a diffuse infiltrate of uniform, medium-sized tumor cells (the "dark sky") interspersed with numerous benign tangible body macrophages (the "stars") that are phagocytosing apoptotic tumor cells, reflecting the tumor's extremely high proliferation rate. The defining genetic abnormality in virtually all cases of Burkitt lymphoma is a translocation involving the MYC proto-oncogene on chromosome 8. The most common translocation, t(8;14), places the MYC gene under the control of the strong immunoglobulin heavy chain gene enhancer, leading to its massive overexpression and driving uncontrolled cell proliferation.

Question 31

For which interpretation of HER2/neu status is Fluorescence In-Situ Hybridization (FISH) indicated?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

2+ The HER2/neu status of a breast cancer is a critical biomarker for determining eligibility for anti-HER2 targeted therapies like trastuzumab. The initial assessment is typically done by immunohistochemistry (IHC), which stains for the HER2 protein on the surface of tumor cells. The result is scored from 0 to 3+. A score of 0 or 1+ is considered HER2 negative. A score of 3+ (strong, complete membrane staining in >10% of tumor cells) is considered HER2 positive. A score of 2+ is considered equivocal or borderline. According to ASCO/CAP guidelines, when the IHC result is equivocal (2+), reflex testing with Fluorescence In-Situ Hybridization (FISH) is required to make a definitive determination. FISH is a molecular test that directly counts the number of copies of the HER2 gene within the tumor cell nuclei to assess for gene amplification. A positive FISH result will then classify the 2+ case as HER2 positive.

Question 32

In evaluating a patient for resistant hypertension, their blood pressure remains uncontrolled despite taking three antihypertensive drugs. Which specific class of antihypertensive medication must be included in this regimen for the diagnosis to apply?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Diuretic Resistant hypertension is defined as blood pressure that remains above the target goal despite the concurrent use of three antihypertensive agents from different classes, taken at optimal doses. Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Half-life remains the same and plasma concentration doubles This question assesses a fundamental concept in pharmacokinetics. For a drug that follows first-order kinetics, a constant fraction of the drug is eliminated per unit of time. A key characteristic of this process is that the elimination half-life (t1/2) is constant and is independent of the drug's plasma concentration or the dose administered. If the dose is doubled while the dosing interval remains the same, the dosing rate is effectively doubled. Consequently, the average steady-state plasma concentration will also double. Thus, the correct answer is that the half-life remains the same while the plasma concentration doubles. This linear relationship between dose and concentration is the defining feature of first-order kinetics.

Question 132

To which biochemical moiety is beta hCG structurally similar?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

LH Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) is a glycoprotein hormone composed of two subunits, an alpha ( α ) subunit and a beta ( β ) subunit. The alpha subunit of hCG is structurally identical to the alpha subunits of three other pituitary glycoprotein hormones : luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). The beta subunit is unique to each hormone and confers its biological specificity. However, the beta subunit of hCG shares significant structural homology (about 80% similarity) with the beta subunit of LH. Because of this marked similarity, hCG can bind to and activate the LH receptor. This is the physiological basis for hCG's primary function in early pregnancy, which is to "rescue" and maintain the corpus luteum to continue progesterone production until the placenta takes over this role.

Question 133

What hinders the production of milk during pregnancy?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

High estrogen During pregnancy, the breast tissue undergoes significant development and proliferation (mammogenesis) under the influence of several hormones, including estrogen, progesterone, prolactin, and human placental lactogen (hPL). The pituitary gland secretes high levels of prolactin, the primary hormone responsible for stimulating milk synthesis (lactogenesis). However, despite these high prolactin levels, active milk secretion is held in check throughout pregnancy. This inhibition is primarily caused by the very high circulating levels of estrogen and progesterone, which are produced by the placenta. These steroid hormones act on the breast tissue to block the binding of prolactin to its receptors on the alveolar cells, thereby hindering its lactogenic effect. After delivery, the abrupt withdrawal of estrogen and progesterone following the expulsion of the placenta removes this inhibition, allowing prolactin to act unopposed and initiate copious milk production.

Question 140

How do local anesthetics exert their mechanism of action?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Block sodium channels Local anesthetics are drugs that produce a transient and reversible loss of sensation, particularly pain, in a localized area of the body. Their primary mechanism of action is the blockade of voltage-gated sodium channels in the nerve cell membrane. A nerve impulse, or action potential, is generated and propagated by the rapid influx of sodium ions through these channels, which causes depolarization of the nerve membrane. Local anesthetics bind to a specific receptor site within the pore of the voltage-gated sodium channel. This binding physically obstructs the channel, preventing the influx of sodium ions. By blocking this crucial step, local anesthetics prevent the nerve from reaching the threshold for depolarization, thereby blocking the generation and conduction of action potentials. This interruption of nerve impulse transmission is what produces the anesthetic effect. RADIOLOGY

Question 144

What is the best treatment for a patient who repeatedly feels their hands are contaminated and compulsively washes them, causing significant distress and reduced functioning?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Exposure and Response Prevention The patient's symptoms—recurrent, intrusive thoughts about contamination (obsessions) and repetitive hand-washing rituals (compulsions)—are the classic presentation of Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD).
