Question 1

Which nerve is not related to olfaction?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Hypoglossal Olfaction (the sense of smell) is a complex sensation. While the olfactory nerve (CN I) is the primary nerve for detecting odorants, other nerves contribute to the overall perception. The trigeminal nerve (CN V) (B) is related because it provides general sensation from the nasal mucosa, detecting chemical irritants or qualities like the "cooling" of menthol or the "burn" of ammonia (chemosensation). The glossopharyngeal (CN IX) (C) and vagus (CN X) (D) nerves are related because they mediate taste from the posterior tongue and pharynx. The perception of "flavor" is a combined sensory experience that intimately links retronasal olfaction with taste. In contrast, the hypoglossal nerve (CN XII) (A) is a purely motor nerve. It supplies the intrinsic and extrinsic muscles of the tongue (except palatoglossus), controlling tongue movement. It has no sensory function related to smell, taste, or flavor perception.

Question 2

What is the nerve supply to the piriform fossa?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Internal laryngeal nerve The piriform fossa (or recess) is a depression in the laryngopharynx located on either side of the laryngeal inlet. The sensory innervation of the larynx is divided by the vocal cords. The mucosa above the vocal cords, which includes the piriform fossa, is supplied by the internal laryngeal nerve (A). This nerve is a branch of the superior laryngeal nerve, which arises from the vagus (CN X). This innervation is critical as it provides the sensory (afferent) limb of the cough reflex for this area. (B) The recurrent laryngeal nerve provides sensory innervation to the mucosa below the vocal cords. (C) The external laryngeal nerve is a motor nerve that supplies the cricothyroid muscle. (D) The glossopharyngeal nerve supplies sensation to the oropharynx (e.g., tonsils, posterior tongue), which is superior to the piriform fossa.

Question 3

A 35-year-old man comes to the emergency department complaining that food is stuck in his throat. Examination reveals a bone lodged in the left piriform recess. Which function is most likely to be affected?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Cough reflex This clinical scenario is a direct application of the anatomy from the previous question. The piriform recess is innervated by the internal laryngeal nerve. This nerve provides the sensory afferent limb for the cough reflex (A) for any foreign material at or above the laryngeal inlet. A foreign body, such as a fishbone, lodged in this recess can mechanically or by local inflammation/edema, "anesthetize" the nerve endings. This impairs the protective reflex, which normally detects the foreign body and triggers a cough to expel it. This impairment significantly increases the risk of aspiration. The gag reflex (B) is mediated by the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) in the oropharynx, a different location. Mastication (C) (CN V) and salivation (D) (CN VII, CN IX) are unrelated to this specific location.

Question 4

Which of the following is not a boundary of the hepatocystic triangle?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Cystic artery The hepatocystic triangle, also known as the Triangle of Calot, is a critical anatomical space for surgeons, especially during a cholecystectomy (gallbladder removal). Its boundaries are: 1. Medially: The common hepatic duct (A) 2. Laterally: The cystic duct (B) 3. Superiorly: The inferior (visceral) surface of the liver (C) The cystic artery (D) is a vital structure, but it is a content of the triangle, not a boundary. The triangle is significant because it typically contains the cystic artery, the cystic lymph node (of Lund), and autonomic nerves. The surgeon must carefully identify all three boundaries of the triangle to safely locate and ligate the cystic duct and cystic artery within it, avoiding damage to the common hepatic duct.

Question 5

Which muscles are supplied by the superficial peroneal nerve?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Peroneus longus & Brevis The common peroneal (fibular) nerve divides into two main branches: the superficial and deep peroneal nerves. 1. The superficial peroneal nerve (also called the superficial fibular nerve) supplies the muscles of the lateral compartment of the leg. These muscles are the Peroneus longus and Peroneus brevis (A). Their primary function is eversion of the foot, and they also assist in plantarflexion. 2. The deep peroneal nerve (deep fibular nerve) supplies the muscles of the anterior compartment of the leg. These muscles are responsible for dorsiflexion of the foot and extension of the toes. They include the tibialis anterior, Extensor hallucis longus (B), extensor digitorum longus, and Peroneus tertius (C). Therefore, only the peroneus longus and brevis are supplied by the superficial peroneal nerve.

Question 6

A teenager comes to the emergency department with wrist pain after falling off his skateboard and has tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox. Which bone is most likely fractured?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Scaphoid This is a classic clinical presentation. A fall on an outstretched hand (FOOSH) is the most common mechanism for a scaphoid fracture (A). The anatomical snuffbox is a triangular depression on the dorsal, radial side of the wrist. Its floor is formed by the scaphoid bone, and tenderness upon palpation in this specific area ("snuff-box tenderness") is the hallmark clinical sign of a scaphoid fracture. The scaphoid is the most commonly fractured carpal bone. It is critical to diagnose and treat properly because it has a tenuous, retrograde blood supply, which puts it at high risk for avascular necrosis and non-union (failure to heal) if the fracture is missed. The trapezium (D) also lies in the snuffbox floor but is fractured far less often. The trapezoid (B) and capitate (C) are not associated with snuffbox tenderness.

Question 7

Primary muscle repair in cleft lip surgery involves reconstruction of which muscle?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Orbicularis oris A cleft lip is a congenital defect resulting from the failure of fusion of the facial prominences during embryonic development. This defect creates a gap in the upper lip. The key muscle that is disrupted is the Orbicularis oris (A). This is the circular muscle that surrounds the mouth and acts as the oral sphincter, responsible for closing and puckering the lips. In a cleft lip, the muscle fibers do not form a continuous ring; instead, they are interrupted and run abnormally, inserting onto the margins of the cleft. A primary goal of surgical cleft lip repair (cheiloplasty) is to dissect these abnormal muscle fibers, re-orient them into their correct horizontal position, and suture them together. This "primary muscle repair" is essential to restore normal lip function (like suckling), symmetry, and cosmetic appearance. The orbicularis oculi (B) , levator palpebrae superioris (C) , and masseter (D) are muscles of the eye and jaw, respectively, and are not involved.

Question 9

Which of the following is seen in high altitude?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Respiratory alkalosis An high altitude, the atmospheric pressure is lower, leading to a decreased partial pressure of inspired oxygen (PO 2 ). This causes hypoxemia (low oxygen in the blood), which is sensed by the peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid bodies). These chemoreceptors stimulate the respiratory center in the brainstem to increase the rate and depth of breathing (hyperventilation). This is a compensatory mechanism to increase oxygen intake. However, this hyperventilation also causes an excessive "blowing off" of carbon dioxide (CO 2 ). Since CO 2 is a volatile acid in the blood (forming carbonic acid), its excessive loss leads to a decrease in arterial PCO 2 . A primary decrease in PCO 2 raises the blood pH, resulting in respiratory alkalosis (D). This is the primary, acute acid-base disturbance seen at high altitude.

Question 10

IGF-1 and IGF-2 are structurally most similar to which molecule?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Insulin Insulin-like growth factors IGF-1 and IGF-2 are structurally most similar to insulin. They are single-chain polypeptides with significant amino acid sequence homology to proinsulin, but in their active circulating form, they closely resemble insulin in overall three-dimensional structure. Both IGFs contain A and B domains analogous to insulin’s A and B chains, along with additional C and D domains. Although they are synthesized as larger precursor molecules, their functional structure shows strong similarity to insulin, including conserved disulfide bonds that stabilize the molecule. Functionally, IGFs also share similarities with insulin. While insulin primarily regulates glucose metabolism, IGF-1 and IGF-2 mainly promote growth, cell proliferation, and differentiation. However, because of their structural resemblance, IGFs can weakly bind to insulin receptors at high concentrations. They are not most similar to preproinsulin, proinsulin, or C-peptide. Preproinsulin and proinsulin are precursor forms in insulin synthesis, and C-peptide is simply a connecting peptide removed during insulin maturation. Therefore, the best answer is Insulin.

Question 11

During which situation does the surface tension of the fluid lining the alveoli increase?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Inspiration The fluid lining the alveoli is coated with pulmonary surfactant, a substance that reduces surface tension. This reduction is not static; it changes with the breathing cycle. During expiration (A), the alveoli decrease in size. This compresses the surfactant molecules on the surface, increasing their concentration and dramatically decreasing surface tension, which prevents the alveoli from collapsing. During inspiration (B), the alveoli expand, and their surface area increases. This spreads the surfactant molecules farther apart, decreasing their concentration at the air-liquid interface. With this "dilution" of surfactant, its tension-reducing effect is lessened, and the surface tension of the fluid lining increases (or, more accurately, returns towards its higher baseline). Posture (C, D) affects ventilation-perfusion matching but not the direct physics of alveolar surface tension in this manner.

Question 12

Which of the following are features of Bezold Jarisch reflex? 1. Hypopnea 2. Tachycardia 3. Bradycardia 4. Hypotension

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

All of the above The Bezold-Jarisch reflex is a cardio-inhibitory reflex, meaning it slows down and unloads the heart. It is a protective reflex originating from chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors in the ventricular walls, often triggered by noxious stimuli, certain drugs, or situations like an "empty" ventricle in severe hypovolemia. The reflex arc involves vagal afferents and efferents. The classic triad of responses is: 1. Hypotension (4), caused by peripheral vasodilation (inhibition of sympathetic tone). 2. Bradycardia (3), caused by a surge in parasympathetic (vagal) output to the heart. 3. Apnea or Hypopnea (1) (a decrease in respiratory rate or depth). Tachycardia (2) (a fast heart rate) is the opposite of this reflex. Therefore, the correct features are 1, 3, and 4. This corresponds to option (B).

Question 13

In a normal awake person resting with eyes closed, which EEG waves diminish when the eyes are opened?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Alpha waves Alpha waves (C) (8-13 Hz) are the dominant EEG rhythm in a normal adult who is awake and at rest with their eyes closed. This state is often called "eyes-closed-relaxed." These waves are most prominent in the occipital (visual) cortex. The most characteristic feature of this alpha rhythm is its immediate attenuation (reduction or blocking) as soon as the person opens their eyes or engages in a mental task. This "alpha block" signifies the brain shifting from a relaxed, idling state to an active, processing state, which is characterized by higher-frequency, lower-amplitude Beta waves (A). Theta waves (B) and Delta waves (D) are associated with drowsiness and deep sleep, respectively.

Question 14

Given a latent period of 10 ms, a contraction period of 40 ms, and a relaxation period of 40 ms, how would you calculate the tetanizing frequency?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

25 Hz Tetanizing frequency is the minimum stimulation frequency required to cause individual muscle twitches to fuse into a smooth, sustained contraction (tetanus). This fusion occurs when a new stimulus arrives before the muscle has finished relaxing from the previous one. To achieve complete, fused tetanus, the next stimulus must arrive before the relaxation phase can even begin. This means the time interval (T) between stimuli must be equal to or less than the contraction period. Given Contraction Period (T) = 40 ms To convert milliseconds (ms) to seconds (s): T = 40 / 1000 = 0.04 seconds Frequency (f) is the inverse of the time period (f = 1 / T). f = 1 / 0.04 s = 25 Hz Therefore, a stimulation frequency of 25 Hz (or higher) will send a new impulse every 40 ms, initiating a new contraction just as the previous one peaks, leading to fused tetanus. The latent period and relaxation period are not needed for this specific calculation.

Question 15

Which of the following is an example of feed forward mechanism?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

HR increases from supine to standing A feed-forward mechanism is an anticipatory control system. It initiates a response before a change (or "error") in the system is actually detected, in anticipation of that change. (A) HR increases from supine to standing: This is the best example. As a person initiates the action of standing, the central nervous system sends signals (central command) that increase heart rate (HR) before the blood pressure has a chance to drop from the effects of gravity. This anticipatory rise helps prevent orthostatic hypotension. (B) Vasoconstriction in response to cooling: This is a classic negative feedback loop. The stimulus (cold) is detected, and the body responds (vasoconstriction) to correct the error (heat loss). (C) Increase in cardiac output in response to anemia: This is a negative feedback compensation. The body detects the error (low oxygen delivery) and responds (increasing cardiac output) to compensate. (D) Temperature regulation: This is a prime example of a system controlled by negative feedback.

Question 16

A patient comes to the OPD with fever. Which area is most likely affected?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Pre-optic nucleus Fever is a regulated rise in the body's thermoregulatory set-point, which is controlled by the hypothalamus. The specific "thermostat" of the body is located in the Pre-optic nucleus (also called the pre-optic area) of the anterior hypothalamus (B). During an infection, inflammatory cytokines (pyrogens) stimulate the production of Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) in this region. PGE2 then acts on neurons within the pre-optic nucleus to "raise" the set-point. The rest of the body then perceives the normal temperature as being "too cold" relative to this new, higher set-point. This triggers heat-generating mechanisms (e.g., shivering, peripheral vasoconstriction) that cause the body temperature to rise until it matches the new febrile set-point.

Question 17

Which of the following is not a mechanism by which ADH acts?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Increases absorption of NaCl in thin ascending limb Antidiuretic hormone (ADH), also called vasopressin, primarily acts on the late distal tubule and collecting duct of the nephron. Its main function is to conserve water and concentrate urine. ADH increases water permeability in the collecting ducts (Option A) by inserting aquaporin-2 channels into the apical membrane of principal cells via V2 receptor activation and cAMP signaling. This allows more water to be reabsorbed into the bloodstream. ADH also increases urea permeability in the inner medullary collecting duct (Option B). This enhances urea recycling into the medullary interstitium, helping maintain the hyperosmotic medullary gradient necessary for water reabsorption. However, ADH does not increase NaCl absorption in the thin ascending limb (Option C). The thin ascending limb has limited active transport capacity and is relatively impermeable to water; ADH does not significantly act here. Sodium reabsorption in the ascending limb is primarily a function of the thick ascending limb via the NKCC2 cotransporter. Option (D) is incorrect physiologically, but the best answer among the given choices is (C).

Question 18

Tight junctions are mainly found in which region of the cell?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Apical Tight junctions (zonula occludens) are located at the apical region of epithelial cells. They form the most apical part of the junctional complex and encircle the cell like a belt. Because they are positioned at the top of the lateral membrane, just beneath the apical surface, they are commonly described in exams as being present in the apical region. Their main function is to seal adjacent epithelial cells together, preventing paracellular movement of substances. This helps maintain selective permeability and preserves cell polarity by separating the apical and basolateral membrane domains. Structurally, tight junctions are composed of proteins such as claudins and occludin, which create a barrier to diffusion between cells. Since they are the uppermost component of the junctional complex, the best exam-oriented answer is Apical. BIOCHEMISTRY

Question 19

Two specimens from two different patients are received. Which technique can be used to confirm that they come from different individuals (in the context of Mycobacterium tuberculosis)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

RFLP The question asks for a technique to determine if two Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M.tb) isolates are from different individuals, which is the fundamental task of molecular epidemiology—tracking an outbreak. This requires a "fingerprinting" method to compare the strains. Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism (RFLP) (A) is the classic, gold-standard method for M.tb genotyping. It uses restriction enzymes to cut the bacterial DNA, and the resulting fragments are separated by size. The pattern, often based on the IS6110 insertion sequence, creates a unique "fingerprint." If the RFLP patterns from the two patients are different, it confirms they are infected with different strains and are not part of the same immediate transmission chain. Mutation analysis (B) and Pyrosequencing (C) are techniques used to look for specific, known mutations (e.g., for drug resistance), not to generate a comprehensive genomic fingerprint. RAPD (D) is another, older fingerprinting method, but RFLP is the traditional standard for M.tb.

Question 21

A 90-kg obese man is consuming a carbohydrate- rich diet. Which enzyme or intermediate is likely to be increased in his body? 1. Malonyl CoA 2. Acetyl CoA Carboxylase 3. PDH 4. Citrate Lyase

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

1,2,3,4 This scenario describes a high-energy, high-carbohydrate state, which strongly promotes de novo lipogenesis (fat synthesis). All four listed components are key players in this pathway. 1. A carbohydrate-rich diet leads to high glucose, high insulin, and high glycolysis, producing pyruvate. Pyruvate enters the mitochondria and is converted to acetyl-CoA by Pyruvate Dehydrogenase (PDH) (3), which is activated (dephosphorylated) by insulin. 2. This acetyl-CoA combines with oxaloacetate to form citrate. When energy needs are met, excess citrate is shuttled from the mitochondria to the cytoplasm. 3. In the cytoplasm, Citrate Lyase (4) cleaves citrate back into acetyl-CoA and oxaloacetate. This is the source of cytoplasmic acetyl-CoA for fatty acid synthesis. 4. The rate-limiting, committed step of lipogenesis is catalyzed by Acetyl-CoA Carboxylase (ACC) (2), which carboxylates acetyl-CoA to form Malonyl-CoA (1). ACC is allosterically activated by citrate and also activated by insulin-mediated dephosphorylation. Therefore, in this fed state, the entire pathway is active, and all four enzymes and intermediates will be elevated.

Question 22

Which of the following does not inhibit cytochrome c oxidase in the electron transport chain?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Nitric oxide (NO) Cytochrome C oxidase (Complex IV) is the final enzyme in the electron transport chain, transferring electrons to molecular oxygen. It contains heme iron and copper centers that are targets for several classic poisons. Cyanide (CN) (C) and Hydrogen Sulfide (H 2 S) (D) are potent inhibitors that bind to the ferric (Fe 3+ ) state of the heme a3 iron, preventing electron transfer and halting aerobic respiration. Carbon Monoxide (CO) (A) also inhibits Complex IV, but it binds to the ferrous (Fe 2+ ) state of the heme iron, competing with oxygen for the same binding site. This is the same mechanism by which it binds to hemoglobin. In contrast, Nitric Oxide (NO) (B) is primarily a physiological regulator of Complex IV, not a classic toxic blocker. While very high, non-physiological concentrations of NO can inhibit the enzyme, its normal role is as a reversible, signaling molecule that modulates respiration rates based on cellular needs. Therefore, in the context of classic ETC "blockers" or poisons, NO is the exception.

Question 23

Which of these tissues is unable to take up glucose in states of insulin resistance, absence of insulin, or diabetes mellitus?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Skeletal muscle Glucose uptake into cells is mediated by glucose transporters (GLUTs). The most important distinction is between insulin-dependent and insulin-independent transporters. Skeletal muscle (A) and adipose tissue are the primary users of the GLUT4 transporter. GLUT4 is an insulin-dependent transporter, meaning it is stored in intracellular vesicles and only translocates to the cell membrane to facilitate glucose uptake in response to insulin signaling. Therefore, in a state of insulin absence (like Type 1 Diabetes) or insulin resistance (like Type 2 Diabetes), glucose uptake into skeletal muscle is severely impaired. In contrast, the Brain (C) uses GLUT1 (at the blood-brain barrier) and GLUT3 (on neurons), both of which are insulin-independent to ensure a constant, vital supply of glucose. Red blood cells (B) exclusively use insulin-independent GLUT1. The Kidney (D) uses insulin-independent GLUT1 and GLUT2 (as well as SGLT) for glucose reabsorption from the filtrate. PATHOLOGY

Question 24

Least common finding in diabetic kidney is

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Armani Ebstein Diabetic nephropathy is characterized by several progressive structural changes. GBM (Glomerular Basement Membrane) thickening (D) is one of the earliest and most consistent findings. Mesangial widening (C), due to the accumulation of extracellular matrix, is also a very early and common feature, which eventually progresses to the classic nodular glomerulosclerosis (Kimmelstiel-Wilson lesions). Podocyte loss (A) and effacement of their foot processes are now understood to be critical and common events that directly contribute to the development of albuminuria. The Armani-Ebstein lesion (B) refers to the presence of glycogen-filled vacuoles within the epithelial cells of the renal proximal convoluted tubules (pars recta). While this finding is virtually pathognomonic for diabetes mellitus, it is a rare finding in modern practice. It was historically seen in patients with extremely poor glycemic control and massive glycosuria, often in the setting of diabetic ketoacidosis, before effective insulin therapy was widely available. Therefore, among the choices, the Armani-Ebstein lesion is the least common finding in a modern context.

Question 25

Alleles of gene pool belong to

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Population The term "gene pool" is a core concept in population genetics. It is defined as the complete set of all genes and their different alleles (genetic variations) that are present within a specific population (C). A population is a group of individuals of the same species that live in the same area and are capable of interbreeding. The gene pool represents the entire genetic diversity available to that population, which serves as the raw material for natural selection and evolution. An individual (A) carries only a small subset of the total gene pool (a maximum of two alleles for any given gene). A family (B) represents a closely related subset of the gene pool, but not its entirety. A single cell (D) contains the genetic information of only one individual. Thus, the concept of a "gene pool" is only meaningful at the level of a population.

Question 26

In which type of thyroid carcinoma are multiple lytic skull lesions typically seen?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Follicular carcinoma The metastatic patterns of thyroid cancers differ significantly. Follicular carcinoma (B) is classically known for its tendency to spread via hematogenous (bloodstream) dissemination, in contrast to papillary carcinoma, which spreads via lymphatics. Common sites for distant metastases from follicular carcinoma are the bone and lungs. Bone metastases are often osteolytic (bone-destroying) and can present as multiple lytic lesions on imaging. The skull, ribs, and vertebrae are common sites. Therefore, multiple lytic lesions in the skull are a classic presentation of metastatic follicular thyroid carcinoma. Papillary carcinoma (A) is the most common type and primarily spreads to cervical lymph nodes. Hurthle cell carcinoma (C), a variant of follicular carcinoma, also has a high propensity for vascular invasion and distant bone metastases, but follicular carcinoma is the classic answer. Thyroid lymphoma (D) is a hematological malignancy and does not typically present with lytic bone lesions as its primary feature.

Question 27

You would test for BRCA1 (and usually BRCA2) mutations, with BRCA1 being the primary gene associated with a strong family history of both breast and ovarian cancers.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

BRCA1/2 A strong family history of both breast and ovarian cancer is the classic hallmark of Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancer (HBOC) syndrome. This syndrome is most commonly caused by an inherited germline mutation in the BRCA1 or BRCA2 tumor suppressor genes (A). Mutations in these genes confer a significantly increased lifetime risk for developing breast cancer (often early-onset, triple-negative, or bilateral) and epithelial ovarian cancer, as well as other cancers like pancreatic and prostate cancer (especially BRCA2). While the other genes are also tumor suppressors, they are associated with different syndromes. P53 (B) mutations cause Li-Fraumeni syndrome, which involves a wide array of cancers (sarcomas, breast cancer, brain tumors, adrenocortical carcinoma). PTEN (C) mutations cause Cowden syndrome, linked to breast, thyroid, and endometrial cancers. CDH1 (D) mutations cause hereditary diffuse gastric cancer and lobular breast cancer. For the specific combination of breast and ovarian cancer, BRCA1/2 testing is the most appropriate first step.

Question 28

BRCA testing is done by

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Whole exome sequencing Testing for hereditary cancer syndromes like those caused by BRCA1/2 mutations involves identifying any of a vast number of possible pathogenic variants (mutations) that can occur anywhere along the entire coding sequence of these large genes. The most comprehensive and standard modern method for this is Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS). Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) (B) is a broad NGS technique that sequences all coding regions (exons) of the genome, which would include the BRCA genes. In clinical practice, a more "targeted" NGS panel that focuses on BRCA1/2 and other relevant cancer-susceptibility genes is often used. RT-PCR (A) and qPCR (C) are methods used to detect and quantify RNA (gene expression), not to identify unknown DNA mutations. Southern blot (D) is an older technique used to detect large-scale DNA rearrangements or deletions. While large deletions can occur in BRCA, they are typically tested for using MLPA (Multiplex Ligation-dependent Probe Amplification), and the primary method for finding the more common point mutations and small insertions/deletions is sequencing.

Question 30

Caspase-1 mediated cell death with inflammation is known as

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Pyroptosis Pyroptosis (A) is a lytic, highly inflammatory form of programmed cell death. It is defined by its dependence on the activation of Caspase-1. Pyroptosis is typically triggered by intracellular pathogens or danger signals, which are sensed by cytosolic inflammasomes (e.g., NLRP3). The inflammasome complex then cleaves pro-Caspase-1 into its active form. Active Caspase-1 has two key functions: 1. It cleaves the pore-forming protein Gasdermin D, which inserts into the cell membrane and causes cell lysis (oncosis) and the release of intracellular contents. 2. It cleaves pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1 β and IL-18 into their active, mature forms, which are then released from the lysing cell, driving a potent inflammatory response. Necroptosis (B) is also programmed and inflammatory but is caspase-independent (mediated by RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL). Ferroptosis (C) is iron-dependent cell death via lipid peroxidation. Necrosis (D) is an un-programmed, pathological form of cell death.

Question 31

Most common inherited bleeding disorder is

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Von Willebrand disease Von Willebrand disease (vWD) (A) is the most common inherited bleeding disorder, with an estimated prevalence of up to 1% of the general population, although the number of clinically symptomatic individuals is lower. It is caused by a quantitative or qualitative defect in von Willebrand factor (vWF), a protein crucial for platelet adhesion (primary hemostasis) and for stabilizing Factor VIII in circulation. It typically presents with mucocutaneous bleeding (e.g., easy bruising, nosebleeds, menorrhagia). Hemophilia A (C), a deficiency of Factor VIII, is the second most common inherited coagulation disorder, but it is much rarer than vWD, affecting approximately 1 in 5,000-10,000 males. Hemophilia B (D), a Factor IX deficiency, is even less common. Factor V deficiency (B) is an extremely rare bleeding disorder. Therefore, vWD is by far the most prevalent inherited bleeding disorder.

Question 32

In which condition is erythropoietin considered beneficial for treatment?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Anemia in chronic kidney disease Erythropoietin (EPO) is a hormone produced primarily by the peritubular interstitial cells of the kidney in response to tissue hypoxia. It stimulates the bone marrow to produce red blood cells (erythropoiesis). In Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) (A), the progressive damage to the kidneys leads to a failure of these cells to produce adequate EPO. This results in the classic "anemia of chronic disease" or "anemia of CKD," which is a normocytic, normochromic anemia. This is a state of true EPO deficiency. Therefore, treatment with synthetic EPO (recombinant human erythropoietin or other erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, ESAs) is a cornerstone of management, as it directly replaces the missing hormone. In Nutritional anemia (C), the problem is a lack of substrates (e.g., iron, B12), and EPO levels are appropriately high. In Aplastic anemia (D), the bone marrow stem cells have failed; EPO levels are extremely high, but the marrow cannot respond. In MDS (B), the anemia is complex, and while some patients may respond to EPO, the most direct and classic indication is CKD.

Question 33

A 32-year-old woman presents with easy bruising and petechiae, a platelet count of 18,000/mm³, normal hemoglobin and leukocyte counts, and a recent mild upper respiratory infection. Her peripheral smear is normal. What is the most appropriate next step in management?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Oral corticosteroids This patient has isolated thrombocytopenia (platelet count 18,000/mm³) with normal hemoglobin and leukocyte counts, a normal peripheral smear, and a recent viral infection. This presentation is classic for immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP), an autoimmune condition in which antibodies (usually IgG) target platelets, leading to their destruction in the spleen. In adults with symptomatic ITP or platelet count below 30,000/mm³, the first-line treatment is corticosteroids (such as oral prednisone or dexamethasone). Steroids reduce antibody production and decrease splenic clearance of platelets. Platelet transfusion is reserved for severe or life-threatening bleeding, as transfused platelets are rapidly destroyed. Bone marrow biopsy is not routinely required when the clinical picture clearly suggests ITP. Splenectomy is considered a second-line therapy for chronic or steroid-refractory cases. Therefore, the most appropriate next step is oral corticosteroids. MEDINK

Question 34

A deficiency of glucose-6-phosphatase is associated with which pattern of bilirubin levels?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Indirect bilirubin increased, direct bilirubin normal, and raised urine urobilinogen Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency is an inherited enzymatic defect that makes red blood cells highly susceptible to oxidative stress. G6PD normally generates NADPH through the pentose phosphate pathway, which helps maintain glutathione in its reduced form. Reduced glutathione protects red blood cells from oxidative damage. When G6PD is deficient, oxidative stress (from infections, certain drugs, or fava beans) leads to hemolysis. During hemolysis, hemoglobin is released and broken down into heme, which is converted into biliverdin and then into unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin. Because this bilirubin is produced in excess, its level in blood rises. The liver itself is structurally normal and capable of conjugation, so direct (conjugated) bilirubin remains normal. However, increased amounts of unconjugated bilirubin reach the liver and then the intestine after conjugation. In the intestine, bilirubin is converted into urobilinogen. Since more bilirubin is being processed due to hemolysis, more urobilinogen is formed and reabsorbed into the bloodstream, leading to increased urinary urobilinogen levels. Thus, G6PD deficiency produces indirect hyperbilirubinemia with normal direct bilirubin and raised urine urobilinogen, corresponding to option (D).

Question 35

Neutrophilic granulomatous mastitis is caused by which organism?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Corynebacterium kroppenstedtii Idiopathic Granulomatous Mastitis (IGM) is a rare, chronic inflammatory breast condition. While often labeled "idiopathic," a specific subgroup known as Cystic Neutrophilic Granulomatous Mastitis has been strongly and specifically associated with infection by the lipophilic, gram-positive rod Corynebacterium kroppenstedtii (A). This organism is fastidious and difficult to culture, which is why the association was only recently established. It is thought to colonize and infect the breast ducts, triggering a robust inflammatory response characterized by granulomas (collections of histiocytes) and a prominent neutrophilic infiltrate, often leading to abscesses and sinus tracts. Mycobacterium tuberculosis (C) causes tuberculous mastitis, which features classic caseating granulomas, but not typically a neutrophilic component. Staphylococcus aureus (D) causes acute suppurative mastitis or abscesses, which are rich in neutrophils but do not form the organized granulomas of IGM. Corynebacterium minutissimum (B) is known for causing the superficial skin infection erythrasma.

Question 36

Which of the following malignancies is unrelated to obesity?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Stomach Obesity is a major modifiable risk factor for many cancers, acting through mechanisms like chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and hormonal (adipokine/estrogen) dysregulation. There are well-established, strong links between obesity and Pancreatic cancer (B), Meningioma (C), and Multiple myeloma (D). The relationship with Stomach cancer (A) is more complex and site-specific. Obesity is a strong risk factor for cancer of the gastric cardia (the upper part of the stomach), which is thought to be mediated by the increased prevalence of gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) in obese individuals. However, obesity is not strongly associated, and may even be inversely related, to cancers of the non-cardia stomach (the main body and antrum), which are more commonly linked to H. pylori infection. Because the association is not straightforward and is location-dependent (unlike the clear links for B, C, and D), "Stomach" is the most likely correct answer for a malignancy that is not uniformly related to obesity.

Question 37

Histopathological features of granulosa cell tumor include all except:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

CA-125 positivity Granulosa cell tumor (GCT) is the most common malignant sex cord-stromal tumor of the ovary. It has a set of classic histopathological and immunohistochemical features. 1. Call-Exner bodies (A) are a pathognomonic finding, representing small, rosette-like structures where granulosa cells arrange around a central eosinophilic, PAS-positive space. 2. Coffee bean nuclei (D) is the descriptive term for the tumor cell nuclei, which are ovoid and have a prominent longitudinal groove. 3. Inhibin A (B) is an immunohistochemical stain. As GCTs are derived from granulosa cells, they produce inhibin, and the tumor cells show strong, diffuse positivity for inhibin, making it a key diagnostic marker. CA-125 (C) is a tumor marker, both in serum and on immunohistochemistry, that is characteristic of epithelial ovarian cancers (e.g., serous carcinoma). While serum CA-125 can sometimes be elevated in GCT, it is not a specific or characteristic histopathological feature of the tumor cells themselves and is not used for primary diagnosis.

Question 38

Which stain is not used to identify organisms in a renal transplant biopsy?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Congo red In a renal transplant (immunocompromised) patient, a biopsy is often stained to rule out opportunistic infections. Gomori Methenamine Silver (GMS) (A) is the classic stain for fungi, highlighting fungal walls in black. It is used to identify Pneumocystis, Candida, or Aspergillus. PAS (Periodic Acid-Schiff) (B), while a standard stain for basement membranes, also stains fungal walls (magenta) and can help identify them. Masson Fontana (C) is a stain for melanin. It is used in microbiology to identify the capsule of Cryptococcus neoformans, which contains melanin-like pigments. In contrast, Congo red (D) is a special stain used to identify amyloid deposits. Amyloid stains orange-red with Congo red, and under polarized light, it exhibits a pathognomonic "apple-green" birefringence. This stain is used to diagnose amyloidosis, a protein deposition disease, and is not used to identify infectious organisms.

Question 39

Why does not every Helicobacter pylori infection lead to ulcers?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Majority of H. pylori infections are asymptomatic Helicobacter pylori is a spiral-shaped, gram-negative bacterium that colonizes the gastric mucosa, particularly the antrum. Although it is a major cause of peptic ulcer disease and is associated with gastritis, gastric carcinoma, and MALT lymphoma, most infected individuals never develop clinically significant disease. The reason not every infection leads to ulcers is that the majority of infections remain asymptomatic. Many people harbor the organism for years without developing mucosal damage severe enough to produce ulceration. Ulcer formation depends on multiple interacting factors such as bacterial virulence (e.g., CagA, VacA strains), host immune response, gastric acid secretion levels, genetic susceptibility, and environmental influences like smoking or NSAID use. In many individuals, the infection causes only mild chronic gastritis without disrupting the protective mucosal barrier to the extent required for ulcer formation. Therefore, while H. pylori infection is common worldwide, only a small proportion of infected individuals develop peptic ulcers. Hence, most H. pylori infections do not lead to ulcers because they are asymptomatic and non-ulcerative in the majority of cases.

Question 40

In which of the following situations does transplant rejection not occur?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (SCID)Transplant rejection is an immune response mediated by the recipient's T-lymphocytes and B-lymphocytes, which recognize the donor graft as foreign. This requires a functional adaptive immune system. Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (SCID) (C) is a group of genetic disorders characterized by a profound failure of both T-cell and B-cell development and function. Patients with "classic" SCID have no adaptive immune system and are therefore incapable of mounting an immune response. They cannot recognize or attack a foreign graft, so transplant rejection does not occur. (Instead, they are at high risk of Graft-versus-Host Disease from any T-cells within the transplant). Bare lymphocyte syndrome (A) is a type of SCID, so it is also correct, but SCID is the broader and more definitive answer. DiGeorge syndrome (D) involves T-cell deficiency but is often partial, so rejection is still possible. Chronic granulomatous disease (B) is a defect in phagocyte (innate) immunity, but T-cell (adaptive) immunity is intact and fully capable of rejection.

Question 41

What explains increased oxygen consumption by cancer cells?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Cancer cells rely on anaerobic glycolysis even in presence of oxygen (Warburg effect) The phenomenon known as the Warburg effect, described by Otto Warburg, explains the altered metabolism of cancer cells. Normally, in the presence of oxygen, healthy cells generate energy primarily through oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria, which is efficient and produces large amounts of ATP. However, cancer cells preferentially use glycolysis to generate energy, even when adequate oxygen is available. This process is termed “aerobic glycolysis.” Although glycolysis produces far less ATP per glucose molecule compared to oxidative phosphorylation, cancer cells compensate by markedly increasing glucose uptake and glycolytic rate. The metabolic intermediates produced during glycolysis are diverted into biosynthetic pathways that support rapid cell proliferation, such as nucleotide, lipid, and amino acid synthesis. Thus, the key explanation is not increased mitochondrial activity or oxidative phosphorylation, but rather the paradoxical reliance on glycolysis despite oxygen availability. This metabolic reprogramming supports tumor growth and is the basis for FDG-PET imaging in oncology.

Question 42

What technique is illustrated in the graph that depicts antigen-antibody interaction and the kinetics of the immune response?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Surface plasmon resonance for affinity Although no graph is shown, the question's text provides the key clues. The technique must be one that demonstrates "response kinetics" for an "antibody-antigen interaction." This implies a real-time measurement of the binding process. ELISA (A, B) is an endpoint assay; it measures the total amount of binding that has occurred after a set time and washing steps, but it does not show the "kinetics" (the rate of binding and unbinding). Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) (C, D) is the gold-standard technique for measuring real-time biomolecular interactions. In SPR, one molecule (e.g., antigen) is fixed to a sensor chip, and the other (e.g., antibody) is flowed over it. The binding and dissociation events are monitored in real-time, producing a "response" curve. From this kinetic data, one can calculate the association rate (kon) and dissociation rate (koff), which are then used to determine the affinity ($K_D$) of the interaction. Therefore, SPR is the technique used to measure interaction kinetics for determining affinity.

Question 43

In which disease is an HLA-mismatched transplant not considered problematic?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Bare lymphocyte syndrome An HLA-mismatched transplant normally fails due to Graft Rejection (recipient T-cells attack the graft) or Graft-versus-Host Disease (GVHD) (donor T-cells attack the recipient). This question asks in which condition this "problem" doesn't occur. Bare Lymphocyte Syndrome (BLS) (A) is a form of Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (SCID). In BLS Type II (the most common form), there is a genetic defect that prevents the expression of MHC Class II molecules. Without MHC II, the patient's antigen-presenting cells cannot activate CD4+ "helper" T-cells. This results in a profound T-cell immunodeficiency, meaning the patient cannot mount an immune response to reject the graft. Furthermore, because the recipient's own tissues also lack MHC II, the donor T-cells in the graft have a reduced ability to recognize the host, significantly mitigating the risk of GVHD. This unique situation makes BLS one of the few conditions where an HLA-mismatched transplant can be successful. The other conditions (B, C, D) are all immunodeficiencies, but T-cell function is sufficiently intact to cause either rejection or severe GVHD.

Question 44

What is the role of Pro calcitonin in pneumonia?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Early antibiotic discontinuation Procalcitonin (PCT) is a biomarker that is highly specific for systemic bacterial infections. Its levels rise significantly in response to bacterial endotoxins but typically remain low in viral infections or non-infectious inflammatory states. This makes it very useful in managing respiratory infections like pneumonia. While a very low PCT level can guide initiation (A) (by suggesting a viral cause where antibiotics may be withheld), its most robust, evidence-based, and widely adopted role is in guiding antibiotic discontinuation (B). In a patient being treated for bacterial pneumonia, PCT levels will fall as the infection is controlled. Serial monitoring of PCT allows clinicians to identify when the infection has resolved, even if the patient has other reasons for inflammation (e.g., post-operative state). Following a PCT-guided algorithm (e.g., stopping antibiotics when PCT drops by >80% from its peak) has been shown to safely reduce the total duration of antibiotic therapy, which helps minimize side effects and antibiotic resistance. Question 45

Which of the following statements regarding thalidomide is incorrect?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Used as an antimetabolite immunosuppressant The incorrect statement is (A). Thalidomide is a powerful immunomodulatory and anti-angiogenic drug. Its mechanism involves binding to the cereblon (CRBN) protein, which alters a "molecular glue" E3 ubiquitin ligase complex, leading to the degradation of specific transcription factors (Ikaros and Aiolos). It is not classified as an antimetabolite. Antimetabolites (e.g., methotrexate, 5-fluorouracil, azathioprine) are drugs that structurally resemble and interfere with key cellular metabolites, thereby blocking the synthesis of DNA, RNA, or proteins. All the other statements are correct: 1. It is teratogenic (D) is its most infamous property, discovered after it caused severe phocomelia (limb defects) in the 1950s. 2. Used in management of erythema nodosum leprosum (ENL) (C) is a classic indication; it is highly effective for this inflammatory complication of leprosy. 3. Used in treatment of multiple myeloma (B), along with its derivatives lenalidomide and pomalidomide, is a cornerstone of modern therapy for this plasma cell malignancy.

Question 46

What is the recommended treatment for complicated gonorrhea caused by penicillin- resistant Neisseria gonorrhoeae?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Ceftriaxone Neisseria gonorrhoeae has a remarkable ability to develop antibiotic resistance. It is now resistant to many older therapies, including penicillins (like Amoxicillin (B)) and Tetracyclines (C). The current standard of care and recommended treatment, especially for penicillin-resistant strains and for complicated gonorrhoea (e.g., pelvic inflammatory disease, disseminated gonococcal infection), is a third-generation cephalosporin. Ceftriaxone (A), given intramuscularly or intravenously, is highly effective and has been the mainstay of treatment for decades. Current guidelines (e.g., CDC) recommend a single 500 mg IM dose of ceftriaxone for uncomplicated infections, with higher doses or multi-day IV therapy for complicated cases. Vancomycin (D) is a glycopeptide used for resistant Gram-positive organisms (like MRSA) and has no role in treating N. gonorrhoeae, which is a Gram-negative diplococcus.

Question 47

All of the following is/are adverse effects of topical corticosteroids except?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Blue-grey discoloration of skin Topical corticosteroids are widely used for their anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects, but they carry a risk of significant local adverse effects, especially with prolonged use or high potency. Atrophy (D) of the epidermis is the most common and characteristic adverse effect, leading to thin, fragile skin with striae. Other common side effects include the development of acne (B) (steroid-induced acne or rosacea) and hypertrichosis (C), which is the growth of fine hair in the treated area. The skin's barrier function is also compromised, increasing the risk of cutaneous infections. In contrast, blue-grey discoloration of the skin (A) is not a feature of topical steroid use. This type of pigmentation is classically associated with drugs like amiodarone, minocycline, or heavy metals such as silver (argyria). Therefore, blue-grey discoloration is the exception among the listed side effects of topical corticosteroids.

Question 48

Which of the following is not true regarding bempedoic acid?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

It non-competitively inhibits ATP citrate lyase Bempedoic acid is a newer, non-statin agent used for lowering LDL cholesterol. It is a prodrug that is activated in the liver. Its mechanism (D) is to inhibit de novo cholesterol synthesis at a step upstream of statins. It blocks ATP citrate lyase (ACL), the enzyme that converts citrate to acetyl-CoA in the cytoplasm, thereby reducing the acetyl-CoA pool available for cholesterol and fatty acid synthesis. This inhibition is competitive with its substrate, citrate, not non-competitive (B). Statement (C) is its correct clinical indication: it is used as an adjunct to diet and maximally tolerated statin therapy for patients who need further LDL-C reduction. While it is technically a prodrug, its active form, bempedoyl-CoA, works, and the parent molecule (A) is indeed a dicarboxylic acid. Question 49

A patient is given imipramine along with diphenhydramine. What effect can be expected in this situation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Both have anti-muscarinic actions This question involves a drug-drug interaction, specifically a pharmacodynamic additive effect. 1. Imipramine is a Tricyclic Antidepressant (TCA). While its primary therapeutic effect is inhibiting the reuptake of norepinephrine and serotonin, it is also known for its "dirty" receptor profile, which includes potent blockade of muscarinic acetylcholine receptors. 2. Diphenhydramine is a first-generation antihistamine (H1 blocker). A primary property of first-generation antihistamines is their strong anti-muscarinic (anticholinergic) activity. When both drugs are administered together (C), their individual anti-muscarinic effects will add up, leading to a high risk of anticholinergic toxicity. This can manifest as dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, constipation, tachycardia, and in severe cases, delirium ("mad as a hatter, blind as a bat..."). Imipramine does not primarily reduce diphenhydramine elimination (B), and diphenhydramine does not inhibit imipramine's action (A) or inhibit norepinephrine reuptake (D).

Question 50

Which of the following is a clinical cure for malaria?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine A clinical cure in malaria means elimination of the asexual erythrocytic (blood) stages of the parasite, thereby resolving symptoms such as fever and anemia. It does not necessarily eliminate dormant liver forms (hypnozoites), so relapse may still occur in Plasmodium vivax or Plasmodium ovale infections. Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine is a combination antifolate therapy that acts synergistically by inhibiting folate synthesis in the malaria parasite. Sulfadoxine inhibits dihydropteroate synthase, while pyrimethamine inhibits dihydrofolate reductase. This dual blockade prevents DNA synthesis in the parasite’s blood stages, leading to clearance of parasitemia and resolution of clinical symptoms. In contrast: 1. Primaquine provides a radical cure by eliminating hepatic hypnozoites. 2. Chloroquine and Artesunate are also blood schizonticides but are not specifically referred to as clinical cure agents in this context. Therefore, sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine achieves a clinical cure by clearing the blood-stage parasites responsible for symptoms.
