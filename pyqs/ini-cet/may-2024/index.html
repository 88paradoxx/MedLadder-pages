Question 1

The spinal sensory nucleus of the trigeminal nerve sends its second-order neurons to transmit which type of sensation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Pain The sensory nucleus of the trigeminal nerve in the brainstem is divided into three parts. The principal (or chief) sensory nucleus processes discriminative touch and pressure. The mesencephalic nucleus handles proprioception from the muscles of mastication. The spinal sensory nucleus is primarily responsible for processing protopathic sensations, which include pain and temperature, as well as crude touch from the ipsilateral side of the face. First-order neurons from the trigeminal ganglion synapse in the spinal nucleus. The second-order neurons then decussate (cross the midline) and ascend in the ventral two-dimensionality tract to the contralateral thalamus (VPM nucleus). From there, third-order neurons project to the somatosensory cortex. Fine touch (Option A) is primarily handled by the principal nucleus, while vibration (Option C) and proprioception (Option D) are carried by other pathways.

Question 2

The substantia nigra has functional connections with which component of the basal ganglia?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Striatum The substantia nigra, located in the midbrain, is a crucial component of the basal ganglia motor circuitry. It is most famously and extensively connected to the striatum (which consists of the caudate nucleus and the putamen). This connection forms the nigrostriatal pathway, where dopaminergic neurons from the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) project to the striatum to modulate its activity. This pathway is essential for the initiation and smooth execution of voluntary movements, and its degeneration is the primary pathology in Parkinson's disease. While the substantia nigra pars reticulata receives input from the striatum and projects to the thalamus (Option D), its most prominent functional connection is its dopaminergic input to the striatum. The pallidum (Option B) and subthalamic nucleus (Option C) are other key nodes within the basal ganglia circuit but are not the primary connection site for the substantia nigra.

Question 3

Which structure is NOT present in the floor of the inferior horn of the lateral ventricle?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Tail of the caudate nucleus The inferior (temporal) horn of the lateral ventricle extends into the temporal lobe. Its floor is a complex surface formed by several structures. These include the hippocampus (Option A), a key structure for memory formation, which creates a prominent bulge in the floor. The fimbria of the hippocampus (Option C), a band of efferent fibers, runs along its medial edge. The most lateral part of the floor is the collateral eminence (Option D), a ridge formed by the underlying collateral sulcus. In contrast, the tail of the caudate nucleus is a component of the roof of the inferior horn. It arches over the thalamus and follows the curve of the lateral ventricle, terminating anteriorly at the amygdala. Therefore, it is not found in the floor of the inferior horn.

Question 4

Which of the following statements accurately describes the anatomy of the breast?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The axillary tail of Spence crosses the anterior axillary fold The axillary tail of Spence is a superolateral extension of breast tissue from the upper outer quadrant of the breast into the axilla. It passes through an opening in the deep fascia known as the foramen of Langer. As it extends toward the axilla, it runs along and effectively crosses the region of the anterior axillary fold, which is formed by the lower border of the pectoralis major muscle. Clinically, this is important because breast tissue in the axillary tail can develop lumps or malignancy and may be mistaken for axillary lymph nodes. 1. Option (A) is incorrect because the upper outer quadrant, not the superior medial quadrant, contains the greatest amount of glandular tissue and is the most common site of breast carcinoma. 2. Option (B) is incorrect since the breast typically contains 15–20 lobes, each drained by a lactiferous duct. 3. Option (D) states that the nipple lies at the fourth intercostal space; although often used as a landmark, its position varies with age and body habitus. Therefore, the most accurate anatomical statement is option (C).

Question 5

Which step in thyroid surgery is least likely to cause hoarseness of voice?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Division of strap muscles Post-thyroidectomy hoarseness is typically caused by injury to the laryngeal nerves. Ligation of the superior thyroid artery (Option A) risks injury to the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve, which affects voice pitch. The recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) is at high risk during dissection near the tubercle of Zuckerkandl (Option C) and within Beahrs triangle (the space between the common carotid artery, inferior thyroid artery, and RLN) (Option D). Injury to the RLN causes significant hoarseness. In contrast, the division of the strap muscles (sternohyoid and sternothyroid) (Option B) is performed to gain surgical access to the thyroid gland. These muscles are innervated by the ansa cervicalis and their division does not directly endanger the laryngeal nerves responsible for vocal cord function. Therefore, this step is the least likely to cause voice changes.

Question 6

Lower lip paralysis following a parotidectomy is most likely caused by damage to which structure?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Mandibular branch of the facial nerve Parotidectomy involves dissection around the facial nerve (CN VII), which passes through the parotid gland. The facial nerve gives off several branches to supply the muscles of facial expression. Weakness or paralysis of the lower lip, specifically the inability to depress the corner of the mouth (resulting in an asymmetric smile), is a classic sign of injury to the marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve. This branch innervates the depressor anguli oris and depressor labii inferioris muscles. The buccal branch (Option B) innervates the buccinator and muscles of the upper lip. The temporal branch (Option C) innervates the frontalis and orbicularis oculi. The cervical branch (Option D) innervates the platysma muscle.

Question 7

S2 The ischial spine is a prominent bony landmark on the posterior border of the ischium. It serves as an important landmark in obstetrics for assessing fetal head descent (station 0) and in anesthesiology for administering a pudendal nerve block. In relation to the vertebral column, its horizontal plane corresponds approximately to the level of the second sacral vertebra (S2). Question 8

Which statement about cardiac muscle depolarization is incorrect?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Tetany Cardiac muscle possesses several unique physiological properties. It adheres to the all or none law (Option C), meaning a stimulus above threshold causes the entire muscle fiber to contract maximally. It also exhibits a length-tension relationship (Option D), known as the Frank-Starling mechanism, where increased initial fiber length (preload) results in a more forceful contraction. A critical feature of the cardiac action potential is its long plateau phase due to calcium influx, which results in a large refractory period (Option A). This long refractory period is essential as it prevents the summation of individual muscle twitches. Consequently, cardiac muscle cannot undergo a sustained contraction, or tetany (Option B). Tetanus is a characteristic of skeletal muscle but is physiologically impossible in cardiac muscle, ensuring the heart functions as a pump with distinct relaxation (filling) phases.

Question 9

A decrease in GFR associated with which of the following?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Increased glomerular oncotic pressure The Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) is determined by the balance of hydrostatic and oncotic pressures across the glomerular capillary wall (Starling forces). GFR is favored by glomerular hydrostatic pressure, and opposed by Bowman's space hydrostatic pressure and glomerular oncotic pressure. A decrease in GFR would be caused by factors that either reduce the forces favoring filtration or increase the forces opposing it. Increased RBF (A) and increased glomerular hydrostatic pressure (B) would both increase GFR. Decreased Bowman's oncotic pressure (C) is typically negligible and a decrease would slightly increase GFR. However, an increased glomerular oncotic pressure (D) directly opposes filtration. This occurs, for example, in dehydration or conditions with high plasma protein concentration, increasing the oncotic "pull" that holds fluid within the capillaries and thereby decreasing the GFR.

Question 10

Erythropoietin is secreted by which of the following? 1. Liver 2. Kidney 3. Muscle 4. Heart

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

1,2 Erythropoietin (EPO) is the primary hormone that stimulates the production of red blood cells (erythropoiesis) in the bone marrow. The main site of EPO production in adults is the kidney (2). Specifically, it is synthesized by peritubular interstitial cells in the renal cortex in response to tissue hypoxia. A smaller amount of EPO, approximately 10-15% of the total, is produced by hepatocytes in the liver (1). During fetal life, the liver is the primary site of EPO production. Muscle (3) and the heart (4) do not produce significant amounts of erythropoietin. Therefore, both the kidney and the liver are sites of EPO secretion, making option A the correct choice.

Question 11

Which features are characteristically seen in upper motor neuron (UMN) lesions?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

DTR increased Upper motor neuron (UMN) lesions affect the neural pathway above the anterior horn cell of the spinal cord or motor nuclei of cranial nerves. The resulting clinical signs are distinct from lower motor neuron (LMN) lesions. Key features of a UMN lesion include spasticity (velocity-dependent increase in muscle tone), weakness, and hyperreflexia, meaning deep tendon reflexes (DTR) are increased or exaggerated (C). Pathological reflexes like the Babinski sign may also be present. In contrast, atrophy (A) (specifically, severe or neurogenic atrophy), fasciculations (B) (visible muscle twitches), and flaccid paralysis are characteristic of LMN lesions. Rigidity (D), a lead-pipe or cogwheel resistance to passive movement, is a hallmark of extrapyramidal disorders like Parkinson's disease, not typically a UMN sign.

Question 12

Which hormone has the smallest influence on fetal growth during pregnancy?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Growth hormone Fetal growth is a complex process regulated by a different set of hormones compared to postnatal growth. Insulin (A) is a crucial fetal growth factor, particularly in the third trimester, promoting somatic growth and fat deposition. Thyroid hormone (B) is essential for normal development of the fetal central nervous system and skeleton. Cortisol (D) plays a vital role in the maturation of fetal organs, especially the lungs (surfactant production). In stark contrast, pituitary Growth Hormone (GH) (C) plays a minimal to insignificant role in fetal somatic growth. Children born with congenital GH deficiency or GH receptor defects have a normal birth weight and length. The major postnatal growth-regulating axis (GHRH-GH-IGF-1) becomes critical only after birth. Therefore, growth hormone has the least significant role during fetal life.

Question 13

Select the correct sequence of events in the CAMP signaling pathway:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Adenyl cyclase → CAMP → РКА The cyclic AMP (cAMP) signaling pathway is a common second messenger system initiated by the binding of a ligand (like epinephrine) to a G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR). This binding activates the G protein, which in turn activates the membrane-bound enzyme adenylyl cyclase. Adenylyl cyclase then catalyzes the conversion of ATP into cyclic AMP (cAMP). The increase in intracellular cAMP concentration activates Protein Kinase A (PKA). Activated PKA then phosphorylates various downstream target proteins, leading to a cellular response. Therefore, the correct sequence of events is the activation of adenylyl cyclase, which produces cAMP, which in turn activates PKA. This corresponds to option B. BIOCHEMISTRY

Question 15

Congenital lactic acidosis results from a defect in which enzyme?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Pyruvate dehydrogenase Lactic acidosis occurs when lactate production exceeds its clearance. A major cause of congenital lactic acidosis is a deficiency in the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex. This crucial mitochondrial enzyme complex converts pyruvate, the end product of glycolysis, into acetyl-CoA for entry into the Krebs cycle. When the PDH complex is deficient, pyruvate cannot be efficiently metabolized aerobically. Instead, it is shunted into the anaerobic pathway and converted to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase, leading to a significant accumulation of lactic acid in the blood. Branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase deficiency (B) causes Maple Syrup Urine Disease. Transketolase (A) is a pentose phosphate pathway enzyme. Isocitrate dehydrogenase (C) is a Krebs cycle enzyme whose deficiency would impair the cycle but not be the primary cause of congenital lactic acidosis.

Question 16

Which of the following is not produced during the breakdown of heme?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Aminolevulinic acid Heme catabolism is the breakdown pathway for heme, primarily from senescent red blood cells. The first and rate-limiting step is catalyzed by the enzyme heme oxygenase. This enzyme opens the porphyrin ring to produce three products: biliverdin (C), a linear tetrapyrrole; ferrous iron (Fe2+) (A), which is recycled; and carbon monoxide (CO) (D), a signaling molecule that is eventually exhaled. Biliverdin is then converted to bilirubin. In contrast, aminolevulinic acid (ALA) is a key intermediate in the synthesis of heme, not a product of its breakdown. The synthesis of heme begins with the condensation of glycine and succinyl-CoA to form ALA, catalyzed by ALA synthase. Therefore, ALA is a precursor to heme, not a catabolic product.

Question 17

Which gene defect causes familial hypercholesterolemia?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

LDL receptor Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder characterized by very high levels of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol in the blood, leading to premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. The most common cause of FH, accounting for over 90% of cases, is a loss-of-function mutation in the gene encoding the LDL receptor. These receptors are located on the surface of cells, primarily hepatocytes, and are responsible for clearing LDL from the circulation. A defect in the LDL receptor leads to impaired uptake of LDL, causing its accumulation in the plasma. Mutations in ApoB-100 (the ligand for the LDL receptor) can also cause a similar phenotype, but LDL receptor defects are the primary cause. Defects in Apo E (A), Apo CII (B), and Apo B48 (D) are associated with other types of dyslipidemias.

Question 18

A male patient with hyperpigmentation and Tanner stage 5 who presents with hypertension and signs of precocious puberty is described. What underlying defect is most likely responsible for this presentation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

11-Beta hydroxylase deficiency This clinical triad—precocious puberty (virilization), hypertension, and hyperpigmentation—is the classic presentation of 11-Beta hydroxylase deficiency, a form of congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH). The enzyme deficiency blocks the conversion of 11-deoxycortisol to cortisol and deoxycorticosterone (DOC) to corticosterone. The lack of cortisol leads to a loss of negative feedback on the pituitary, causing overproduction of ACTH. High ACTH levels lead to adrenal hyperplasia and shunting of steroid precursors toward androgen synthesis, causing precocious puberty. The accumulation of DOC, a potent mineralocorticoid, causes sodium and water retention, leading to hypertension. High ACTH also stimulates melanocytes, causing hyperpigmentation. 21-hydroxylase deficiency (A) causes virilization but with hypotension. 17-alpha hydroxylase deficiency (D) causes hypertension but prevents sex steroid synthesis, leading to sexual infantilism.

Question 19

Interpret the following arterial blood gas (ABG) values: pH=7.5, pCO 2 =50 mm Hg, HCO 3- =30 mEq/L

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Metabolic alkalosis To interpret an ABG, we follow a systematic approach. First, check the pH. A pH of 7.5 is above the normal range (7.35-7.45), indicating alkalosis. Second, determine if the primary cause is respiratory or metabolic. The pCO 2 is 50 mm Hg, which is higher than the normal range (35-45 mm Hg). An elevated pCO 2 causes acidosis, which is opposite to the patient's alkalotic state, so the primary problem is not respiratory. The HCO 3- is 30 mEq/L, which is higher than the normal range (22-26 mEq/L). An elevated HCO 3- causes alkalosis. Therefore, the primary disorder is metabolic alkalosis. The elevated pCO 2 represents a respiratory compensation, where the body hypoventilates to retain CO 2 (an acid) to counteract the metabolic alkalosis.

Question 20

Which of the following is not involved in post- transcriptional modification?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Glycosylation Post-transcriptional modification refers to the processes that a primary RNA transcript (pre-mRNA) undergoes to become a mature messenger RNA (mRNA) before it can be translated into a protein. Key modifications include the addition of a 5' cap (a modified guanine nucleotide) (C), splicing to remove introns which involves endonuclease cleavage (B), and the addition of a poly-A tail to the 3' end. RNA molecules themselves can also undergo chemical modifications like methylation (A). In contrast, glycosylation (D) is a post-translational modification. It is the process of adding carbohydrate chains (glycans) to proteins or lipids, which occurs in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus after the protein has been synthesized (translated) from the mRNA template. Therefore, glycosylation is not a post-transcriptional modification.

Question 21

Which deficiency is linked to impaired sexual maturation and hypogonadism?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Zinc (Zn) Zinc (Zn) is an essential trace mineral that functions as a cofactor for hundreds of enzymes and is crucial for numerous physiological processes, including immune function, wound healing, and protein synthesis. A key role of zinc is in the function of the reproductive system and normal growth and development. Zinc deficiency is classically associated with growth retardation, impaired immune response, skin lesions (acrodermatitis enteropathica), and hypogonadism, which leads to delayed or failed sexual maturation. This is because zinc is necessary for the synthesis and action of gonadotropins and testosterone. Deficiencies in selenium (A), copper (C), and iron (D) cause other distinct clinical syndromes but are not primarily characterized by hypogonadism and defective sexual maturation.

Question 22

Which type of mutation leads to restoration of the wild-type phenotype when the mutant gene is suppressed?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Mutation of tRNA This question describes a suppressor mutation, where a second mutation at a different site reverses the effect of the first mutation, restoring the wild-type phenotype. One classic example of this is a nonsense suppressor, which involves a mutation of a tRNA gene. For instance, if a primary mutation changes a codon for an amino acid into a premature stop codon (a nonsense mutation), it causes truncated protein synthesis. A second mutation in the anticodon of a tRNA molecule can allow that tRNA to recognize the stop codon and insert an amino acid instead, allowing translation to continue and producing a full-length (and often functional) protein. A frameshift mutation (A) is not typically reversed this way. Adding a normal gene (B) is complementation, not suppression. Deleting the mutant gene (C) would not restore the function.

Question 23

Which diagnostic tool is preferred for metabolic disease screening in children?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Tandem mass spectrometry Newborn screening for inborn errors of metabolism has been revolutionized by the use of tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS). This powerful analytical technique allows for the rapid, simultaneous, and quantitative detection of dozens of different metabolites (like amino acids and acylcarnitines) from a single dried blood spot collected from a heel prick. This enables screening for a wide array of metabolic disorders, including amino acidopathies (e.g., PKU), organic acidemias, and fatty acid oxidation defects, from one sample. While urinalysis (C) and complete blood count (B) are common tests, they are not comprehensive screening tools for metabolic diseases. Genetic testing (D) is typically used as a confirmatory test after an abnormal screening result from MS/MS, rather than as the initial screening tool itself due to cost and complexity.

Question 24

Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) can identify all of the following except which one?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Point mutation Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) is a cytogenetic technique that uses fluorescently labeled DNA probes to visualize specific DNA sequences on chromosomes. It is highly effective for detecting large-scale chromosomal abnormalities. It can identify translocations (A), where a piece of one chromosome breaks off and attaches to another (e.g., using fusion or break-apart probes). It can detect gene amplification (B), seen as multiple copies of a fluorescent signal for a specific gene. It can also identify deletions (C) of chromosomal regions, indicated by the absence of a fluorescent signal where one is expected. However, FISH is not sensitive enough to detect very small-scale changes like point mutations (D), which involve a change in a single DNA base pair. Detecting point mutations requires DNA sequencing or allele-specific PCR techniques.

Question 25

In folate deficiency, which of the following statements is FALSE?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Low homocysteine & high methylmalonic acid The metabolism of folate and vitamin B12 are interconnected, and a deficiency in either can lead to megaloblastic anemia by impairing purine and pyrimidine synthesis (D). Both vitamins are required for the conversion of homocysteine to methionine, so a deficiency in either leads to elevated homocysteine. However, only vitamin B12 is needed for the conversion of methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA. Therefore, an elevated methylmalonic acid (MMA) level is specific to B12 deficiency; in folate deficiency, MMA levels are normal. Statement (A) is false because folate deficiency causes high homocysteine and normal MMA. The anemia is megaloblastic (due to ineffective erythropoiesis), not hemolytic (B is also false, but A is metabolically definitive). Clinically, it is often wise to supplement with B12 alongside folate (C) until a B12 deficiency is ruled out. PATHOLOGY

Question 26

Which of the following serves as an intranuclear immunohistochemical marker for neuroendocrine tumors?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

INSM1 Immunohistochemistry is crucial for diagnosing neuroendocrine tumors (NETs). Synaptophysin (A) and Chromogranin A (B) are the two most common and classic markers for NETs, but they are both cytoplasmic proteins. NCAM1/CD56 (D) is a cell surface marker also expressed by many NETs. In contrast, Insulinoma-associated protein 1 (INSM1) is a zinc-finger transcription factor that is expressed early in neuroendocrine differentiation. As a transcription factor, INSM1 is located in the nucleus. It has emerged as a highly sensitive and specific intranuclear marker for neuroendocrine tumors across various sites, including the lung, pancreas, and gastrointestinal tract, and is particularly useful in poorly differentiated cases where cytoplasmic markers may be weak or absent.

Question 27

A patient presents with a neck mass causing tracheal and esophageal compression, and histology shows cell nests with pink extracellular amyloid. What is the tumor’s cell of origin?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Parafollicular C cells The histopathological description of "cell nests and pink extracellular amyloid stroma" is the pathognomonic finding for Medullary Thyroid Carcinoma (MTC). The amyloid material consists of deposited calcitonin precursor proteins. MTC is a neuroendocrine tumor that arises from the parafollicular C cells of the thyroid gland. These cells are responsible for producing the hormone calcitonin. Follicular cells (D) give rise to papillary and follicular thyroid carcinomas. Hurthle cells or Askanazy cells (B, C) are eosinophilic, mitochondrion-rich follicular cells that form Hürthle cell adenomas and carcinomas. The presence of amyloid is the key feature pointing specifically to a tumor of C-cell origin.

Question 28

Which of the following is not one of the diagnostic criteria for Kawasaki disease?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Purulent conjunctivitis Kawasaki disease is an acute febrile vasculitis of childhood. The diagnosis is based on the presence of fever for at least five days plus at least four of the five principal clinical criteria. These criteria are: 1. Changes in extremities, such as erythema or edema of hands and feet. 2. Polymorphous rash. 3. Bilateral bulbar conjunctival injection, which is characteristically non-purulent (non-exudative). 4. Changes in the lips and oral cavity, such as erythema, cracked lips, or a strawberry tongue. 5. Cervical lymphadenopathy. Purulent conjunctivitis (conjunctivitis with pus discharge) is characteristic of bacterial infections and is an exclusion criterion for Kawasaki disease, not a diagnostic one. Therefore, it is the incorrect option.

Question 29

Which of the following are not considered markers of gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

NRG-1 Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumors (GISTs) are the most common mesenchymal tumors of the GI tract. The vast majority (around 80%) are driven by gain-of-function mutations in the KIT proto-oncogene (D), a receptor tyrosine kinase. A smaller subset has mutations in a related receptor, PDGFRA. Wild-type GISTs (those without KIT or PDGFRA mutations) can have mutations in other genes, including the succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) complex genes (B). PDGFRB (A) mutations are rare but have been described in GIST. In contrast, NRG1 (Neuregulin 1) fusions are oncogenic drivers found in other solid tumors, particularly lung adenocarcinoma, but they are not characteristic markers or drivers of GISTs.

Question 30

All of the following are growth-promoting proto-oncogenes except?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

TGF beta Proto-oncogenes are normal genes that, when mutated or overexpressed, can contribute to cancer formation. Many code for growth factors or their receptors. Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF, A), Platelet-Derived Growth Factor (PDGF, B), and Transforming Growth Factor-alpha (TGF-alpha, C) are all potent growth factors whose genes function as proto-oncogenes; their overexpression can drive cell proliferation. In contrast, Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-beta) has a complex, context-dependent role but is primarily considered a tumor suppressor. It inhibits the proliferation of many cell types, including epithelial cells, and promotes apoptosis. Loss-of-function mutations in the TGF-beta signaling pathway are common in many cancers, allowing cells to escape its growth-inhibitory effects. Therefore, TGF-beta is not a growth-promoting proto-oncogene.

Question 31

In a trauma patient with an unknown blood type who is unstable and needs an immediate transfusion, which blood type should be given?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

O- In an emergency trauma situation where a patient is hemodynamically unstable and requires immediate blood transfusion before blood typing and cross-matching can be completed, the universal donor blood type is used. This is O-negative (O-) blood. Group O red blood cells lack both A and B antigens, so they will not be attacked by the recipient's anti-A or anti-B antibodies. The Rh-negative designation means they also lack the RhD antigen, preventing an immune reaction in an Rh-negative recipient. While O-positive (C) blood can be given to Rh-positive recipients, it can cause sensitization in Rh-negative recipients, especially females of childbearing age. A+ (A) and AB+ (B) blood types have A and/or B antigens and cannot be given universally. Therefore, O-negative is the safest universal red blood cell product for emergency transfusion.

Question 33

Which of the following is not a feature of G6PD deficiency?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Males and females are equally affected Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency is an X-linked recessive disorder. Because it is X-linked, it primarily affects males who are hemizygous for the mutant allele. Females are usually asymptomatic carriers, although they can be affected due to lyonization (random X-chromosome inactivation). Therefore, the statement that males and females are equally affected is false. The G6PD enzyme is critical for producing NADPH (C) in red blood cells. NADPH is required to keep glutathione in its reduced state, which protects the cell from oxidative stress (B). In G6PD deficiency, exposure to oxidative stressors (like certain drugs or infections) leads to hemoglobin denaturation and precipitation within red blood cells, forming Heinz bodies (D), which ultimately causes hemolytic anemia.

Question 34

Which of the following is/are associated with an anaphylactic reaction?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Both A & C Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening, systemic Type I hypersensitivity reaction. It is caused by the widespread release of inflammatory mediators (like histamine) from mast cells and basophils. These mediators cause vasodilation and increased vascular permeability, leading to characteristic signs. Skin and mucosal involvement, such as urticaria (hives) and angioedema (swollen lips), is very common (C). Gastrointestinal symptoms like persistent abdominal cramps, nausea, and vomiting can also occur due to smooth muscle contraction and mucosal edema (A). A key cardiovascular sign is hypotension (low blood pressure) due to vasodilation and fluid leakage from the capillaries, not hypertension (B). Therefore, both persistent abdominal cramps and urticaria/swollen lips are associated with anaphylaxis.

Question 35

Which neurotransmitter is released by the SA node when blood pressure rises?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Acetylcholine This question describes the baroreceptor reflex. When blood pressure increases, baroreceptors in the aortic arch and carotid sinus increase their firing rate. These signals travel to the cardiovascular control center in the medulla oblongata. The medulla responds by increasing parasympathetic (vagal) outflow to the heart and decreasing sympathetic outflow. The parasympathetic nerve fibers, part of the vagus nerve, innervate the sinoatrial (SA) node and atrioventricular (AV) node. At the SA node, these nerve endings release the neurotransmitter acetylcholine (ACh). ACh binds to muscarinic (M2) receptors on the pacemaker cells, which increases potassium permeability, hyperpolarizes the cell, and decreases the rate of spontaneous depolarization, thereby slowing the heart rate to help lower blood pressure. Epinephrine (C) and norepinephrine (D) are sympathetic neurotransmitters that would increase heart rate.

Question 36

A 30-year-old woman who recently lost her newborn is experiencing breast engorgement from milk production, putting her at risk for milk stasis and a possible breast abscess. Which medication can be used to prevent this?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Cabergoline Lactation (breast milk production) is primarily stimulated by the hormone prolactin, which is released from the anterior pituitary gland. Prolactin secretion itself is tonically inhibited by dopamine from the hypothalamus. To suppress lactation, a dopamine agonist can be administered. Cabergoline is a potent, long-acting D2 dopamine receptor agonist that effectively suppresses prolactin secretion from the pituitary gland, thereby inhibiting milk production and relieving symptoms of engorgement. It is the drug of choice for the suppression of postpartum lactation. Metoclopramide (D) is a dopamine antagonist and can actually increase prolactin levels, causing galactorrhea. Chlorpromazine (C) is also a dopamine antagonist. Mifepristone (A) is a progesterone and glucocorticoid receptor antagonist used for medical termination of pregnancy.

Question 37

Which antidiabetic medication is linked to a higher risk of urinary tract infections?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Dapagliflozin Dapagliflozin belongs to the class of drugs known as sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors. These drugs work by blocking the reabsorption of glucose in the proximal convoluted tubules of the kidney, thereby promoting the excretion of glucose in the urine (glucosuria). While this mechanism effectively lowers blood glucose levels, the resulting high concentration of sugar in the urine creates a favorable environment for bacterial growth. This significantly increases the risk of genitourinary infections, including urinary tract infections (UTIs) and genital mycotic infections (yeast infections). Sitagliptin (B), a DPP-4 inhibitor, pioglitazone (C), a thiazolidinedione, and metformin (D) do not work by this mechanism and are not associated with a similar increased risk of UTIs.

Question 38

Which medication will not help slow the progression of kidney involvement in a 55-year- old patient with hyperglycemia and confirmed type 2 diabetes?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Hydrochlorothiazide Managing diabetic nephropathy involves both glycemic and blood pressure control. Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) (D) and ACE inhibitors are first-line agents as they reduce intraglomerular pressure and albuminuria, providing specific renal protection beyond their blood pressure-lowering effects. More recently, SGLT2 inhibitors (A) have shown significant renal benefits, slowing the progression of diabetic kidney disease. Finerenone (B) is a newer non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist that has also demonstrated a reduction in the risk of CKD progression in patients with type 2 diabetes. In contrast, hydrochlorothiazide (C), a thiazide diuretic, is used to control blood pressure but does not offer the specific intrarenal protective effects seen with ARBs, SGLT2 inhibitors, or finerenone. It is not considered a primary agent for delaying the progression of diabetic kidney disease.

Question 39

Which antidiabetic medication is known to cause unexplained diarrhea as a side effect?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Metformin Metformin is a first-line biguanide oral hypoglycemic agent for type 2 diabetes. Its most frequently reported adverse effects are gastrointestinal in nature. Diarrhea is a very common side effect, often occurring at the initiation of therapy or with dose increases. Other common GI symptoms include nausea, abdominal discomfort, and a metallic taste. The mechanism is thought to involve effects on gut motility and absorption. These side effects are often transient and can be minimized by starting with a low dose and titrating slowly or by using an extended-release formulation. Pioglitazone (A) is associated with fluid retention and weight gain. Sitagliptin (B) is generally well-tolerated. Glimepiride (C), a sulfonylurea, carries a significant risk of hypoglycemia and weight gain.

Question 40

Which antibiotic can be safely prescribed for a 45-year-old patient with enterococcal endocarditis who has a known penicillin allergy causing rash and swelling?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Vancomycin Enterococcal endocarditis requires bactericidal therapy, often with a cell wall active agent. In a patient with a significant penicillin allergy, beta-lactam antibiotics are approached with caution. Piperacillin (D) is a penicillin derivative and is contraindicated. Ceftriaxone (C), a cephalosporin, has a low but present risk of cross-reactivity. Aztreonam (A) is a monobactam with no cross-reactivity with penicillins, but it lacks activity against Gram-positive organisms like Enterococcus. Therefore, the safest and most effective alternative is Vancomycin, a glycopeptide antibiotic. It has a different mechanism of action (inhibiting cell wall synthesis by binding to D-Ala-D-Ala precursors) and has no structural similarity to beta-lactams, eliminating the risk of cross-allergenicity. It is a standard choice for serious Gram-positive infections in penicillin-allergic patients.

Question 42

Which of the following substances are antidotes for cyanide poisoning? 1. Amyl nitrite 2. Sodium nitrate 3. Sodium thiosulphate 4. Benzyl thiocyanate

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

1,2,3 The standard treatment for cyanide poisoning involves a multi-step approach using a specific kit of antidotes. The strategy is to convert hemoglobin to methemoglobin, which has a higher affinity for cyanide than cytochrome oxidase does. This is achieved by administering nitrites, such as inhaled amyl nitrite (1) followed by intravenous sodium nitrite (2). This sequesters the cyanide as cyanmethemoglobin. The second step is to enhance the body's natural detoxification pathway by providing a sulfur donor. Intravenous sodium thiosulfate (3) is administered, which allows the enzyme rhodanese to convert the cyanide from cyanmethemoglobin into the much less toxic thiocyanate, which is then excreted in the urine. Benzyl thiocyanate (4) is not part of this standard antidote regimen.

Question 44

Which of the following is not associated with Korsakoff psychosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Polyneuropathy Korsakoff psychosis is a chronic neuropsychiatric syndrome caused by thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, most commonly seen in chronic alcohol use. It primarily affects memory and cognition. The core features of Korsakoff psychosis include: Amnesia – especially severe anterograde amnesia, meaning inability to form new memories. Confabulation – patients fabricate stories to fill gaps in memory, without intent to deceive. Disorientation and cognitive impairment – sometimes loosely referred to as “psychosis” in exam contexts due to behavioral disturbances. Polyneuropathy, however, is not a defining feature of Korsakoff psychosis. Although chronic alcoholics may develop peripheral neuropathy due to nutritional deficiencies, this is a separate complication and not part of the core clinical features of Korsakoff syndrome itself. Therefore, the option not associated with Korsakoff psychosis is (B) Polyneuropathy.

Question 45

The World Medical Association’s 1970 Declaration of Oslo refers to which statement?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Therapeutic abortion The World Medical Association (WMA) issues various declarations and statements on medical ethics. The Declaration of Oslo, first adopted in 1970 and subsequently amended, specifically addresses the issue of therapeutic abortion. It provides guidance to physicians regarding their ethical duties and personal convictions when faced with a request for the termination of a pregnancy. It affirms the physician's right to conscientious objection while also emphasizing the duty to ensure the patient receives adequate medical care. The WMA's statement on torture and treatment of prisoners (B) is the Declaration of Tokyo. The primary document on ethical aspects of medical research (C) is the Declaration of Helsinki. Question 46

What is the proper sequence of examinations during a fetal autopsy to distinguish a live birth from a stillbirth?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Head > Thorax > Abdomen In a medicolegal autopsy to determine live birth versus stillbirth, a specific sequence of dissection is followed to preserve key evidence. The head is examined first to look for signs of birth trauma, such as caput succedaneum or cephalhematoma, which indicate the fetus was alive during delivery. Next, the thorax is opened to perform the hydrostatic test on the lungs. This test determines if the lungs float in water, which is a crucial sign that the infant breathed after birth. The abdominal organs are examined last. Opening the abdomen first (A, B, C) could introduce air artifacts into the thoracic cavity or damage the diaphragm, potentially compromising the results of the hydrostatic test, which is the most critical piece of evidence for establishing live birth. Therefore, the correct sequence is Head > Thorax > Abdomen.

Question 47

A 40-year-old woman ingested a white rodenticide powder and now has a potassium level of 2.5 mEq/L, an irregular pulse, muscle paralysis, and an ECG showing multiple ventricular ectopics. What poison is most likely responsible?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Barium carbonate The clinical presentation is highly suggestive of barium poisoning. Barium carbonate is a component of some rodenticides. Barium ions are potent toxins that block potassium channels, leading to a rapid and severe influx of potassium into cells from the extracellular fluid. This causes profound hypokalemia, as seen in the patient (K+ of 2.5 mEq/L). The severe hypokalemia is responsible for the characteristic symptoms of barium poisoning, including muscle weakness progressing to paralysis and significant cardiac arrhythmias, such as ventricular ectopics, due to altered membrane excitability. Phosphide-based rodenticides (A, B) cause a different toxicity profile related to phosphine gas production. Warfarin toxicity (C) causes coagulopathy and bleeding, not acute hypokalemia and paralysis. MICROBIOLOGY

Question 48

Assertion

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Assertion is true, reason is false. The assertion is true: Myocarditis is a well-known and serious complication of faucial diphtheria, caused by Corynebacterium diphtheriae. However, the reason is false. Diphtheria is primarily a toxin-mediated disease. The bacterium itself typically remains localized in the pharynx and does not cause a significant bacteremia (widespread presence of bacteria in the blood). The systemic complications, including myocarditis, polyneuropathy, and renal damage, are caused by the powerful diphtheria exotoxin produced by the bacteria at the local site of infection. This toxin is absorbed into the bloodstream and travels to distant organs like the heart, where it inhibits protein synthesis and causes cellular damage. Question 49

FISH can detect all except

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Point mutation Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) is a cytogenetic technique that uses fluorescently labeled DNA probes to visualize specific DNA sequences on chromosomes. It is highly effective for detecting large-scale chromosomal abnormalities. It can identify gene amplification (A), seen as multiple copies of a fluorescent signal for a specific gene. It can also identify deletions (B) of chromosomal regions, indicated by the absence of a fluorescent signal where one is expected. It can identify translocations (C), where a piece of one chromosome breaks off and attaches to another (e.g., using fusion or break-apart probes). However, FISH is not sensitive enough to detect very small-scale changes like point mutations (D), which involve a change in a single DNA base pair. Question 50

Match each blood product with its corresponding shelf life: a. Single donor platelet 1. 24 hours b. Packed red blood cells 2. 5 days c. Cryoprecipitate/fresh frozen plasma 3. 42 days d. Granulocyte concentrate 4. 1 year

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

a-2, b-3, c-4, d-1 The shelf life of blood products varies based on the component and storage conditions. 1. Single donor platelets (apheresis platelets) are stored at room temperature with continuous agitation to maintain viability and function, and have a short shelf life of 5 to 7 days (a -> 2). 2. Packed red blood cells stored with additive solutions like ADSOL have a shelf life of 42 days when refrigerated (b -> 3). 3. Cryoprecipitate and Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP) are stored frozen at -18°C or colder and have a long shelf life of 1 year (c -> 4). 4. Granulocyte concentrates are highly labile, stored at room temperature without agitation, and must be transfused as soon as possible, with a maximum shelf life of just 24 hours (d -> 1). The correct matching is therefore a-2, b-3, c-4, d-1.
