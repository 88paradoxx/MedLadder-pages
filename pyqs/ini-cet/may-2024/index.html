Question 1

The spinal sensory nucleus of the trigeminal nerve sends its second-order neurons to transmit which type of sensation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Pain The sensory nucleus of the trigeminal nerve in the brainstem is divided into three parts. The principal (or chief) sensory nucleus processes discriminative touch and pressure. The mesencephalic nucleus handles proprioception from the muscles of mastication. The spinal sensory nucleus is primarily responsible for processing protopathic sensations, which include pain and temperature, as well as crude touch from the ipsilateral side of the face. First-order neurons from the trigeminal ganglion synapse in the spinal nucleus. The second-order neurons then decussate (cross the midline) and ascend in the ventral two-dimensionality tract to the contralateral thalamus (VPM nucleus). From there, third-order neurons project to the somatosensory cortex. Fine touch (Option A) is primarily handled by the principal nucleus, while vibration (Option C) and proprioception (Option D) are carried by other pathways.

Question 2

The substantia nigra has functional connections with which component of the basal ganglia?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Striatum The substantia nigra, located in the midbrain, is a crucial component of the basal ganglia motor circuitry. It is most famously and extensively connected to the striatum (which consists of the caudate nucleus and the putamen). This connection forms the nigrostriatal pathway, where dopaminergic neurons from the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) project to the striatum to modulate its activity. This pathway is essential for the initiation and smooth execution of voluntary movements, and its degeneration is the primary pathology in Parkinson's disease. While the substantia nigra pars reticulata receives input from the striatum and projects to the thalamus (Option D), its most prominent functional connection is its dopaminergic input to the striatum. The pallidum (Option B) and subthalamic nucleus (Option C) are other key nodes within the basal ganglia circuit but are not the primary connection site for the substantia nigra.

Question 3

Which structure is NOT present in the floor of the inferior horn of the lateral ventricle?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Tail of the caudate nucleus The inferior (temporal) horn of the lateral ventricle extends into the temporal lobe. Its floor is a complex surface formed by several structures. These include the hippocampus (Option A), a key structure for memory formation, which creates a prominent bulge in the floor. The fimbria of the hippocampus (Option C), a band of efferent fibers, runs along its medial edge. Question 8

Which statement about cardiac muscle depolarization is incorrect?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Tetany Cardiac muscle possesses several unique physiological properties. It adheres to the all or none law (Option C), meaning a stimulus above threshold causes the entire muscle fiber to contract maximally. It also exhibits a length-tension relationship (Option D), known as the Frank-Starling mechanism, where increased initial fiber length (preload) results in a more forceful contraction. A critical feature of the cardiac action potential is its long plateau phase due to calcium influx, which results in a large refractory period (Option A). This long refractory period is essential as it prevents the summation of individual muscle twitches. Consequently, cardiac muscle cannot undergo a sustained contraction, or tetany (Option B). Tetanus is a characteristic of skeletal muscle but is physiologically impossible in cardiac muscle, ensuring the heart functions as a pump with distinct relaxation (filling) phases.

Question 9

A decrease in GFR associated with which of the following?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Increased glomerular oncotic pressure The Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) is determined by the balance of hydrostatic and oncotic pressures across the glomerular capillary wall (Starling forces). GFR is favored by glomerular hydrostatic pressure, and opposed by Bowman's space hydrostatic pressure and glomerular oncotic pressure. A decrease in GFR would be caused by factors that either reduce the forces favoring filtration or increase the forces opposing it. Increased RBF (A) and increased glomerular hydrostatic pressure (B) would both increase GFR. Decreased Bowman's oncotic pressure (C) is typically negligible and a decrease would slightly increase GFR. However, an increased glomerular oncotic pressure (D) directly opposes filtration. This occurs, for example, in dehydration or conditions with high plasma protein concentration, increasing the oncotic "pull" that holds fluid within the capillaries and thereby decreasing the GFR.

Question 10

Erythropoietin is secreted by which of the following? 1. Liver 2. Kidney 3. Muscle 4. Heart

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

1,2 Erythropoietin (EPO) is the primary hormone that stimulates the production of red blood cells (erythropoiesis) in the bone marrow. The main site of EPO production in adults is the kidney (2). Specifically, it is synthesized by peritubular interstitial cells in the renal cortex in response to tissue hypoxia. A smaller amount of EPO, approximately 10-15% of the total, is produced by hepatocytes in the liver (1). During fetal life, the liver is the primary site of EPO production. Muscle (3) and the heart (4) do not produce significant amounts of erythropoietin. Therefore, both the kidney and the liver are sites of EPO secretion, making option A the correct choice.

Question 11

Which features are characteristically seen in upper motor neuron (UMN) lesions?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

DTR increased Upper motor neuron (UMN) lesions affect the neural pathway above the anterior horn cell of the spinal cord or motor nuclei of cranial nerves. The resulting clinical signs are distinct from lower motor neuron (LMN) lesions. Key features of a UMN lesion include spasticity (velocity-dependent increase in muscle tone), weakness, and hyperreflexia, meaning deep tendon reflexes (DTR) are increased or exaggerated (C). Pathological reflexes like the Babinski sign may also be present. In contrast, atrophy (A) (specifically, severe or neurogenic atrophy), fasciculations (B) (visible muscle twitches), and flaccid paralysis are characteristic of LMN lesions. Rigidity (D), a lead-pipe or cogwheel resistance to passive movement, is a hallmark of extrapyramidal disorders like Parkinson's disease, not typically a UMN sign.

Question 12

Which hormone has the smallest influence on fetal growth during pregnancy?

Correct answer: C

Growth hormone Fetal growth is a complex process regulated by a different set of hormones compared to postnatal growth. Insulin (A) is a crucial fetal growth factor, particularly in the third trimester, promoting somatic growth and fat deposition. Thyroid hormone (B) is essential for normal development of the fetal central nervous system and skeleton. Cortisol (D) plays a vital role in the maturation of fetal organs, especially the lungs (surfactant production). Question 15

Congenital lactic acidosis results from a defect in which enzyme?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Pyruvate dehydrogenase Lactic acidosis occurs when lactate production exceeds its clearance. A major cause of congenital lactic acidosis is a deficiency in the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex. This crucial mitochondrial enzyme complex converts pyruvate, the end product of glycolysis, into acetyl-CoA for entry into the Krebs cycle. When the PDH complex is deficient, pyruvate cannot be efficiently metabolized aerobically. Instead, it is shunted into the anaerobic pathway and converted to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase, leading to a significant accumulation of lactic acid in the blood. Branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase deficiency (B) causes Maple Syrup Urine Disease. Transketolase (A) is a pentose phosphate pathway enzyme. Isocitrate dehydrogenase (C) is a Krebs cycle enzyme whose deficiency would impair the cycle but not be the primary cause of congenital lactic acidosis.

Question 16

Which of the following is not produced during the breakdown of heme?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Aminolevulinic acid Heme catabolism is the breakdown pathway for heme, primarily from senescent red blood cells. The first and rate-limiting step is catalyzed by the enzyme heme oxygenase. This enzyme opens the porphyrin ring to produce three products: biliverdin (C), a linear tetrapyrrole; ferrous iron (Fe2+) (A), which is recycled; and carbon monoxide (CO) (D), a signaling molecule that is eventually exhaled. Biliverdin is then converted to bilirubin. In contrast, aminolevulinic acid (ALA) is a key intermediate in the synthesis of heme, not a product of its breakdown. The synthesis of heme begins with the condensation of glycine and succinyl-CoA to form ALA, catalyzed by ALA synthase. Therefore, ALA is a precursor to heme, not a catabolic product.

Question 17

Which gene defect causes familial hypercholesterolemia?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

LDL receptor Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder characterized by very high levels of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol in the blood, leading to premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. The most common cause of FH, accounting for over 90% of cases, is a loss-of-function mutation in the gene encoding the LDL receptor. These receptors are located on the surface of cells, primarily hepatocytes, and are responsible for clearing LDL from the circulation. A defect in the LDL receptor leads to impaired uptake of LDL, causing its accumulation in the plasma. Mutations in ApoB-100 (the ligand for the LDL receptor) can also cause a similar phenotype, but LDL receptor defects are the primary cause. Defects in Apo E (A), Apo CII (B), and Apo B48 (D) are associated with other types of dyslipidemias.

Question 18

A male patient with hyperpigmentation and Tanner stage 5 who presents with hypertension and signs of precocious puberty is described. What underlying defect is most likely responsible for this presentation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

11-Beta hydroxylase deficiency This clinical triad—precocious puberty (virilization), hypertension, and hyperpigmentation—is the classic presentation of 11-Beta hydroxylase deficiency, a form of congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH). The enzyme deficiency blocks the conversion of 11-deoxycortisol to cortisol and deoxycorticosterone (DOC) to corticosterone. The lack of cortisol leads to a loss of negative feedback on the pituitary, causing overproduction of ACTH. Question 19

Interpret the following arterial blood gas (ABG) values: pH=7.5, pCO 2 =50 mm Hg, HCO 3- =30 mEq/L

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Metabolic alkalosis To interpret an ABG, we follow a systematic approach. First, check the pH. A pH of 7.5 is above the normal range (7.35-7.45), indicating alkalosis. Second, determine if the primary cause is respiratory or metabolic. The pCO 2 is 50 mm Hg, which is higher than the normal range (35-45 mm Hg). An elevated pCO 2 causes acidosis, which is opposite to the patient's alkalotic state, so the primary problem is not respiratory. The HCO 3- is 30 mEq/L, which is higher than the normal range (22-26 mEq/L). An elevated HCO 3- causes alkalosis. Therefore, the primary disorder is metabolic alkalosis. The elevated pCO 2 represents a respiratory compensation, where the body hypoventilates to retain CO 2 (an acid) to counteract the metabolic alkalosis.

Question 20

Which of the following is not involved in post- transcriptional modification?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Glycosylation Post-transcriptional modification refers to the processes that a primary RNA transcript (pre-mRNA) undergoes to become a mature messenger RNA (mRNA) before it can be translated into a protein. Key modifications include the addition of a 5' cap (a modified guanine nucleotide) (C), splicing to remove introns which involves endonuclease cleavage (B), and the addition of a poly-A tail to the 3' end. RNA molecules themselves can also undergo chemical modifications like methylation (A). In contrast, glycosylation (D) is a post-translational modification. It is the process of adding carbohydrate chains (glycans) to proteins or lipids, which occurs in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus after the protein has been synthesized (translated) from the mRNA template. Therefore, glycosylation is not a post-transcriptional modification.

Question 21

Which deficiency is linked to impaired sexual maturation and hypogonadism?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Zinc (Zn) Zinc (Zn) is an essential trace mineral that functions as a cofactor for hundreds of enzymes and is crucial for numerous physiological processes, including immune function, wound healing, and protein synthesis. A key role of zinc is in the function of the reproductive system and normal growth and development. Question 22

Which type of mutation leads to restoration of the wild-type phenotype when the mutant gene is suppressed?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Mutation of tRNA This question describes a suppressor mutation, where a second mutation at a different site reverses the effect of the first mutation, restoring the wild-type phenotype. One classic example of this is a nonsense suppressor, which involves a mutation of a tRNA gene. For instance, if a primary mutation changes a codon for an amino acid into a premature stop codon (a nonsense mutation), it causes truncated protein synthesis. A second mutation in the anticodon of a tRNA molecule can allow that tRNA to recognize the stop codon and insert an amino acid instead, allowing translation to continue and producing a full-length (and often functional) protein. A frameshift mutation (A) is not typically reversed this way. Adding a normal gene (B) is complementation, not suppression. Deleting the mutant gene (C) would not restore the function.

Question 24

Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) can identify all of the following except which one?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Point mutation Fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) is a cytogenetic technique that uses fluorescently labeled DNA probes to visualize specific DNA sequences on chromosomes. It is highly effective for detecting large-scale chromosomal abnormalities. It can identify translocations (A), where a piece of one chromosome breaks off and attaches to another (e.g., using fusion or break-apart probes). It can detect gene amplification (B), seen as multiple copies of a fluorescent signal for a specific gene. It can also identify deletions (C) of chromosomal regions, indicated by the absence of a fluorescent signal where one is expected. However, FISH is not sensitive enough to detect very small-scale changes like point mutations (D), which involve a change in a single DNA base pair. Detecting point mutations requires DNA sequencing or allele-specific PCR techniques.

Question 30

All of the following are growth-promoting proto-oncogenes except?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

TGF beta Proto-oncogenes are normal genes that, when mutated or overexpressed, can contribute to cancer formation. Many code for growth factors or their receptors. Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF, A), Platelet-Derived Growth Factor (PDGF, B), and Transforming Growth Factor-alpha (TGF-alpha, C) are all potent growth factors whose genes function as proto-oncogenes; their overexpression can drive cell proliferation. Therefore, TGF-beta is not a growth-promoting proto-oncogene.

Question 31

In a trauma patient with an unknown blood type who is unstable and needs an immediate transfusion, which blood type should be given?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

O- In an emergency trauma situation where a patient is hemodynamically unstable and requires immediate blood transfusion before blood typing and cross-matching can be completed, the universal donor blood type is used. Question 34

Which of the following is/are associated with an anaphylactic reaction?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Both A & C Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening, systemic Type I hypersensitivity reaction. It is caused by the widespread release of inflammatory mediators (like histamine) from mast cells and basophils. These mediators cause vasodilation and increased vascular permeability, leading to characteristic signs. Skin and mucosal involvement, such as urticaria (hives) and angioedema (swollen lips), is very common (C). Question 35

Which neurotransmitter is released by the SA node when blood pressure rises?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Acetylcholine This question describes the baroreceptor reflex. When blood pressure increases, baroreceptors in the aortic arch and carotid sinus increase their firing rate. These signals travel to the cardiovascular control center in the medulla oblongata. The medulla responds by increasing parasympathetic (vagal) outflow to the heart and decreasing sympathetic outflow.
