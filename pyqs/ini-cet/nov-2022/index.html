Question 1

Arrange the following structures in the correct order as they participate in the direct pathway of the basal ganglia : 1 Striatum 2 Globus pallidus 3 Substantia nigra 4 Thalamus 5 Cortex

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

5,1,2,3,4 The direct pathway of the basal ganglia is a crucial circuit that facilitates the initiation of voluntary movement. The sequence begins with excitatory glutamatergic signals from the cerebral cortex (5) to the striatum (1) (caudate and putamen). The striatum then sends inhibitory GABAergic projections directly to the internal segment of the globus pallidus (GPi) and the substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNr) (2 & 3). This inhibitory signal from the striatum suppresses the tonic inhibitory output of the GPi/SNr complex. The GPi/SNr normally inhibits the thalamus (4). By inhibiting the GPi/SNr, the striatum effectively disinhibits the thalamus, allowing it to send excitatory signals back to the motor cortex, thus promoting movement. Therefore, the correct sequence of information flow in the direct pathway is Cortex → Striatum → Globus Pallidus Internus/Substantia Nigra Pars Reticulata → Thalamus → Cortex. The provided option C reflects this pathway core sequence.

Question 2

Which ligament links the medial cuneiform bone to the base of the second metatarsal?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Lisfranc The Lisfranc ligament is a critical structure for maintaining the stability of the midfoot. It is a strong, short ligament that connects the medial cuneiform bone to the base of the second metatarsal. This ligament forms a key part of the Lisfranc joint complex, which comprises the articulations between the tarsal bones (cuneiforms and cuboid) and the bases of the metatarsals. Injury to this ligament can lead to significant instability and dislocation of the tarsometatarsal joint, an injury known as a Lisfranc fracture-dislocation. The spring ligament (A) supports the head of the talus and the longitudinal arch of the foot. The deltoid ligament (B) is on the medial side of the ankle, connecting the tibia to the tarsal bones. The Chopart joint (C) refers to the transverse tarsal joint, not a specific ligament connecting the described bones.

Question 4

Which pharyngeal pouch is most likely responsible for the defective development in a child who lacks the thymus and the inferior parathyroid glands?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

3 The pharyngeal pouches are endodermal outpouchings that give rise to several important structures in the head and neck. The third pharyngeal pouch has two components : a dorsal wing and a ventral wing. The dorsal wing develops into the inferior parathyroid glands, while the ventral wing develops into the thymus. Failure of the third pharyngeal pouch to develop properly leads to DiGeorge syndrome, a condition characterized by thymic aplasia (leading to T-cell deficiency and immunodeficiency) and absence of the inferior parathyroid glands (leading to hypocalcemia). The first pouch forms the middle ear cavity and Eustachian tube (A). The second pouch contributes to the palatine tonsil (B). The fourth pouch's dorsal part forms the superior parathyroid glands (D). Therefore, the concurrent absence of the thymus and inferior parathyroid glands points directly to a developmental defect of the third pharyngeal pouch.

Question 5

What is the function of the superior oblique muscle?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Intorsion, abduction, depression The superior oblique is one of the six extraocular muscles responsible for eye movement. It is uniquely innervated by the trochlear nerve (CN IV). Its tendon passes through a fibrous pulley called the trochlea and inserts on the superolateral aspect of the posterior globe. This unique anatomical course dictates its complex actions. The primary action of the superior oblique muscle is intorsion, which is the inward rotation of the top of the eye towards the nose. Its secondary action is depression (moving the eye downward), which is most effective when the eye is adducted. Its tertiary action is abduction (moving the eye outward). Therefore, the combination of intorsion, abduction, and depression correctly describes the functions of the superior oblique muscle. The other options list incorrect combinations of movements, such as extorsion, adduction, or elevation, which are not primary functions of this muscle.

Question 6

What is the typical prostate volume in adult males with normal health?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

20cc The prostate is a gland in the male reproductive system located just below the bladder. In a healthy young adult male, the prostate is roughly the size of a walnut. Its volume is typically measured in cubic centimeters (cc) or milliliters (mL), which are equivalent units. The normal prostate volume is approximately 20 cc (or 20 grams). With age, the prostate often undergoes benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), which causes an increase in its size. Volumes of 50 cc, 75 cc, or 100 cc are indicative of significant prostatic enlargement, commonly seen in older men with BPH, and can lead to lower urinary tract symptoms due to compression of the urethra. Therefore, for an adult male in normal health without pathology, 20 cc represents the typical prostate volume. This standard value is important for diagnosing and staging conditions like BPH and prostate cancer.

Question 7

Arrange the lung hilar structures in order from anterior to posterior. 1 Primary bronchus 2 Bronchial artery 3 Pulmonary vein 4 Pulmonary artery

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

3,4,1,2 The hilum of the lung is the region on the medial surface where structures enter and exit. The arrangement of these structures from anterior to posterior is a key anatomical relationship. The most anterior structure is typically the superior pulmonary vein (3). Posterior to the pulmonary vein is the pulmonary artery (4). The most posterior major structure is the primary bronchus (1). The bronchial arteries (2), which supply oxygenated blood to the lung tissue itself, are usually located on the posterior aspect of the bronchi. Therefore, the correct sequence from anterior to posterior is Pulmonary vein, Pulmonary artery, Primary bronchus, and Bronchial artery. This arrangement can be remembered with the mnemonic "V-A-B" (Vein, Artery, Bronchus) from front to back. The bronchial arteries are closely associated with the posterior surface of the bronchus, making them the most posterior elements.

Question 8

At high altitudes, which of the following physiological adaptations does not occur?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Respiratory acidosis Acclimatization to high altitudes involves several physiological changes to cope with the lower partial pressure of oxygen (hypobaric hypoxia). Hypoxia (D) is the primary stimulus for these adaptations. In response to hypoxia, the peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid bodies) are stimulated, leading to hyperventilation. This increased breathing rate blows off more carbon dioxide (), leading to a decrease in arterial . A decrease in causes respiratory alkalosis, not respiratory acidosis. Respiratory acidosis would imply retention. Hypoxia also causes pulmonary vasoconstriction (A) as a direct effect on the pulmonary arterioles, which can lead to pulmonary hypertension. For long-term adaptation, the kidneys produce more erythropoietin (EPO), which stimulates red blood cell production, resulting in polycythemia (B) to increase the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood. Therefore, respiratory acidosis is the physiological change that does not occur.

Question 9

In which parts of the body is the countercurrent mechanism found? 1 Eye 2 Testes 3 Kidney 4 Gut 5 Lungs

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

1, 2, 3 are correct A countercurrent mechanism involves two adjacent fluids flowing in opposite directions to maximize the exchange of heat, ions, or gases. The most well-known example is in the kidney (3), specifically the loop of Henle and vasa recta, which establish a medullary osmotic gradient crucial for concentrating urine. The testes (2) utilize a countercurrent heat exchange mechanism in the pampiniform plexus of veins, which cools arterial blood before it reaches the testes, maintaining a temperature optimal for spermatogenesis. The choroid plexus in the eye (1) is also thought to utilize a countercurrent mechanism to help regulate ion transport and fluid secretion. The gut and lungs do not employ a classical countercurrent exchange system for their primary functions. Therefore, the eye, testes, and kidney are the correct locations among the options provided where this mechanism is found.

Question 10

Match the following molecules with their corresponding carriers in plasma : 1. Thyroxine a. Hemopexin 2. Fatty acid b. Prealbumin 3. Hemoglobin c. Albumin 4. Heme d. Haptoglobin

Correct answer: A

1-b, 2-c, 3-d, 4-a Plasma proteins serve as carriers for various molecules. Question 11

In a pressure-volume curve, what does a reading of zero pressure imply?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Functional residual capacity The pressure-volume curve of the respiratory system plots lung volume against pressure. The pressure referred to is the transthoracic or trans-respiratory system pressure, which is the pressure difference between the airway opening (mouth) and the body surface. A reading of zero pressure signifies the point where the respiratory system is at rest, with no muscular effort being exerted. At this equilibrium point, the inward elastic recoil of the lungs is perfectly balanced by the outward elastic recoil of the chest wall. The volume of air remaining in the lungs at this specific point is the Functional Residual Capacity (FRC). It is the sum of the expiratory reserve volume (ERV) and the residual volume (RV). Residual volume (A) is the air left after maximal forced expiration. Tidal volume (B) is the air exchanged during normal breathing. Inspiratory reserve volume (D) is the extra air that can be inhaled after a normal inspiration.

Question 12

Among the given options, which organ exhibits the smallest disparity in oxygen levels between the arteries and veins?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Kidney The disparity in oxygen levels between arteries and veins is known as the arteriovenous oxygen difference ( diff). It reflects the amount of oxygen extracted and consumed by the organ's tissues. The kidney receives a very high blood flow, approximately 20-25% of the cardiac output, which is far in excess of its metabolic needs. The primary function of this high flow is filtration, not oxygen delivery. Consequently, the kidneys extract a relatively small fraction of the oxygen delivered to them, resulting in the smallest arteriovenous oxygen difference among all major organs. The brain (A) and liver (D) are metabolically active and have significant oxygen extraction. The skin (C) has a variable difference, as its blood flow is highly regulated for thermoregulation, but it is generally higher than that of the kidney.

Question 13

What is the corrected reticulocyte count when the hemoglobin (Hb) level is 5 g/dL and the absolute reticulocyte count percentage is 9%?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

3 The corrected reticulocyte count (CRC) is calculated to account for the degree of anemia, as a lower red blood cell count can falsely elevate the reticulocyte percentage. The formula for CRC is : CRC (%) = Reticulocyte count (%) × (Patient's Hemoglobin / Normal Hemoglobin) Assuming a normal hemoglobin level of 15 g/dL for calculation purposes. Given : Reticulocyte count = 9% Patient's Hb = 5 g/dL Normal Hb = 15 g/dL CRC = 9 × (5 / 15) = 9 × (1/3) = 3% A further correction, the Reticulocyte Production Index (RPI), is often calculated to account for the premature release of reticulocytes (shift cells), but based on the options, only the CRC is required. The corrected count of 3% indicates the bone marrow's response to the anemia. A value greater than 2-3% suggests an adequate marrow response (e.g., hemolysis or hemorrhage), while a value less than 2% suggests an inadequate response (hypoproliferation).

Question 14

Where is the signaling sequence located in a type 2 membrane protein that has its N-terminal facing the cytoplasm?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Inside the membrane Type II transmembrane proteins are single-pass proteins oriented with their N-terminus in the cytoplasm and their C-terminus in the extracellular space or organelle lumen. Unlike proteins with a cleavable N-terminal signal peptide (Type I proteins), Type II proteins utilize an internal, non-cleavable signal sequence. This sequence serves two functions : it acts as a signal peptide to target the ribosome-nascent chain complex to the endoplasmic reticulum, and it also functions as a transmembrane anchor. This internal signal-anchor sequence is hydrophobic and becomes embedded inside the membrane, establishing the protein's orientation. The flanking positive charges on the N-terminal side of the sequence ensure that the N-terminus remains in the cytoplasm according to the "positive-inside rule." Therefore, the signal sequence is not at the N-terminal (C) or C-terminal (B), but is an internal segment that becomes the transmembrane domain.

Question 15

For glucose uptake, which tissues rely on insulin?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Skeletal muscle and adipose tissue The uptake of glucose into cells is mediated by glucose transporter (GLUT) proteins. The expression and activity of these transporters vary among tissues. Skeletal muscle and adipose tissue express the GLUT4 transporter. The translocation of GLUT4 from intracellular vesicles to the plasma membrane is an insulin-dependent process. In the presence of insulin, GLUT4 is inserted into the cell membrane, facilitating the entry of glucose into these cells. This is a primary mechanism by which insulin lowers blood glucose levels. In contrast, the brain (C) primarily uses GLUT1 and GLUT3, the liver (B) uses GLUT2, and the kidneys (D) also use insulin-independent transporters like GLUT1 and GLUT2. These transporters are constitutively present on the cell surface and do not require insulin for glucose uptake, ensuring these vital organs have a constant glucose supply regardless of insulin levels.

Question 16

What is the typical amount of water loss through insensible means?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

50 mL/hour Insensible water loss refers to the water lost from the body through routes that are not easily perceived or measured, primarily through evaporation from the skin (not sweating) and from the respiratory tract during breathing. In a healthy, afebrile adult at rest in a temperate environment, the total insensible water loss is approximately 700-1000 mL per day. To convert this to an hourly rate : 800 mL / 24 hours ≈ 33 mL/hour. 1000 mL / 24 hours ≈ 42 mL/hour. The value of 50 mL/hour (which equates to 1200 mL/day) is a commonly used clinical estimate, especially when considering factors that might increase this loss, such as fever, hyperventilation, or low ambient humidity. The other options represent significantly higher rates of water loss : 100 mL/hr (2400 mL/day), 150 mL/hr (3600 mL/day), and 200 mL/hr (4800 mL/day), which are not typical for basal insensible loss.

Question 17

What is the correct sequence of events that occur during vomiting in a woman who feels nauseated after eating spicy food? 1 LES is open and UES is closed 2 Strong contractions in the stomach 3 Inspiration against a closed glottis 4 Relaxation of the pyloric sphincter 5 LES opens and UES opens 6 Reverse peristalsis in the small intestine (LES : Lower esophageal sphincter; UES : Upper esophageal sphincter)

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

6,4,2,3,1,5 Vomiting is a coordinated reflex controlled by the vomiting center in the medulla. First, reverse peristalsis in the small intestine (6) occurs, pushing intestinal contents back toward the stomach. Next, the pyloric sphincter relaxes (4), allowing duodenal contents to enter the stomach. Then, strong contractions of the stomach (2) take place along with contraction of abdominal muscles to increase intra-abdominal pressure. Following this, there is inspiration against a closed glottis (3). This action fixes the diaphragm and increases intra-abdominal pressure, helping propel gastric contents upward. At this stage, the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) opens while the upper esophageal sphincter (UES) remains closed (1), allowing contents to move into the esophagus but preventing immediate expulsion. Finally, both LES and UES open (5), permitting expulsion of gastric contents through the mouth. Thus, the correct physiological sequence is 6 → 4 → 2 → 3 → 1 → 5, corresponding to option (B).

Question 19

In a patient with maple syrup urine disease (MSUD), the diet should restrict all of the following amino acids except which one?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Methionine Maple Syrup Urine Disease (MSUD) is an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by a deficiency in the branched-chain alpha-ketoacid dehydrogenase (BCKAD) complex. This enzyme is essential for the catabolism of the branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) : leucine (C), isoleucine (D), and valine (B). When this enzyme is deficient, these amino acids and their toxic byproducts (alpha-ketoacids) accumulate in the blood and urine, leading to neurotoxicity and a characteristic maple syrup or burnt sugar odor in the urine. The mainstay of management for MSUD is a lifelong dietary restriction of these three branched-chain amino acids. Methionine (A) is a sulfur-containing amino acid, not a BCAA, and its metabolic pathway is different. Therefore, methionine is not restricted in the diet of a patient with MSUD.

Question 20

What is the product of deamination of methylated cytosine?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Thymine Deamination is the removal of an amine group from a molecule. In the context of DNA bases, spontaneous deamination is a common form of DNA damage. When an unmethylated cytosine undergoes deamination, it is converted into uracil (A). This is easily recognized by DNA repair enzymes (uracil DNA glycosylase) as an abnormal base in DNA and is efficiently removed. However, in many organisms, cytosine can be methylated at the 5th carbon position to form 5-methylcytosine, an important epigenetic mark. When 5-methylcytosine undergoes spontaneous deamination, it is converted into thymine. Since thymine is a normal DNA base, this C-to-T transition is not as easily recognized by repair systems, making methylated cytosine residues "hotspots" for mutation. This process is a significant source of single nucleotide polymorphisms in the genome.

Question 21

What is the cause of elevated H+ ions in the intermembrane space of mitochondria?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Increased ATP production During cellular respiration, the electron transport chain (ETC) located on the inner mitochondrial membrane actively pumps hydrogen ions (H ⁺ ) from the mitochondrial matrix into the intermembrane space. This process occurs as electrons pass through complexes I, III, and IV. The energy released from electron transfer is used to pump protons against their concentration gradient, resulting in an accumulation of H ⁺ ions in the intermembrane space. This creates a proton gradient and an electrochemical potential difference known as the proton motive force. The elevated concentration of H ⁺ ions in the intermembrane space is essential for ATP synthesis. When protons flow back into the matrix through ATP synthase, the energy released drives the conversion of ADP and inorganic phosphate into ATP. Thus, increased ATP production is associated with active electron transport and proton pumping, which leads to elevated H ⁺ concentration in the intermembrane space. Therefore, option (B) is correct.

Question 23

How does the mutation of the cystic fibrosis gene occur?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Phenylalanine at 508 position, chloride channel Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an autosomal recessive genetic disorder caused by mutations in the gene that encodes the Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane Conductance Regulator (CFTR) protein. The CFTR protein functions as a chloride channel on the surface of epithelial cells in the lungs, pancreas, sweat glands, and other organs. The most common mutation, accounting for approximately 70% of CF cases worldwide, is a deletion of three nucleotides that results in the loss of a phenylalanine amino acid at position 508 of the CFTR protein. This mutation is denoted as Δ F508. The Δ F508 mutation causes the CFTR protein to misfold, preventing its proper processing and trafficking to the cell membrane. This leads to a severe reduction in chloride ion transport, resulting in thick, sticky mucus and the characteristic multi-organ pathology of cystic fibrosis.

Question 24

Antigen binding to MHC class I activates which of the following cells?

Cytotoxic T cells Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) class I molecules are present on the surface of almost all nucleated cells. Question 25

Which statement regarding anti-CMV IgG antibodies is accurate?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

IgG avidity assay helps in differentiating past and primary infection The presence of anti-CMV (Cytomegalovirus) IgG antibodies indicates that a person has been infected with CMV at some point in their life, but it does not, by itself, distinguish between a recent (primary) infection and a past or latent infection (A, C). After a primary infection, IgG antibodies are produced and typically persist for life. To determine the timing of the infection, an IgG avidity assay is used. This differentiation is critically important, especially in managing pregnant women and transplant recipients. Statement (B) is incorrect as immunity is serotype-specific.

Question 26

In a healthy adult, what is the proportion between T cells and B cells?

Correct answer: B

3 :1 T cells (T lymphocytes) and B cells (B lymphocytes) are the two major types of lymphocytes that constitute the adaptive immune system. They are found in blood, lymph, and lymphoid organs. Question 27

Except for which of the following conditions, can thrombolysis be considered?

Correct answer: B

Blood pressure of more than 185/110 mmHg Thrombolysis with tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) is a critical treatment for acute ischemic stroke, but it has strict inclusion and exclusion criteria to minimize the risk of hemorrhagic transformation. Question 28

Which abdominal site is most commonly involved in tuberculosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Ileocecal junction Abdominal tuberculosis can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract, peritoneum, lymph nodes, or solid organs. However, the most common site of involvement within the gastrointestinal tract is the ileocecal junction. This predilection is attributed to several factors in this region : a relative abundance of lymphoid tissue (Peyer's patches), which are a primary site for mycobacterial invasion; physiological stasis, which allows for prolonged contact time between the bacilli and the mucosa; and a higher rate of fluid and electrolyte absorption. Question 32

Which of the following cytokines does not play a direct role in causing the Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Question 33

Which of the following is associated with neurogenic shock?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Hypotension + bradycardia Neurogenic shock is a form of distributive shock resulting from a severe injury to the central nervous system, typically a spinal cord injury above the T6 level. This injury disrupts the descending sympathetic pathways, leading to a loss of sympathetic tone to the blood vessels and the heart. The loss of vascular tone causes massive vasodilation, leading to a pooling of blood in the peripheral circulation and a sharp drop in systemic vascular resistance, resulting in profound hypotension. Unlike other forms of shock where hypotension triggers a compensatory tachycardia, neurogenic shock is characterized by the loss of sympathetic input to the heart. This leaves unopposed vagal (parasympathetic) stimulation, which causes bradycardia. Therefore, the classic hemodynamic profile of neurogenic shock is the unique combination of hypotension and bradycardia. Tachycardia is notably absent.

Question 34

What is the primary cause of affinity maturation of antibodies?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Somatic hypermutation Affinity maturation is the process by which B cells produce antibodies with progressively higher affinity for their specific antigen during the course of an immune response. This critical process occurs within the germinal centers of secondary lymphoid organs. The primary molecular mechanism underlying affinity maturation is somatic hypermutation (SHM). SHM involves the introduction of random point mutations into the variable region genes of the immunoglobulin (antibody) chains, primarily mediated by the enzyme activation-induced deaminase (AID). This creates a diverse pool of B cell clones with slightly different B cell receptors (antibodies). B cells that express antibodies with a higher affinity for the antigen are then preferentially selected to survive and proliferate, leading to an overall increase in the affinity of the antibodies produced. Gene rearrangements (D) create the initial antibody diversity (V(D)J recombination), but not affinity maturation.

Question 40

What is the advised length of time a woman should stop taking tamoxifen before attempting pregnancy after undergoing a mastectomy for breast cancer?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

3 months Tamoxifen is a selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM) used as adjuvant therapy for hormone receptor-positive breast cancer. It is a known teratogen and is contraindicated during pregnancy due to the risk of significant fetal abnormalities. Additionally, it has a long elimination half-life of about 5-7 days, and its active metabolites can persist even longer. To ensure that the drug and its metabolites are completely cleared from the system before conception is attempted, a "washout" period is required. The standard recommendation is to discontinue tamoxifen for at least 2 to 3 months before attempting to conceive. This allows for adequate clearance of the drug, minimizing the teratogenic risk to the fetus. Therefore, stopping and conceiving immediately is unsafe, and a 1-month period is generally considered insufficient.

Question 50

Which enzymes does Finasteride inhibit in order to treat male-pattern baldness?

Show answer and explanation
