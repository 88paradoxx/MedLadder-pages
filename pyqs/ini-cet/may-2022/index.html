Question 1

Which of the following vessels is not a branch of the facial artery within the facial region?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Mental artery The facial artery, a branch of the external carotid artery, supplies the superficial structures of the face. Its major branches in the facial region include the inferior labial artery, the superior labial artery, and the lateral nasal artery. The inferior and superior labial arteries supply the lower and upper lips, respectively, while the lateral nasal artery supplies the skin of the ala and dorsum of the nose. The mental artery, conversely, is not a direct branch of the facial artery. It is the terminal branch of the inferior alveolar artery, which itself originates from the maxillary artery. The inferior alveolar artery travels through the mandibular canal and exits onto the face through the mental foramen as the mental artery, where it supplies the skin of the chin and the lower lip. Therefore, the mental artery has a different origin and is not considered a branch of the facial artery.

Question 2

Which of the following structures does not receive innervation from the superior gluteal nerve?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Piriformis The superior gluteal nerve originates from the sacral plexus, with contributions from spinal nerve roots L4, L5, and S1. It exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, superior to the piriformis muscle. Its primary function is to provide motor innervation to three key muscles in the gluteal region: the gluteus medius, the gluteus minimus, and the tensor fasciae latae. These muscles are essential for abduction and medial rotation of the thigh and play a crucial role in stabilizing the pelvis during walking. Damage to this nerve leads to a characteristic Trendelenburg gait. The piriformis muscle, while located in the same region, receives its innervation from the nerve to piriformis, which arises directly from the ventral rami of S1 and S2. The piriformis functions as a lateral rotator of the thigh. Thus, it is the only muscle listed that is not supplied by the superior gluteal nerve.

Question 3

Which foramen in the skull is created by the union of three bones?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Foramen lacerum The foramen lacerum is a unique, irregularly shaped foramen located in the base of the middle cranial fossa. It is distinguished by being formed at the junction of three different bones: the apex of the petrous part of the temporal bone, the basilar part of the occipital bone, and the body and greater wing of the sphenoid bone. In living individuals, the foramen is largely occluded by a layer of fibrocartilage, and no major structures pass vertically through its entire length. In contrast, the other foramina listed are formed differently. The foramen magnum (B) is a large opening situated entirely within the occipital bone. The foramen rotundum (C) is a passage located completely within the sphenoid bone. The foramen jugulare (D), or jugular foramen, is formed at the suture line between just two bones: the petrous part of the temporal bone and the occipital bone.

Question 4

Which muscles together form the pelvic diaphragm? 1. Iliococcygeus 2. Pubococcygeus 3. Obturator internus 4. Deep transverse perinei

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

1,2 The pelvic diaphragm is a broad, muscular sheet that forms the floor of the pelvic cavity, providing essential support for the abdominopelvic viscera. It is composed of the levator ani and coccygeus muscles. The levator ani muscle is the major component and is itself a composite of three separate muscles: puborectalis, pubococcygeus, and iliococcygeus. Therefore, both the iliococcygeus (1) and the pubococcygeus (2) are integral parts of the pelvic diaphragm. The obturator internus (3) is a muscle located on the lateral wall of the pelvis and functions primarily as a lateral rotator of the thigh; it is not part of the pelvic floor. The deep transverse perinei muscle (4) is a component of the urogenital diaphragm (perineal membrane), which is a separate structure situated inferior to the pelvic diaphragm. Hence, only the iliococcygeus and pubococcygeus muscles from the given list contribute to the formation of the pelvic diaphragm.

Question 5

How might a fracture of the jugular canal manifest itself?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Loss of taste sensation in posterior 1/3rd of tongue The jugular foramen is an aperture in the base of the skull through which three cranial nerves—the glossopharyngeal (CN IX), vagus (CN X), and accessory (CN XI)—and the internal jugular vein pass. A fracture involving this foramen can lead to damage to these nerves, resulting in a constellation of symptoms known as jugular foramen syndrome. The glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) provides both general and special (taste) sensation to the posterior one-third of the tongue. Therefore, a lesion of CN IX would result in the loss of taste in this specific region. The sensation of taste from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue (A) is carried by the chorda tympani, a branch of the facial nerve (CN VII). General sensation from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue (C) is provided by the lingual nerve, a branch of the trigeminal nerve (CN V3). Motor innervation to the tongue is via the hypoglossal nerve (CN XII).

Question 6

Which of the subsequent options are included in the limbic system? 1. Amygdala 2. Hippocampus 3. Thalamus

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

1,2,3 The limbic system is a set of interconnected brain structures that play a major role in emotion, memory, learning, and motivation. The amygdala (1) is a core component, critically involved in processing emotions like fear and aggression and in forming emotional memories. The hippocampus (2) is also central to the limbic system, being essential for the consolidation of information from short-term to long-term memory. While the entire thalamus is not considered part of the limbic system, certain thalamic nuclei, particularly the anterior nucleus and the dorsomedial nucleus, act as crucial relay stations within limbic circuits. For example, the anterior nucleus is a key part of the Papez circuit, receiving input from the mammillary bodies and projecting to the cingulate gyrus. Because of these integral functional connections, these parts of the thalamus (3) are considered components of the broader limbic system. Therefore, all three structures listed are included.

Question 7

In Meckel's diverticulum, which ectopic tissue is most frequently encountered?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Gastric and pancreatic Meckel's diverticulum, a remnant of the vitelline duct, is the most common congenital malformation of the gastrointestinal tract. While it is often asymptomatic, it can contain ectopic (heterotopic) mucosa in about 50% of cases, which can lead to complications. The most common type of ectopic tissue found is gastric mucosa. The acid secreted by this ectopic gastric tissue can cause ulceration, bleeding, or perforation of the adjacent ileal mucosa. The second most common type of ectopic tissue is pancreatic tissue. The presence of these two tissues accounts for the vast majority of ectopic findings in Meckel's diverticulum. Other tissues, such as colonic or duodenal mucosa, can be found but are much less frequent. Ectopic thyroid or lung tissue is not associated with this condition. Therefore, the combination of gastric and pancreatic tissue is the most frequently encountered, with gastric mucosa being the most clinically significant. PHYSIOLOGY

Question 8

When a patient aspirates while lying in the supine position, which lobe of the lung is most likely to be affected?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Upper part of right inferior lobe The location of aspirated material in the lungs is determined by gravity and the patient's position. In the supine position (lying on the back), the most dependent portions of the tracheobronchial tree are the superior segments of the lower lobes and the posterior segments of the upper lobes. Due to the anatomy of the main bronchi—the right being wider and more vertical than the left—aspiration occurs more frequently into the right lung. When an individual is supine, the aspirated contents are most likely to flow into the superior (apical) segment of the right inferior lobe, as it is the most dependent segment in this position. The lingula (A) is part of the left upper lobe. The right superior (C) and middle (D) lobes are located more anteriorly and are therefore less likely to be the primary sites of aspiration in a supine patient.

Question 9

What causes the detachment of myosin from actin filaments?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Attachment of ATP to the myosin head The process of muscle contraction is governed by the sliding filament theory, involving a cycle of attachment, pivoting, and detachment of myosin heads from actin filaments. After the "power stroke," during which the myosin head pulls the actin filament, the myosin remains firmly bound to the actin in a low-energy state, known as the rigor state. The detachment of the myosin head from the actin binding site is an active process that requires the binding of a new molecule of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to the myosin head. This binding of ATP causes a conformational change in the myosin head, reducing its affinity for actin and leading to its release. Subsequently, ATP is hydrolyzed to ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi), which "re-cocks" the myosin head into a high-energy position, ready for the next contraction cycle. The dissociation of ADP and Pi (D) initiates the power stroke, it does not cause detachment.

Question 10

Which proteins facilitate the transport of iron into enterocytes?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Divalent metal transporter - 1 The absorption of dietary non-heme iron from the gut lumen into the intestinal epithelial cells (enterocytes) is a regulated process. First, dietary ferric iron (Fe 3+ ) is reduced to ferrous iron (Fe 2+ ) by an enzyme on the brush border called duodenal cytochrome B (Dcytb). The ferrous iron is then transported across the apical membrane of the enterocyte into the cell by the Divalent Metal Transporter 1 (DMT1). Therefore, DMT1 is the specific protein responsible for the initial uptake of iron into the enterocyte. Once inside the cell, iron can be stored as ferritin or transported out of the cell into the bloodstream. This exit across the basolateral membrane is mediated by another protein called ferroportin (B). Transferrin (A) is the protein that binds and transports iron within the bloodstream. Hepcidin (C) is a liver-produced hormone that regulates iron levels by downregulating ferroportin, thereby controlling iron's exit from the enterocyte.

Question 12

Which of the following is not a component of innate immunity?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

B lymphocytes The immune system consists of two main branches: innate and adaptive immunity. Innate immunity provides immediate, non-specific defense against pathogens. Its components include physical barriers, phagocytic cells (like macrophages), antimicrobial peptides such as alpha defensins (A), and the complement system, which can be activated via pathways like the lectin pathway (D). Gamma Delta T lymphocytes (C) are a type of T cell that possesses characteristics of both innate and adaptive immunity and are often considered part of the innate-like immune response. In stark contrast, B lymphocytes (B) are a cornerstone of adaptive immunity. Their primary function is to differentiate into plasma cells that produce highly specific antibodies against a particular antigen and to form memory cells that provide long-lasting immunity. This specificity and memory are the defining features of the adaptive immune system, not the innate system.

Question 15

What is the location of Macula densa located?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Junction of thick ascending limb and DCT The macula densa is a specialized plaque of modified epithelial cells in the wall of the nephron tubule. It is a key component of the juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA), which is critical for regulating blood pressure and glomerular filtration rate (GFR). The precise anatomical location of the macula densa is at the very end of the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, at the transition point where it becomes the distal convoluted tubule (DCT). In this location, the tubule comes into direct physical contact with the afferent and efferent arterioles of its own glomerulus. These specialized cells function as chemoreceptors, sensing the concentration of sodium chloride in the tubular fluid. This information is used to provide feedback to the glomerulus (tubuloglomerular feedback) to modulate GFR. This strategic placement allows for the autoregulation of individual nephron function. Question 16

Misincorporation of uracil into DNA is seen in deficiency of:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Folate DNA synthesis requires a balanced supply of all four deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates (dNTPs): dATP, dGTP, dCTP, and dTTP. The synthesis of thymidine triphosphate (dTTP) involves the conversion of deoxyuridine monophosphate (dUMP) to deoxythymidine monophosphate (dTMP) by the enzyme thymidylate synthase. This critical methylation step requires N5,N10-methylenetetrahydrofolate as a methyl group donor. This cofactor is derived from folate (vitamin B9). In folate deficiency, the pool of this cofactor is depleted, which impairs the conversion of dUMP to dTMP. This leads to an imbalance in the dNTP pool, with an accumulation of dUTP relative to dTTP. DNA polymerase can then mistakenly incorporate dUTP (uracil) into the growing DNA strand instead of dTTP (thymine). This uracil misincorporation can lead to DNA strand breaks and mutations, contributing to the megaloblastic anemia seen in folate deficiency. Vitamin B12 deficiency can indirectly affect this by trapping folate in an unusable form, but folate is the direct cofactor.

Question 18

A baby is brought to the outpatient clinic with lethargy and feeding difficulties, and the urine has a characteristic burnt-sugar smell. This condition is linked to a deficiency of which enzyme?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Branched-chain ketoacid dehydrogenase The clinical scenario described—lethargy, feeding problems, and urine that smells like burnt sugar or maple syrup in an infant—is the classic presentation of Maple Syrup Urine Disease (MSUD). MSUD is an autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism involving the catabolism of the branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs): leucine, isoleucine, and valine. The underlying enzymatic defect is a deficiency of the branched-chain α -ketoacid dehydrogenase (BCKAD) complex. This enzyme is responsible for the oxidative decarboxylation of the α -ketoacids that are formed after the initial transamination of the BCAAs. The deficiency leads to the accumulation of both the BCAAs and their corresponding toxic α -ketoacids in the blood and urine. The characteristic odor is caused by the buildup of sotolone, a compound derived from the α -ketoacid of isoleucine.

Question 19

Which G protein-coupled receptor does phospholipase C act through?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Gq G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) transduce extracellular signals into intracellular responses via heterotrimeric G proteins, which are classified based on their α -subunit. The G α subunit determines the downstream effector enzyme or ion channel. The Gq family of α -subunits (including Gq and G11) couples to the effector enzyme phospholipase C- β (PLC β ). Upon activation by a ligand-bound GPCR, the Gq protein activates PLC β . PLC β then hydrolyzes the membrane phospholipid phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP 2 ) into two important second messengers: inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP 3 ) and diacylglycerol (DAG). IP 3 triggers the release of calcium from intracellular stores, while DAG activates protein kinase C. In contrast, Gs proteins activate adenylyl cyclase, and Gi proteins inhibit adenylyl cyclase. Therefore, the phospholipase C pathway is the canonical signaling cascade for Gq-coupled receptors.

Question 20

What is the source of fructosamine formation through glycation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Albumin Fructosamine is a term used to describe glycated proteins, primarily glycated serum albumin. Glycation is a non-enzymatic reaction where a sugar molecule, such as glucose, binds to a protein. In the bloodstream, glucose reacts with the amino groups of proteins, particularly albumin, due to albumin's high concentration in the serum. This process forms a stable ketoamine product. The concentration of fructosamine in the blood reflects the average blood glucose levels over the preceding 2 to 3 weeks, which corresponds to the half-life of albumin. This makes the fructosamine test a useful tool for monitoring short- to medium-term glycemic control in diabetic patients, especially when HbA1c measurements may be unreliable (e.g., in hemoglobinopathies). Therefore, albumin is the primary protein source for the clinical measurement of fructosamine.

Question 21

Refsum disease is due to:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Absence of alpha oxidation in peroxisomes Adult Refsum disease is an autosomal recessive disorder of lipid metabolism characterized by the accumulation of phytanic acid, a branched-chain fatty acid obtained from the diet. The methyl group on the beta-carbon of phytanic acid prevents it from being metabolized by the usual beta-oxidation pathway. Instead, it must first undergo alpha-oxidation. This process occurs exclusively in peroxisomes and involves the removal of the carboxyl carbon, shortening the chain by one carbon and allowing subsequent beta-oxidation to proceed. The key enzyme for the initial step is phytanoyl-CoA hydroxylase (PAHX). In Refsum disease, a deficiency in this enzyme (or other proteins involved in the pathway) leads to the absence of alpha-oxidation in peroxisomes. This causes phytanic acid to accumulate to toxic levels in plasma and tissues, leading to neurological symptoms, retinitis pigmentosa, and anosmia.

Question 22

An increased anion gap is seen in:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Metabolic acidosis The serum anion gap (AG) is a calculated value used to help determine the cause of metabolic acidosis. It is calculated as: AG = [Na + ] - ([Cl - ] + [HCO 3 - ]). This value represents the concentration of unmeasured anions (e.g., albumin, phosphate, sulfate) in the plasma. Metabolic acidosis can be broadly divided into two types based on the anion gap. A high anion gap metabolic acidosis occurs when there is an increase in unmeasured anions due to the accumulation of acids other than hydrochloric acid. Examples include lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis, and ingestion of toxins like methanol or ethylene glycol. In these conditions, the added acid is buffered by bicarbonate (HCO 3 ), which decreases, and the unmeasured anion of the acid increases, thus widening the anion gap. In contrast, normal anion gap metabolic acidosis involves a loss of bicarbonate that is compensated by an increase in chloride, keeping the gap normal.

Question 23

How does 2,3-BPG enhance the unloading of oxygen to body tissues?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Binding to the beta subunit of Hb 2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG), also known as 2,3-diphosphoglycerate (DPG), is a negative allosteric regulator of hemoglobin's affinity for oxygen. It plays a crucial role in adapting oxygen delivery to metabolic needs. 2,3-BPG binds to a specific site in the central cavity of the deoxygenated (T, or taut) hemoglobin tetramer. This binding pocket is formed by positively charged amino acid residues on the two beta-globin chains. The binding of the negatively charged 2,3-BPG molecule stabilizes the T-state conformation of hemoglobin. This stabilization makes it energetically less favorable for hemoglobin to switch to the high-oxygen-affinity R (relaxed) state. As a result, the overall affinity of hemoglobin for oxygen is reduced, causing a rightward shift in the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve. This shift promotes the unloading and release of oxygen from hemoglobin into the peripheral tissues where oxygen tension is lower.

Question 24

Which of the following is true about phenylketonuria (PKU)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

PKU is characterized by a mousy odor in the urine due to a decrease in tyrosine, dopamine, and norepinephrine levels. Phenylketonuria (PKU) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by a deficiency in the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase. This enzyme normally converts phenylalanine into tyrosine. In its absence, phenylalanine accumulates and is metabolized into phenylketones, including phenylacetate, which is excreted in the urine and sweat, imparting a characteristic "mousy" or "musty" odor. Because the conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine is blocked, there is a resultant deficiency of tyrosine. Tyrosine is a crucial precursor for the synthesis of catecholamine neurotransmitters (dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine) and melanin. Therefore, the biochemical consequences of untreated PKU include not only the mousy odor but also a decrease in the levels of tyrosine and its downstream products, which contributes to the severe intellectual disability and hypopigmentation associated with the disease. A fruity odor is more typical of ketoacidosis. PATHOLOGY

Question 25

A woman with chronic stable angina arrives at the emergency department with marked fatigue and exhaustion, and her blood sample appears brownish in color. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Methemoglobinemia The combination of symptoms suggesting severe tissue hypoxia (fatigue, prostration, exacerbated angina) and the classic finding of "chocolate-brown" blood is pathognomonic for methemoglobinemia. In this condition, the iron atom in the heme group of hemoglobin is oxidized from the ferrous ($Fe^{2+}$) state to the ferric ($Fe^{3+}$) state. Hemoglobin in the ferric state, known as methemoglobin, is incapable of binding and transporting oxygen. This results in a functional anemia and impaired oxygen delivery to the tissues. Patients with angina may be taking nitrates, which are known oxidizing agents that can induce methemoglobinemia, providing a potential link in this case. Carboxyhemoglobinemia (A) from carbon monoxide poisoning causes a cherry-red blood color. Sideroblastic anemia (B) does not alter blood color in this way. Sulfhemoglobinemia (D) causes a greenish-black discoloration and is much rarer.

Question 26

Which of the following is not observed in anemia of chronic disease?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Low serum ferritin Anemia of chronic disease (ACD), also called anemia of inflammation, develops in the setting of chronic inflammatory conditions, such as chronic infections (C), autoimmune diseases, or malignancies. The underlying pathophysiology involves inflammatory cytokines (like IL-6) stimulating the liver to produce high levels of hepcidin (A). Hepcidin is the master regulator of iron homeostasis; high levels block iron absorption from the gut and prevent the release of iron from macrophages of the reticuloendothelial system. This leads to iron being sequestered within the body's stores. Consequently, despite having normal or even increased total body iron stores (D), the iron is not available for erythropoiesis. Serum ferritin, which reflects these iron stores and is also an acute phase reactant, is characteristically normal or elevated in ACD. A low serum ferritin is the defining feature of true iron deficiency anemia, not ACD.

Question 27

Neurosecretory bodies are seen in all the following except:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Adrenal cortical tumor Neurosecretory bodies, also known as dense-core granules, are membrane-bound vesicles containing hormones or neurotransmitters. They are the characteristic ultrastructural feature of neuroendocrine cells. Medullary thyroid carcinoma (A) is a neuroendocrine neoplasm arising from the calcitonin-producing C-cells of the thyroid. Pituitary adenomas (B) are tumors of the anterior pituitary gland, which is composed of various hormone-secreting neuroendocrine cells. Paragangliomas (C), including pheochromocytomas, are tumors arising from chromaffin cells, which are also of neuroendocrine origin. All of these tumors are expected to contain neurosecretory granules. In contrast, adrenal cortical tumors originate from the adrenal cortex, which has a mesodermal origin, not a neuroectodermal one. The adrenal cortex produces steroid hormones (e.g., cortisol, aldosterone). Steroids are synthesized and released on demand and are not stored in secretory granules. Therefore, adrenal cortical tumors lack these characteristic neurosecretory bodies.

Question 29

Which of the following conditions follows an X-linked dominant pattern of inheritance?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Rett syndrome Rett syndrome is a progressive neurodevelopmental disorder that almost exclusively affects females. It is caused by mutations in the MECP2 gene, which is located on the X chromosome. The inheritance pattern is X-linked dominant. Because the affected gene is essential for normal development, the mutation is typically lethal in males (who have only one X chromosome) either in utero or shortly after birth. Question 30

"Cell in cell" appearance is seen in which of the following?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Entosis The "cell-in-cell" structure describes the presence of one or more cells within the cytoplasm of another cell. While this can be seen when a phagocyte engulfs an apoptotic body (emperipolesis), the term is most specifically associated with entosis. Entosis is a distinct biological process where one living epithelial cell actively invades and is engulfed by a neighboring cell. The internalized "guest" cell remains alive for some time within a vacuole inside the "host" cell. The fate of the internalized cell can vary: it may be degraded, it may be released, or it may undergo cell division within the host. This phenomenon is distinct from phagocytosis of dead cells. Entosis is often observed in tumors and is thought to be a mechanism of cell competition. Necrosis (A) and necroptosis (C) are forms of lytic cell death and do not involve cell engulfment.

Question 33

What is the oncotic pressure value of normal tissue?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

25 mmHg The question is slightly ambiguous but most likely refers to the plasma colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure, which is a major determinant of fluid movement between the capillaries and the interstitial tissue. This pressure is generated by plasma proteins, primarily albumin, which are too large to pass freely across the capillary wall. Question 43

For which drug is urinary alkalinization most useful in treating toxicity?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Aspirin Urinary alkalinization is a method of enhanced elimination used for overdoses of certain drugs, particularly weak acids. The principle is "ion trapping." Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) is a weak acid (pKa ≈ 3.5). By administering intravenous sodium bicarbonate, the pH of the urine is raised (made more alkaline). In an alkaline environment, the weak acid ($HA$) is driven to dissociate into its ionized form ($H^+ + A^-$). This charged, ionized form (salicylate anion) is less lipid-soluble and cannot easily diffuse back across the renal tubular epithelium into the blood. It is thus "trapped" within the tubule and excreted in the urine, significantly increasing the drug's clearance from the body. This method is not effective for beta-blockers, acetaminophen, or calcium channel blockers, whose elimination pathways are not significantly affected by urinary pH. Acetaminophen toxicity is treated with N-acetylcysteine.
