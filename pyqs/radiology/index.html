Question 1

What constitutes the Malpigiian layer?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The Malpighian layer, also known as the germinative layer of the epidermis, refers to the actively dividing portion of the epidermis responsible for regeneration of the skin. It is composed of two layers: the stratum basale (basal layer) and the stratum spinosum (prickle cell layer).The stratum basale is the deepest layer of the epidermis. It consists of a single row of cuboidal or columnar cells that continuously undergo mitosis to produce new keratinocytes. These newly formed cells gradually move upward into the stratum spinosum.The stratum spinosum lies just above the basal layer and contains several layers of polygonal cells connected by desmosomes, giving them a spiny appearance under the microscope. These cells provide strength and flexibility to the skin.The stratum granulosum and stratum lucidum are more superficial layers and are not part of the Malpighian layer. Thus, the Malpighian layer consists of the stratum basale and stratum spinosum.

Question 2

A patient receives sevoflurane and succinylcholine for abdominal surgery and subsequently develops postoperative muscle rigidity. What is the drug of choice for management?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The patient's presentation of muscle rigidity and likely hyperthermia after receiving a volatile anesthetic (sevoflurane) and a depolarizing muscle relaxant (succinylcholine) is the classic picture of malignant hyperthermia (MH). MH is a life-threatening pharmacogenetic disorder of skeletal muscle calcium regulation. In susceptible individuals, these triggering agents cause a massive, uncontrolled release of calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, leading to sustained muscle contraction (rigidity), a hypermetabolic state, rhabdomyolysis, and a rapid rise in body temperature. The specific and life-saving antidote for malignant hyperthermia is dantrolene. Dantrolene works by binding to the ryanodine receptor (RyR1) on the sarcoplasmic reticulum, directly inhibiting the further release of calcium and thereby relaxing the muscles and controlling the hypermetabolic crisis.

Question 3

A patient develops erythema, facial flushing, and hypotension after receiving a muscle relaxant. Which drug is most likely responsible for these adverse effects?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The symptoms of erythema (redness), facial flushing, and hypotension are characteristic of histamine release. Several neuromuscular blocking agents (muscle relaxants) can cause non-immunological histamine release from mast cells. Among the options listed, atracurium is well-known for this side effect, particularly when administered rapidly or in large doses. The released histamine causes vasodilation (leading to hypotension and flushing) and can also cause bronchospasm. Cisatracurium (A) is an isomer of atracurium that was specifically developed to have a much lower propensity for causing histamine release, making it a more hemodynamically stable alternative. The aminosteroid muscle relaxants, rocuronium (C) and vecuronium (D), are also known to cause minimal to no histamine release and are generally associated with excellent cardiovascular stability.

Question 5

A patient develops rigidity, tremors, and trismus following the administration of an anesthetic agent. Which anesthetic agent is most likely responsible?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The symptoms of muscle rigidity, tremors, and trismus (lockjaw) after administration of an intravenous anesthetic are characteristic of myoclonus, which is a known side effect of etomidate. Etomidate is an IV anesthetic agent valued for its hemodynamic stability (minimal effect on blood pressure and heart rate), making it useful for induction in critically ill or trauma patients. However, its use is associated with a high incidence of excitatory phenomena, including involuntary muscle movements (myoclonus) and pain on injection. The myoclonus is thought to be caused by disinhibition of subcortical neuronal activity. These movements can be minimized by pre-treatment with opioids or benzodiazepines. Thiopentone (C) is a barbiturate, and halothane (A) is an inhaled agent; neither is typically associated with myoclonus. Nitrous oxide (B) can cause shivering but not rigidity and trismus.

Question 6

When to put patient in given position?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The image displays the recovery position. This position is used for an unconscious patient who is breathing and has a pulse, but is not suspected of having a spinal injury. The purpose of the recovery position is to maintain a clear and open airway. By placing the person on their side, it prevents their tongue from falling back and obstructing the pharynx. It also allows any fluid, such as saliva or vomit, to drain safely out of the mouth, reducing the risk of aspiration into the lungs. If a patient is unconscious and not breathing, or has no pulse (A, C, D), they require immediate cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), not placement in the recovery position.

Question 7

Murphy's eye is seen in which of the following?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The Murphy's eye is a specific design feature of most modern endotracheal tubes (ETTs). It is a small, oval-shaped hole located on the side of the ETT, opposite the beveled tip. The primary purpose of the Murphy's eye is to serve as a safety feature that ensures ventilation can continue even if the main opening at the beveled tip of the tube becomes occluded. For example, if the tip of the ETT rests against the tracheal wall or becomes blocked by mucus, respiratory gases can still flow into and out of the lungs through the Murphy's eye. This provides an alternative pathway for air movement and reduces the risk of complete airway obstruction. Laryngoscopes are used for intubation, and an LMA is a supraglottic airway device; neither has a Murphy's eye.

Question 9

Which nerve is assessed to determine the adequacy of anaesthesia?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

This question refers to the monitoring of neuromuscular blockade during general anesthesia. When neuromuscular blocking agents (muscle relaxants) are used, a peripheral nerve stimulator is employed to assess the depth of the block. The stimulator delivers a small electrical current to a peripheral nerve, and the resulting muscle contraction (twitch) is observed or measured. The most common site for this monitoring is the ulnar nerve at the wrist or elbow. Stimulation of the ulnar nerve causes contraction of the adductor pollicis muscle, which results in adduction (movement towards the palm) of the thumb. The response of the adductor pollicis to different patterns of stimulation (e.g., train-of-four) allows the anesthesiologist to precisely titrate the dose of muscle relaxant and to determine when the block has sufficiently worn off for the patient to breathe safely on their own.

Question 10

Most effective circuit in spontaneous anaesthesia is-

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The patient has suffered a cardiac arrest (unresponsive, unrecordable pulse) immediately following the administration of bupivacaine, a long-acting local anesthetic. This is the classic presentation of Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST), specifically cardiotoxicity. Bupivacaine is particularly cardiotoxic, causing refractory arrhythmias and myocardial depression. The standard management of cardiac arrest due to LAST involves immediate initiation of high-quality CPR (CPCR) and airway management. Crucially, the specific antidote is the intravenous administration of a 20% lipid emulsion solution (Intralipid). The lipid emulsion acts as a lipid sink, sequestering the lipophilic bupivacaine molecules from their sites of action in the cardiac tissue, thereby reversing the toxicity. This lipid rescue therapy is a critical and life-saving intervention for LAST-induced cardiac arrest.

Question 12

A patient undergoing surgery with halothane anesthesia develops hyperthermia and muscle rigidity. Which agent is most likely responsible for triggering this condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The patient is developing malignant hyperthermia (MH), a rare but life-threatening pharmacogenetic disorder of skeletal muscle. It is characterized by a hypermetabolic state that leads to hyperthermia, muscle rigidity, tachycardia, hypercapnia, and rhabdomyolysis. MH is triggered by exposure to specific anesthetic agents in susceptible individuals. The classic triggering agents are all volatile halogenated anesthetics (like halothane, sevoflurane) and the depolarizing neuromuscular blocker suxamethonium (succinylcholine). The other neuromuscular blockers listed—d-tubocurarine, cisatracurium, and rocuronium—are non-depolarizing agents and are considered safe as they do not trigger MH. Therefore, the combination of halothane and suxamethonium is highly likely to be responsible for this fulminant reaction. The definitive treatment for an MH crisis is the immediate administration of dantrolene.

Question 13

What percentage of oxygen is provided during mouth-to-mouth respiration?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Non re breathing face maskThe device shown is a non-re breathing face mask (NRM). Its key features are a face mask connected to an oxygen reservoir bag and a one-way valve system. A valve between the mask and the bag prevents the patient's exhaled air from entering the reservoir, while valves on the side ports of the mask allow exhaled gas to escape but prevent room air from being entrained during inhalation. This design allows the NRM to deliver a high concentration of oxygen (FiO2 of 60% to over 90%) to a spontaneously breathing patient. Question 14

Which of the following anesthetic drugs is NOT cardiodepressive?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Most intravenous anesthetic induction agents cause some degree of cardiovascular depression, typically manifesting as a decrease in blood pressure, heart rate, and myocardial contractility. Propofol and thiopentone are both significant cardiodepressants, causing vasodilation and direct myocardial depression that can lead to hypotension. Etomidate is known for its remarkable cardiovascular stability, causing minimal changes in hemodynamics. However, ketamine is unique in that it is not cardiodepressive; instead, it is a cardiovascular stimulant. Ketamine acts as a direct myocardial depressant, but this effect is completely overridden by its powerful central sympathomimetic action. It stimulates the sympathetic nervous system, leading to an increase in heart rate, blood pressure, and cardiac output. This makes ketamine a particularly useful induction agent for hemodynamically unstable patients, such as those in shock from trauma or sepsis.

Question 15

Which method is most commonly used to assess the depth of anesthesia during surgery?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

While clinical signs (heart rate, blood pressure, movement) are traditionally used to assess the depth of anesthesia, they can be unreliable. Processed electroencephalogram (EEG) monitors have been developed to provide a more objective measure of the hypnotic component of anesthesia. The most common and widely used of these is the Bispectral Index (BIS) monitor. The BIS monitor uses a sensor placed on the patient's forehead to record EEG data. It then applies a proprietary algorithm to process this complex EEG information and convert it into a single, dimensionless number ranging from 0 (complete cortical suppression) to 100 (fully awake). A BIS value between 40 and 60 is generally considered indicative of an adequate depth of general anesthesia for surgery, with a reduced risk of intraoperative awareness. Capnography monitors ventilation, not anesthetic depth.

Question 19

During strabismus (squint) surgery, the anesthesiologist notices a sudden fall in the patient’s blood pressure to 40 mmHg. What should be the immediate management?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Strabismus surgery can trigger the oculocardiac reflex, a trigemino-vagal reflex that occurs due to traction on the extraocular muscles. This reflex may cause sudden bradycardia, hypotension, arrhythmias, or even cardiac arrest. A sudden fall in blood pressure to 40 mmHg is a serious event and requires immediate intervention. The first and most important step is to stop the surgical stimulus. Asking the surgeon to immediately release traction on the eye muscles often results in rapid resolution of the reflex and stabilization of vital signs. After stopping the stimulus, the anesthesiologist can assess and manage the patient further. If bradycardia persists, atropine may be administered. However, the priority is removal of the triggering factor. Therefore, the best immediate management is to ask the surgeon to stop the surgery.

Question 20

Which anesthetic gas is known to cause the greatest degree of respiratory irritation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The modern volatile anesthetic gases vary in their pungency and their tendency to cause airway irritation. This property is important as it determines their suitability for inhalational induction of anesthesia. Desflurane is well-known for being the most pungent and irritant of the commonly used volatile anesthetics. When administered at higher concentrations, it can cause significant airway irritation, leading to coughing, breath-holding, laryngospasm, and increased salivation and respiratory secretions. Because of this high degree of pungency, desflurane is not suitable for and is generally contraindicated for inhalational induction of anesthesia, especially in children. In contrast, sevoflurane (D) is very non-pungent, has a sweet smell, and causes minimal airway irritation, making it the agent of choice for smooth inhalational inductions. Halothane (A) is also non-pungent but is rarely used now due to other side effects.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The radiograph shows a coin lodged in the esophagus. When a button battery is ingested and lodged in the esophagus, it presents a significant medical emergency due to the risk of liquefaction necrosis and perforation. On an anteroposterior (AP) X-ray, a button battery has a characteristic appearance that distinguishes it from a simple coin : the double-ring or halo sign. Question 26

A 5-year-old patient presents with acute epigastric pain and has elevated serum lipase levels. After stabilization over three days, a chest X-ray is obtained. What is the most common underlying pathology responsible for this clinical presentation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The initial presentation with acute epigastric pain and elevated lipase is diagnostic of acute pancreatitis. Acute pancreatitis is a major risk factor for developing Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS). This is a severe form of non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema and acute lung injury. The systemic inflammatory response triggered by severe pancreatitis can lead to the release of inflammatory cytokines and digestive enzymes into the circulation. These mediators damage the alveolar-capillary membrane in the lungs, causing increased permeability and leakage of protein-rich fluid into the alveolar spaces. The chest X-ray in ARDS typically shows bilateral, diffuse, fluffy alveolar infiltrates, as likely depicted in the associated image. The resulting severe hypoxemia is often refractory to standard oxygen therapy.

Question 30

A pediatric patient presented with a swelling over the head that has been present since birth (for the past two months). What is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

This question describes a head swelling in a newborn. Caput succedaneum is edema of the scalp that is present at birth, pits on pressure, and importantly, crosses suture lines. It resolves within a few days. A cephalhematoma, on the other hand, is a subperiosteal collection of blood. It appears a few hours after birth, is firm, and is strictly limited by the suture lines of the skull bone it overlies. Critically, a cephalhematoma can take several weeks to months to resolve. The history of a swelling that has been present for 2 months since birth makes caput succedaneum impossible. This prolonged duration is highly characteristic of a cephalhematoma, which may even calcify over time.

Question 31

A construction worker developed a persistent cough and shortness of breath and was later diagnosed with mesothelioma. What is the most likely underlying diagnosis or cause of this condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The development of mesothelioma, a rare and aggressive cancer of the pleura, is almost exclusively linked to prior exposure to asbestos. Construction work is a high-risk occupation for asbestos exposure. The patients respiratory symptoms (cough and shortness of breath) are likely due to the underlying interstitial lung disease caused by the inhalation of asbestos fibers, which is known as asbestosis. Asbestosis is a form of pulmonary fibrosis. Therefore, the overall diagnosis, encompassing both the benign fibrotic lung disease and the resulting malignancy, is asbestosis. While the patient also has cancer, the term asbestosis refers to the underlying pneumoconiosis that led to the development of mesothelioma.
