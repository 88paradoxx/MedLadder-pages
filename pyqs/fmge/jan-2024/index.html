Question 3

Common carotid artery pulse is taken at the level of?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The common carotid artery pulse is typically palpated in the neck, lateral to the larynx. Specifically, it is assessed at the level of the cricoid cartilage, which corresponds to the C6 vertebrae level. At this point, the artery can be compressed against the prominent carotid tubercle (Chassaignac's tubercle) of the C6 transverse process. The bifurcation of the common carotid into internal and external branches usually occurs higher, at the upper border of the thyroid cartilage (C4 level), which corresponds to the laryngeal prominence (Option B). The suprasternal notch (Option C) is too low for a reliable carotid pulse, and the angle of the mandible (Option D) is the site for the facial artery or the termination of the external carotid. Clinically, the carotid pulse is the most reliable peripheral pulse to check during cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Understanding these surface landmarks is essential for rapid clinical assessment and procedures such as carotid endarterectomy or central line placement.

Question 4

The structure shown originates from which pharyngeal arch during embryological development?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The image displays the malleus and incus, which are two of the three middle ear ossicles. Embryologically, the malleus and incus are derived from the cartilage of the first pharyngeal arch, known as Meckel's cartilage. Specifically, the dorsal end of Meckel's cartilage ossifies to form these structures. In contrast, the stapes (except for its footplate) is derived from the cartilage of the second pharyngeal arch, known as Reichert's cartilage (Option B). The third arch (Option C) gives rise to the greater horn of the hyoid and the stylopharyngeus muscle. The fourth arch (Option D) contributes to laryngeal cartilages like the thyroid and epiglottis. Understanding ossicular development is vital for diagnosing congenital hearing loss syndromes, such as Treacher Collins, where first arch derivatives are malformed. The malleus remains attached to the tympanic membrane, while the incus acts as a bridge to the stapes, facilitating sound conduction to the inner ear.

Question 5

Mention the type of joint present at the marked region.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The arrow in the X-ray identifies the first carpometacarpal (CMC) joint, which is the articulation between the trapezium and the base of the first metacarpal. This joint is functionally classified as a saddle (sellar) joint. Saddle joints feature reciprocal concave-convex surfaces that allow for a wide range of motion, including flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and uniquely, opposition. Ellipsoid joints (Option A), such as the wrist (radiocarpal) joint, allow for biaxial movement but lack the axial rotation seen in saddle joints. Condylar joints (Option B), like the knee, allow primarily for flexion and extension with limited rotation. Plane joints (Option C), found between the other carpal bones, permit only limited gliding movements. The saddle configuration of the first CMC joint is what gives the human thumb its exceptional mobility and ability to grasp objects, making it a common site for osteoarthritis due to the high mechanical loads it bears during daily activities.

Question 7

The subcostal line corresponds to which vertebrae level?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The subcostal line (or subcostal plane) is a transverse anatomical plane used to divide the abdomen into nine regions. It is defined as the horizontal line connecting the lowest points of the costal margins, which typically correspond to the 10th costal cartilages. This plane passes through the body of the L3 vertebra. It should not be confused with the transpyloric plane (Option B), which passes through the L1 vertebra and marks the pylorus of the stomach. The T12 level (Option A) corresponds to the origin of the celiac trunk and the esophageal hiatus. The L2 level (Option C) is the site where the spinal cord typically ends (conus medullaris) and the renal arteries originate. The subcostal plane at L3 serves as an important landmark for the third part of the duodenum and the lower borders of the kidneys. Understanding these vertebral levels is essential for accurate physical examination and the interpretation of cross-sectional abdominal imaging in clinical practice.

Question 8

During a modified radical neck dissection, which of the following anatomical structures is not preserved?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Neck dissections are performed to remove lymph nodes involved in head and neck cancers. A modified radical neck dissection (MRND) is defined by the removal of all lymph node levels (I-V) while preserving one or more of the non-lymphatic structures typically removed in a "radical" dissection. These structures include the Sternocleidomastoid muscle (Option A), the Internal jugular vein (Option B), and the Spinal accessory nerve (Option C). However, the submandibular gland (found in Level Ib) is routinely excised during MRND because it is intimately associated with the Level I lymph nodes, and its preservation would compromise the oncological thoroughness of the procedure. Preserving the spinal accessory nerve is particularly crucial to avoid "shoulder syndrome," characterized by pain and limited abduction. MRND aims to achieve a balance between effective cancer control and minimizing functional morbidity. Distinguishing between radical, modified radical, and selective neck dissections is a core requirement for surgical oncology and ENT clinical practice. PHYSIOLOGY

Question 11

Which of the following regulate body temperature?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The Hypothalamus serves as the body's master thermostat and primary center for thermoregulation. It contains specialized thermosensitive neurons that monitor both the core blood temperature and signals from peripheral skin receptors. The anterior (preoptic) hypothalamus primarily coordinates heat loss mechanisms, such as sweating and vasodilation, while the posterior hypothalamus coordinates heat conservation and production mechanisms, such as shivering and vasoconstriction. Basal ganglia (Option B) are involved in motor control and habit formation. The thalamus (Option C) acts as a major relay station for sensory information to the cortex. The limbic system (Option D) is primarily responsible for emotional processing and memory. Dysregulation of the hypothalamic set-point is the physiological basis of fever, where cytokines induce prostaglandin E2 production, leading the hypothalamus to perceive normal core temperatures as cold and initiating heat-generating responses. This regulatory function is critical for maintaining metabolic homeostasis within a narrow temperature range.

Question 12

In patients who are susceptible to refeeding syndrome, several electrolyte disturbances are commonly observed. Which of the following electrolyte abnormalities is NOT typically associated with refeeding syndrome?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Refeeding syndrome occurs when nutrition is reintroduced too rapidly to severely malnourished patients. The physiological hallmark is a massive release of insulin in response to carbohydrates, which causes a rapid shift of electrolytes from the extracellular space into the cells. This leads to profound Hypophosphatemia (Option D, the most common and dangerous sign), Hypokalemia (Option A), and Hypomagnesemia. Hypocalcemia (Option C) can also occur secondary to magnesium depletion. Hyperkalemia (Option B) is not associated with refeeding syndrome; instead, the problem is a life-threatening deficiency of serum potassium. These electrolyte drops can cause cardiac arrhythmias, respiratory failure, and seizures. Clinicians must identify high-risk patients (e.g., those with anorexia nervosa, chronic alcoholism, or marasmus) and "start low and go slow" with caloric intake while vigilantly monitoring and replacing electrolytes. Hyperkalemia is more typical of conditions like acute renal failure or cell lysis syndromes, which are pathophysiologically distinct from the anabolic shift seen in refeeding.

Question 13

What nerve fibers are responsible for the transmission of proprioception?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The Erlanger-Gasser classification categorizes nerve fibers based on their diameter and conduction velocity. Proprioception—the sense of self-movement and body position—is transmitted by the largest and fastest myelinated fibers, categorized as A alpha fibers (or Group Ia/Ib). These fibers carry signals from muscle spindles and Golgi tendon organs to the central nervous system with speeds up to 120 m/s. A beta fibers (Option B) are slightly smaller and slower, primarily responsible for carrying sensations of touch, pressure, and vibration. A gamma fibers (Option C) are motor fibers that innervate the contractile ends of muscle spindles to regulate their sensitivity. A delta fibers (Option D) are thin, myelinated fibers that transmit "fast" pain and cold temperature. C fibers (not listed) are unmyelinated and carry "slow" burning pain and warmth. High-speed proprioceptive feedback is essential for maintaining balance, coordinating complex motor tasks, and protecting joints from overextension during movement. BIOCHEMISTRY

Question 14

Deficiency of the enzyme tyrosine transaminase leads to the development of which of the following conditions?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Type II Tyrosinemia (also known as Richner-Hanhart syndrome) is an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by a deficiency of the enzyme tyrosine transaminase (also called tyrosine aminotransferase). This enzyme catalyzes the first step of tyrosine breakdown. Its deficiency leads to elevated levels of tyrosine in the blood and tissues, characteristically presenting with painful corneal erosions (pseudodendritic keratitis) and hyperkeratotic lesions on the palms and soles. Type I Tyrosinemia (Option A) is caused by a deficiency of fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase and is the most severe form, often leading to liver failure. Alkaptonuria (Option C) results from a deficiency of homogentisate oxidase, causing dark urine and ochronosis. Type III Tyrosinemia (Option D) is rare and caused by a deficiency of 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase. Understanding these specific enzymatic defects in the tyrosine catabolic pathway is essential for diagnosing inborn errors of metabolism and initiating targeted dietary management to prevent systemic and ocular complications.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Lactulose is a synthetic, non-absorbable disaccharide widely used as a laxative and in the management of hepatic encephalopathy. It is composed of the monosaccharides galactose and fructose. 1. Galactose + glucose (Option A) form lactose, the natural sugar found in milk. 2. Glucose + fructose (Option B) form sucrose, commonly known as table sugar. 3. Fructose + mannose (Option D) do not form a commonly occurring or clinically relevant disaccharide. Question 16

Heme is derived from which of the following amino acids?

Correct answer: B

The synthesis of heme is a complex multi-step process that begins in the mitochondria. The initial, rate-limiting step involves the condensation of succinyl-CoA (from the TCA cycle) with the amino acid Glycine. This reaction is catalyzed by the enzyme delta-aminolevulinate synthase (ALA synthase) and requires pyridoxal phosphate (Vitamin B6) as a cofactor. Question 17

Which amino acid plays a key role in the buffering capacity of blood at physiological pH?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Histidine is the primary amino acid responsible for the buffering capacity of proteins at physiological pH (around 7.4). This unique property is due to its imidazole side chain, which has a pKa of approximately 6.0. Since this pKa is relatively close to physiological pH, the side chain can easily accept or donate protons, allowing histidine-rich proteins like hemoglobin to stabilize blood pH effectively. Methionine (Option A) is a sulfur-containing amino acid primarily involved in methylation. Alanine (Option B) is a key participant in glucose-alanine cycling between muscle and liver. Arginine (Option C) is highly basic (pKa ~12) and is involved in the urea cycle and nitric oxide production. The buffering action of histidine in hemoglobin is particularly important for the Bohr effect, where the protein picks up protons released during production in the tissues, helping to regulate oxygen release and maintain acid-base balance. This makes histidine a critical component for maintaining systemic pH homeostasis.

Question 18

In what amino acid form is ammonia transported from the brain to the liver for detoxification?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Ammonia is a toxic byproduct of nitrogen metabolism, particularly dangerous to the central nervous system. In the brain, excess ammonia is detoxified by being combined with glutamate to form Glutamine, a reaction catalyzed by the enzyme glutamine synthetase. Glutamine is a non-toxic, neutral amino acid that can safely circulate in the blood to be transported to the liver (and kidneys). Once in the liver, glutamine is broken back down into glutamate and ammonia by glutaminase, and the ammonia is then converted into urea for excretion. Glutamate (Option A) itself cannot cross the blood-brain barrier effectively for transport. Alanine (Option B) is the primary form of ammonia transport from skeletal muscle to the liver (via the Cahill cycle). Glycine (Option C) is involved in various metabolic pathways but not primary systemic ammonia transport. The "glutamine shuttle" is essential for preventing hyperammonemia, which can lead to cerebral edema, encephalopathy, and potentially fatal brain damage in liver failure.

Question 19

Aromatic ring is seen in which of the following amino acids?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

In biochemistry, aromatic amino acids are those that contain a benzene-like ring in their side chains. The three primary aromatic amino acids are Phenylalanine, Tryptophan, and Tyrosine. Phenylalanine contains a simple phenyl ring. Tyrosine is derived from phenylalanine and contains a phenolic hydroxyl group. Tryptophan contains an indole ring. These amino acids are unique because they absorb ultraviolet light (typically at 280 nm), which is used by researchers to quantify protein concentration. Valine (Option A) and Lysine (Option D) are aliphatic amino acids. Cysteine and Methionine (Option C and D) are sulfur-containing amino acids. Beyond protein structure, these aromatic precursors are vital for synthesizing important neurotransmitters and hormones: phenylalanine and tyrosine lead to dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine; tryptophan leads to serotonin and melatonin. Knowledge of these structural categories is fundamental for understanding protein chemistry and the metabolic pathways of biologically active nitrogenous compounds in the human body.

Question 20

Which receptor is primarily responsible for mediating glucose uptake in skeletal muscle?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

GLUT 4 is the primary glucose transporter found in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue. It is uniquely characterized as the insulin-responsive transporter. Under resting conditions, GLUT 4 is sequestered in intracellular vesicles. When insulin binds to its receptor on the cell surface, or during physical exercise, these vesicles translocate to the plasma membrane, allowing for the rapid uptake of glucose from the blood into the muscle. GLUT 1 (Option A) provides basal glucose uptake in most tissues, including the blood-brain barrier. GLUT 2 (Option B) is a high-capacity, low-affinity transporter found in the liver, pancreas, and kidneys. GLUT 3 (Option C) is the primary transporter for neurons in the brain. The dysregulation of GLUT 4 translocation is a hallmark of insulin resistance in type 2 diabetes. Because exercise can stimulate GLUT 4 translocation independently of insulin, physical activity remains a cornerstone of managing blood sugar levels in diabetic patients.

Question 21

What is the rate-limiting enzyme of cholesterol synthesis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The synthesis of cholesterol occurs primarily in the liver. The rate-limiting and primary regulated step of this pathway is the conversion of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA (HMG-CoA) to mevalonate. This reaction is catalyzed by the enzyme HMG-CoA reductase. This enzyme is highly regulated by feedback inhibition from cholesterol levels and hormonal control (stimulated by insulin, inhibited by glucagon). 7-alpha-hydroxylase (Option B) is the rate-limiting enzyme for the synthesis of bile acids from cholesterol. Because cholesterol synthesis is most active during fasting or sleep, these medications are often taken at night. Understanding this enzyme is fundamental for managing atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and hyperlipidemia.

Question 28

Apoptotic bodies comprise of?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Apoptosis is "programmed cell death," a tidy process designed to remove unwanted cells without triggering inflammation. During apoptosis, the cell shrinks and the DNA fragments. The cell then breaks apart into several membrane-bound fragments called apoptotic bodies. These bodies comprise cytoplasm, tightly packed organelles, and fragments of the nucleus (intranuclear material). Because they are enclosed in an intact plasma membrane, the toxic cellular contents do not leak out. Options B, C, and D are incomplete descriptions of these bodies. These fragments are rapidly recognized by phagocytes (like macrophages) through "eat-me" signals such as the flip of phosphatidylserine to the outer membrane leaflet. This efficient clearance prevents the inflammatory response that typically follows necrosis. Apoptosis is a vital physiological process during embryogenesis, immune system regulation, and the removal of DNA-damaged cells. Its dysregulation can lead to autoimmune diseases or cancer, making it a central theme in cell pathology.

Question 29

Which mutation is linked to the philadelphia chromosome?

The Philadelphia chromosome (Ph) is a specific genetic abnormality associated with Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML). It results from a reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, specifically written as t(9;22)(q34;q11). In this process, a part of the ABL1 gene from chromosome 9 breaks off and attaches to the BCR gene on chromosome 22. This fusion creates the BCR-ABL1 hybrid gene, which codes for a continuously active (constitutive) tyrosine kinase protein. This unregulated kinase drives the uncontrolled proliferation of white blood cells. Inversion (Option A) and deletion (Option B) involve different types of structural chromosomal rearrangements. Insertion (Option D) involves a non-reciprocal movement of DNA. The Ph chromosome is present in over 95% of CML patients and is the specific target of tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy like imatinib. Its discovery was a landmark in oncology, proving that a specific chromosomal translocation could directly cause cancer.

A unique and lethal property of P. falciparum is cytoadherence. This occurs when the parasite expresses proteins (like PfEMP-1) on the surface of infected red blood cells (iRBCs), forming "knobs." These knobs bind to endothelial receptors (like ICAM-1 or CD36) in capillary beds. This sequestration prevents the iRBCs from passing through the spleen, where they would be cleared by macrophages. Rosetting (Option A) is the adherence of iRBCs to uninfected RBCs, which also contributes to microvascular obstruction. Agglutination (Option B) is a general term for cell clumping. Schizogony (Option D) is the asexual reproductive phase within the host. Sequestration in the brain leads to the confusion and coma seen in cerebral malaria. Understanding these mechanical properties of the parasite is critical for managing severe malaria and predicting its multisystem complications.

Question 35

Question 36

A 7-year-old boy is brought with a history of recurrent bacterial and fungal infections, mainly affecting the skin and respiratory system. He has experienced repeated episodes of abscess formation and lymphadenitis. An abnormal dihydrorhodamine (DHR) test is noted. Based on these findings, what is the most likely underlying condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The clinical picture of recurrent infections by catalase-positive organisms (like Staphylococcus aureus or Aspergillus), multiple skin abscesses, and lymphadenitis in a young boy is highly suspicious for Chronic Granulomatous Disease (CGD). Question 37

All of the following are correct about NETs except?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs) are webs of extracellular DNA fibers decorated with antimicrobial proteins (like myeloperoxidase) that neutrophils eject to trap and kill pathogens. Unlike phagocytosis (Option B), which involves internalizing a pathogen and destroying it within a vacuole, NETosis is an extracellular killing mechanism that often results in the death of the neutrophil itself. NETs are triggered during severe infections and sepsis (Option C) to control bacterial spread. However, the persistence of these extracellular DNA-protein complexes can trigger autoimmune responses, potentially resulting in SLE (Option A). The formation of NETs involves the citrullination of histones by PAD4; while arginine is the amino acid converted to citrulline, some exam banks list "Arginine needed" (Option D) as a correct feature or a slightly misworded distractor. Regardless, the fundamental contrast is that NETs represent an alternative, non-phagocytic mode of innate immune defense. Phagocytosis is the primary distractor as it represents the traditional, intracellular way neutrophils handle debris and pathogens.

Question 38

A patient presents with resting tremor, bradykinesia, and postural instability, and over time becomes bedridden, eventually dying from respiratory failure. On brain biopsy, which pathological finding is most likely to be observed?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The classic triad of resting tremors, bradykinesia (slowness of movement), and postural instability is diagnostic for Parkinson's Disease. This neurodegenerative disorder results from the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta. The characteristic pathological hallmark is the presence of Lewy bodies—eosinophilic, round, intracytoplasmic inclusions composed primarily of alpha-synuclein. Hirano bodies (Option B) are rod-shaped actin-containing inclusions seen in Alzheimer's disease. Guarneri bodies (Option C) are eosinophilic inclusions found in cells infected with smallpox. Identifying Lewy bodies is essential for post-mortem confirmation of the disease and differentiating it from other "Parkinson-plus" syndromes.

Question 39

Which of the following is associated with MEN- 1 syndrome?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 1 (MEN-1), also known as Wermer syndrome, is an autosomal dominant condition characterized by the "3 Ps" rule: tumors of the Parathyroid, Pancreas, and Pituitary. Parathyroid adenomas (causing hyperparathyroidism) are the most common feature, present in nearly 100% of affected individuals by age 40. Pancreatic tumors (like gastrinomas or insulinomas) and pituitary adenomas (like prolactinomas) complete the classic triad. Parotid tumors (Option B) are not a standard feature. Renal cell carcinoma (Option C) and Pheochromocytoma (Option D) are associated with other syndromes; specifically, pheochromocytoma is a hallmark of MEN-2A and MEN-2B (along with medullary thyroid cancer), as well as von Hippel-Lindau disease. Early recognition of MEN-1 is critical for screening family members and managing multi-organ endocrine dysfunction. Patients often present first with kidney stones or bone pain due to hypercalcemia from the parathyroid adenomas. This 3P mnemonic is essential for correctly categorizing endocrine syndromes in exams.

Question 40

All of the below are features of Conn's syndrome except?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Conn’s syndrome refers to primary hyperaldosteronism, most commonly caused by an aldosterone-secreting adrenal adenoma. Excess aldosterone acts on the distal tubules and collecting ducts of the kidney to increase sodium and water reabsorption while promoting the excretion of potassium (K⁺) and hydrogen (H⁺) ions. This hormonal effect produces the classic clinical and biochemical features of Conn’s syndrome: 1. Hypertension (Option C) due to sodium and water retention 2. Hypokalemia, leading to muscle cramps, weakness, or paralysis (Option D) 3. Metabolic alkalosis (Option A) due to increased hydrogen ion loss 4. Metabolic acidosis (Option B) is not a feature of Conn’s syndrome and is therefore the incorrect option. Typical laboratory findings include elevated aldosterone levels with suppressed renin activity. Patients often present with resistant hypertension that does not respond to standard antihypertensive therapy. Management includes surgical removal of the adenoma when appropriate or medical therapy with aldosterone antagonists such as spironolactone or eplerenone.

Question 42

Correct answer: A

Question 43

Arrange the stages of wound healing in the correct sequence?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Wound healing is a dynamic, overlapping process that follows a strictly programmed sequence to restore tissue integrity. 1. Hemostasis: Immediate vasoconstriction and platelet plug formation to stop bleeding. 2. Inflammation: Begins within minutes; neutrophils and macrophages arrive to clear debris and pathogens. 3. Proliferation: Fibroblasts migrate to the site, depositing collagen and creating granulation tissue, while new blood vessels form (angiogenesis). 4. Remodeling (Maturation): The final, longest phase where the temporary Type III collagen is replaced by stronger Type I collagen and the scar matures. Options A, B, and D provide incorrect chronological orders. Understanding this sequence is vital for wound management; for example, excessive inflammation can lead to chronic non-healing wounds, while a failure in the remodeling phase can result in hypertrophic scars or keloids. Clinicians must ensure that each phase progresses normally to achieve optimal functional and aesthetic outcomes.

Question 44

In the context of solid organ transplantation, which transplanted organ has the highest rate of rejection?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

In the field of solid organ transplantation, the small intestine is associated with the highest incidence of rejection among all listed organs. This is primarily because the intestine is an "immunologically active" organ containing a large amount of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) and a high density of donor immune cells (Graft-versus-Host risk). Furthermore, the constant exposure to a diverse luminal microbiome increases the risk of infection and inflammation, which triggers the recipient's immune system. Lungs (Option A) also have a high rejection rate compared to the heart or kidneys, but the intestine remains the most challenging. The Kidney (Option C) and Heart (Option D) have relatively lower rates of acute rejection due to established immunosuppression protocols and the lower load of lymphoid tissue. Because of this high risk, intestinal transplant recipients require extremely high doses of immunosuppressive drugs and vigilant monitoring via frequent endoscopic biopsies to detect early signs of rejection.
