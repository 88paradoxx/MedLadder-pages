Question 1

Based on the electrocardiogram (ECG) shown, what is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Hypothermia is a life-threatening condition in which the core body temperature falls below 35°C (95°F), leading to significant effects on cardiac conduction. The ECG shown demonstrates Osborn waves (J waves), which are characteristic findings in hypothermia. These appear as distinct positive deflections at the junction (J-point) between the QRS complex and ST segment, giving a typical “camel hump” appearance, especially in the precordial leads (V3–V6). In addition to Osborn waves, hypothermia can cause sinus bradycardia, prolonged PR interval, and widening of the QRS complex due to slowed electrical conduction in the myocardium. These changes are reversible with gradual rewarming of the patient. Question 3

Post-exposure Measles vaccine should be given ?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Measles is a highly contagious viral infection. For susceptible individuals who have been exposed to the virus, the measles vaccine can prevent or modify the disease if administered promptly. The recommended window for post-exposure vaccination is within 72 hours (3 days) of exposure. Administration within this timeframe provides active immunity that can overtake the natural infection's incubation period. If the 3-day window has passed, immunoglobulin (IG) can be given up to 6 days after exposure to provide passive immunity. Options A, C, and D do not match the clinically established "golden period" for post-exposure prophylaxis with the vaccine. This intervention is crucial during outbreaks to protect unimmunized contacts. Therefore, the 3-day rule is the standard epidemiological practice for post-exposure measles control.

Correct answer: A

According to the Biomedical Waste Management Rules in India, the yellow bag is used for highly infectious, non-plastic waste. Question 5

What is the method used to assess the relative risk (RR)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Relative Risk (RR) is a measure of the strength of the association between an exposure and an outcome. It is defined as the ratio of the incidence of the disease in the exposed group to the incidence in the non-exposed group. Because a cohort study begins with exposed and non-exposed individuals and follows them forward in time to observe the development of disease, it allows for the direct calculation of incidence, and thus, the Relative Risk. A Case-control study (Option A) uses the Odds Ratio (OR) because it looks backward and cannot determine true incidence. A Randomized Controlled Trial (Option C) also allows for RR calculation but is an experimental rather than observational design. A Cross-sectional study (Option D) measures prevalence rather than incidence. Therefore, the cohort study is the classic observational method for determining RR.

Therefore, because the zygomatic and mandibular bones are the primary structures affected, the first pharyngeal arch is the definitive site of abnormal embryological development.

Question 13

How does the affinity of carbon monoxide for hemoglobin compare with that of oxygen?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Hemoglobin is the primary protein in red blood cells responsible for transporting gases throughout the body. While it is designed to bind oxygen, it has a remarkably higher affinity for carbon monoxide (CO). Physiologically, the affinity of carbon monoxide for hemoglobin is approximately 210 to 250 times greater than that of oxygen. This extremely high affinity means that even at low concentrations, carbon monoxide can displace oxygen from hemoglobin binding sites to form carboxyhemoglobin, significantly reducing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity. Options A (100), B (400), and D (150) are incorrect based on standard physiological measurements. This preferential binding is the underlying mechanism of carbon monoxide poisoning, as the molecule not only blocks oxygen binding but also shifts the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve to the left, further impeding the release of oxygen to the tissues. Consequently, 250 is the most accurate representative value for this increased affinity.

Question 17

Which statement correctly explains the key features of the Michaelis–Menten enzyme kinetics curve?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The Michaelis constant (Km) is a fundamental parameter in enzyme kinetics that reflects the affinity of an enzyme for its substrate. By definition, Km is equal to the substrate concentration at which the reaction velocity is exactly half of the maximum velocity (Vmax). A critical characteristic of Km is that it is an intrinsic property of the enzyme-substrate pair; therefore, it does not change regardless of the amount of enzyme present in the system. Statements A, C, and D are incorrect because Km is defined at a specific substrate concentration but is not proportional or inversely proportional to it as a variable; rather, Km is a constant used to describe the enzyme's behavior. While Vmax increases with higher enzyme concentration, Km remains stable. Question 21

Which enzyme acts as the primary glucose sensor in pancreatic β-cells and plays a key role in regulating insulin secretion?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells is triggered by rising blood glucose levels. The critical first step in this process is the phosphorylation of glucose, which is catalyzed by hexokinase IV, also known as glucokinase. Unlike Hexokinases I, II, and III (Options A, B, and C), which have a high affinity for glucose and are inhibited by their product (glucose-6-phosphate), hexokinase IV has a low affinity and is not inhibited by its product. This low affinity allows the enzyme to act as a "glucose sensor," ensuring that its activity increases linearly as blood glucose levels rise above normal fasting ranges. This metabolic rate-limiting step determines the rate of ATP production, which ultimately leads to the closing of K+ channels and the release of insulin. Therefore, hexokinase IV is the specific enzyme uniquely suited for glucose sensing and the regulation of insulin secretion.

Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) is the enzyme complex that converts pyruvate into acetyl-CoA, linking glycolysis to the TCA cycle. Insulin is the primary anabolic hormone responsible for lowering blood glucose levels. Indian childhood cirrhosis (Option C) typically involves liver failure in older children. Kawasaki disease (Option D) is an acute vasculitis characterized by high fever and a "strawberry tongue". Therefore, the combination of a high-carbohydrate/low-protein diet and hypoalbuminemic edema confirms the diagnosis of Kwashiorkor.

Question 27

A patient presents with paresthesia of the hands and feet, generalized weakness, easy fatigability, pallor, and ataxia. Which vitamin deficiency best explains this combination of symptoms?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The clinical presentation combines hematological and neurological symptoms. Pallor and fatigue suggest anemia, while tingling (paresthesia), weakness, and ataxia (loss of coordination) indicate subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord. This specific combination is the classic manifestation of Vitamin B12 deficiency. Vitamin B12 is essential for both red blood cell production and the maintenance of the myelin sheath in the nervous system. Vitamin B1 (thiamine) deficiency (Option A) causes beriberi, which involves peripheral neuropathy or heart failure but typically not ataxia and pallor together. Vitamin B2 (riboflavin) deficiency (Option C) presents with cheilosis and glossitis. Vitamin B3 (niacin) deficiency (Option D) causes pellagra, characterized by dermatitis and diarrhea. Because B12 deficiency is unique in causing both macrocytic anemia and progressive dorsal column neurological deficits, it is the most likely diagnosis.

Question 28

A 50-year-old man presents with episodic headaches, excessive sweating, and palpitations, and imaging shows a small mass in the left adrenal gland. Which investigation would best confirm the suspected diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The triad of recurrent headaches, sweating (diaphoresis), and palpitations in the presence of an adrenal mass is highly suggestive of a pheochromocytoma. Pheochromocytoma is a catecholamine-secreting tumor that typically arises from the adrenal medulla. Vanillylmandelic acid (VMA) is a major metabolic end-product of catecholamines (epinephrine and norepinephrine) excreted in the urine. Therefore, measuring 24-hour urinary VMA levels is a standard confirmatory test for this condition. Blood or urine 5-HIAA (Option B) is used to diagnose carcinoid syndrome, which presents with flushing and diarrhea. Urine myoglobin (Option C) is a marker for rhabdomyolysis. Stool trypsin (Option D) is used to evaluate pancreatic exocrine function in cystic fibrosis. Consequently, because VMA levels directly reflect the excessive catecholamine production characteristic of pheochromocytoma, this is the most appropriate test for confirmation.

Question 29

A patient with a history of bronchospasm is found to have a tumor in the ileum along with elevated urinary 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5- HIAA) levels. What is the most likely diagnosis?

The Medical Termination of Pregnancy (Amendment) Act, 2021 (based on the 2020 bill), significantly expanded the legal window for abortion in India for certain vulnerable categories of women. Question 30

Adverse effect of Lamotrigine?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Lamotrigine is a commonly used antiepileptic drug, but it is notorious for causing dermatological side effects. A skin rash occurs in approximately 10% of patients. While many of these rashes are mild, there is a significant risk of progression to life-threatening hypersensitivity reactions, such as Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (SJS) or Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (TEN). To minimize this risk, clinicians must adhere to a slow dose titration schedule. While diplopia (Option A) can occur as a dose-related neurotoxic effect, and tremors (Option B) or neutropenia (Option C) are rare idiosyncratic reactions, the "rash" is the most characteristic and clinically important adverse effect emphasized in medical examinations. Patients are advised to stop the medication immediately and seek medical attention if any skin lesions develop during the initiation phase. Question 31

Which factor is the most likely cause of an acute asthma attack in a patient being treated for glaucoma who presents to the emergency department?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Timolol is a non-selective beta-adrenergic antagonist commonly administered as topical eye drops to treat open-angle glaucoma by reducing aqueous humor production. Despite topical administration, it can be absorbed systemically through the nasolacrimal duct into the blood. In susceptible individuals, systemic beta-blockade triggers bronchoconstriction by antagonizing receptors in the lungs, leading to an acute asthma exacerbation. Betaxolol (Option B) is a cardioselective blocker and is much less likely to cause pulmonary side effects. Latanoprost (Option C) and Bimatoprost (Option D) are prostaglandin analogs that lower intraocular pressure without affecting the adrenergic system, and they do not cause asthma. Therefore, Timolol is the classic causative agent for medication-induced asthma in glaucoma patients. To reduce this risk, patients are taught punctal occlusion to minimize systemic absorption.

Question 32

Drug causing adverse effects on the renal tubules?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Cisplatin is a potent platinum-based chemotherapeutic agent used to treat various malignancies. Its most common dose-limiting toxicity is nephrotoxicity, specifically targeting the renal tubules. Cisplatin causes acute tubular necrosis (ATN) by inducing oxidative stress and direct cellular damage, often manifesting as electrolyte wasting (magnesium/calcium) and rising creatinine. Streptozocin (Option B) also has renal toxicities but is more specifically associated with islet cell damage. Methysergide (Option C) is linked to retroperitoneal fibrosis rather than direct tubular injury. Cyclophosphamide (Option D) is classically associated with hemorrhagic cystitis (bladder toxicity) caused by its metabolite acrolein, rather than primary tubular injury. To minimize cisplatin-induced renal damage, patients are typically pre-hydrated aggressively with saline and sometimes given mannitol or amifostine. Therefore, cisplatin is the definitive drug in this list known for damaging the renal tubules.

Question 33

Which adverse effect is considered the most clinically significant with tamoxifen therapy?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Tamoxifen is a Selective Estrogen Receptor Modulator (SERM) primarily used in the treatment of ER-positive breast cancer. It acts as an estrogen antagonist in breast tissue but has an agonistic (estrogen-like) effect on the uterus. This stimulation of the uterine lining can lead to endometrial hyperplasia, which significantly increases the risk of developing endometrial cancer. Tamoxifen actually decreases the risk of cancer in the opposite breast (Option A). In postmenopausal women, tamoxifen helps prevent osteoporosis due to its bone-protective estrogenic effects (Option B). While it can affect hormonal cycles, "endometrial hyperplasia" is the most clinically significant and well-documented serious side effect requiring routine gynecological monitoring. Patients on tamoxifen are typically advised to report any abnormal vaginal bleeding immediately to rule out malignancy. Thus, the tissue-specific estrogenic action on the endometrium is its most concerning toxicity.

Question 34

A patient with known hypertension presents to the emergency department with palpitations and breathlessness and is diagnosed with hypertensive encephalopathy, with a blood pressure of 220/140 mmHg. What is the most appropriate initial drug to administer?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

This patient is in a hypertensive emergency, defined by severely elevated blood pressure (220/140 mmHg) associated with acute end-organ damage (hypertensive encephalopathy). Immediate management requires controlled reduction of blood pressure using intravenous medications. Nicardipine, a dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker, is a preferred agent due to its predictable pharmacokinetics, ease of titration, and effectiveness in hypertensive crises. Phentolamine (Option A) is specifically indicated for catecholamine-mediated crises, such as pheochromocytoma. Mannitol (Option C) is an osmotic diuretic used to lower intracranial pressure but is not a primary antihypertensive. Prazosin (Option D) is an oral alpha-blocker used for chronic management, not for the acute parenteral stabilization required in an emergency. The goal in these cases is typically to reduce the mean arterial pressure by no more than 25% within the first hour to prevent cerebral ischemia.

Question 35

Which drug has been shown to be most effective in improving survival in patients with chronic stable angina who also exhibit features of heart failure?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

For patients with chronic heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), ACE inhibitors like Lisinopril are a cornerstone of therapy. They work by blocking the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, reducing ventricular remodeling, and lowering afterload. Lisinopril has a proven mortality benefit, meaning it effectively improves the lifespan of these patients. Nifedipine (Option A) is a calcium channel blocker used for angina but can actually worsen outcomes in systolic heart failure. Digoxin (Option B) can improve symptoms and reduce hospitalizations but has never been shown to decrease overall mortality. Torsemide (Option D) is a loop diuretic essential for managing fluid overload; while it improves quality of life, it does not provide a mortality benefit like ACE inhibitors or beta-blockers. Therefore, because Lisinopril directly targets the disease process to extend life, it is the most effective choice among the options provided.

Question 36

Which drug is most effective for reducing uric acid levels to prevent renal injury in patients with tumor lysis syndrome?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS) is an oncological emergency characterized by the massive release of intracellular contents, including nucleic acids which are metabolized into uric acid. Severe hyperuricemia can cause acute urate nephropathy and renal failure. Rasburicase is a recombinant urate oxidase enzyme that directly converts uric acid into allantoin, a much more soluble metabolite that is easily excreted by the kidneys. It acts rapidly to lower existing high levels of uric acid. Febuxostat (Option D) and Allopurinol are xanthine oxidase inhibitors that prevent the formation of new uric acid but do not clear what is already in the blood. Furosemide (Option A) is a diuretic used to maintain urine flow but doesn't target uric acid. Corticosteroids (Option C) are part of chemotherapy but not a primary treatment for TLS hyperuricemia. Thus, Rasburicase is the most potent and direct agent for managing established hyperuricemia in TLS. FORENSIC MEDICINE

Question 37

In a patient presenting with a blister on the hand and axillary lymphadenopathy, which specific history-related questions should be asked based on the findings of the clinical examination?

The combination of a primary inoculating lesion (hand blister) and regional lymphadenopathy (axillary) is highly suggestive of Cat Scratch Disease (CSD). CSD is caused by the bacterium Bartonella henselae, which is typically transmitted to humans through the scratch or bite of an infected cat, particularly kittens. Asking about "exposure to cats" is the most specific and relevant history to confirm this clinical suspicion. Exposure to rats (Option A) would suggest conditions like rat-bite fever or leptospirosis, which present differently. Family history (Option C) is less relevant for this acute infectious process. A tick bite (Option D) could suggest Lyme disease or Rickettsial infections, but the classic presentation of CSD focuses on the regional nodes draining a feline-induced skin lesion. Question 38

A 23-year-old farm worker from a remote village presents 8 hours after a snake bite that occurred early in the morning. He complains of headache, blurred vision, perioral tingling, dizziness, vertigo, and has had repeated vomiting since the bite. On examination, he shows respiratory depression and bradycardia (heart rate < 50/ min). The bite site has mild swelling and blistering without bleeding. He has already received 10 vials of antivenom. What should be the next step in management for this patient?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The patient is displaying classic signs of neurotoxic snake envenomation, likely from a cobra or krait bite. Symptoms such as blurred vision (ptis), respiratory depression, and tingling are hallmark neurological effects. While Polyvalent Anti-Snake Venom (ASV) has been started, the presence of respiratory distress requires adjunct therapy. Neostigmine, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, can transiently improve neuromuscular transmission in neurotoxic envenomation (particularly cobra bites). It must always be given with Atropine to block the muscarinic side effects, such as bradycardia and increased secretions. Checking the coagulation profile (Option A) is more relevant for vasculotoxic (Viper) bites where bleeding is prominent. Epinephrine (Option C) and hydrocortisone (Option D) are used to manage ASV-induced anaphylaxis, but not for the primary neurotoxic symptoms. Therefore, the neostigmine-atropine challenge is the critical next step to potentially reverse muscle paralysis and stabilize the patient.

Question 39

An internal medicine consultant managing a patient with chronic renal disease on hemodialysis provides appropriate dietary, fluid, and treatment advice with regular monitoring. Despite this, the patient independently consumes ayurvedic medications and intermittently fails to follow medical instructions without informing the doctor, leading to clinical deterioration and respiratory distress. The patient’s attendant later files a negligence case against the doctor. Which factor would serve as a valid legal defense for the doctor in court?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Contributory negligence occurs when the patient’s own failure to exercise reasonable care for their health contributes directly to their injury or clinical deterioration. In this scenario, the doctor provided standard care, but the patient disregarded medical advice and took unauthorized supplements without informing the physician. This non-compliance is a strong defense for the doctor against a malpractice claim. Vicarious liability (Option A) refers to an employer being responsible for an employee's actions. Res Ipsa loquitur (Option B) means "the thing speaks for itself," used when negligence is obvious (e.g., a sponge left in a patient). Therapeutic misadventure (Option C) refers to an injury resulting from a recognized complication of a correctly performed procedure. Since the patient’s intentional disregard for instructions caused the worsening condition, contributory negligence is the correct legal defense.

Question 40

What is the term used to denote the number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

It is a common error to use the terms "rate" and "ratio" interchangeably in maternal health. The Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR) specifically measures the number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births. This measure reflects the obstetric risk associated with pregnancy. The Maternal Mortality Rate (Option A), in contrast, measures deaths per 1,000 women of reproductive age, reflecting the risk of death in the general female population. The Infant Mortality Rate (Option C) measures deaths of infants under 1 year per 1,000 live births. The Perinatal Mortality Rate (Option D) includes late fetal deaths and early neonatal deaths. Because the denominator "live births" is used to define the obstetric risk, the "ratio" is the correct statistical term. Understanding this distinction is vital for accurate public health reporting and international comparisons.

Question 41

Which of the following statements is not applicable or permissible during cross- examination in a court of law?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

In a legal trial, cross-examination is the stage where the opposing party’s lawyer questions the witness. A key feature of cross-examination is that leading questions ARE allowed and are frequently used to challenge the witness's credibility or to elicit specific answers. This distinguishes it from the "examination-in-chief," where leading questions are strictly prohibited. The witness is indeed examined by the defense (or opposing) lawyer (Option A). The primary objective is to weaken the opponent's case or to reveal inconsistencies in the testimony (Option C). While the judge has the authority to prevent irrelevant or repetitive questioning, there is generally no strict "time limit" fixed by law for this process (Option D). Therefore, statement B is incorrect because leading questions are a fundamental tool during cross-examination.

Question 42

A 28-year-old man presents in a disoriented state with bradycardia (heart rate 50/min), hypotension (80/60 mmHg), marked respiratory depression (7 breaths/min), hypothermia (95°F), and pinpoint pupils. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Correct answer: D

The patient presents with the classic "opioid toxidrome" triad: CNS depression (disorientation), respiratory depression (7 breaths/min), and pinpoint pupils (miosis). Other supporting clinical signs include bradycardia (50 bpm), hypotension (80/60 mmHg), and hypothermia (95 F). Cocaine intoxication (Option B) is a sympathomimetic syndrome characterized by tachycardia, hypertension, and dilated pupils (mydriasis). Question 43

A farmer presents to the emergency department with diarrhea, sweating, excessive lacrimation, and salivation. Regarding the management of this condition, which of the following statements is incorrect?

The patient’s symptoms are characteristic of a cholinergic crisis caused by organophosphate (OP) or carbamate poisoning. Question 44

A woman presents to the gynecology OPD with foul-smelling frothy vaginal discharge, severe itching, and a strawberry cervix on examination. Which statement regarding the causative organism is incorrect?

Question 45

Which of the following methods is utilized for sterilizing a glass petri dish?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Sterilization of glassware, such as petri dishes and pipettes, is most effectively achieved using dry heat in a Hot Air Oven. The standard sterilization cycle for glassware is 160 degrees Celsius for 120 minutes (2 hours). Higher temperatures like 170 or 180 degrees Celsius can be used for shorter durations. Autoclaving (Option A), which uses moist heat, is generally used for surgical instruments and media but is less ideal for dry glassware as it leaves them wet. Flash autoclaving (Option C) is an emergency method for unwrapped instruments. Option D is incorrect as 121 degrees Celsius in a hot air oven is insufficient for sterilization. Dry heat kills organisms by oxidative damage and protein coagulation. Therefore, the hot air oven at the specified temperature and time is the correct microbiological protocol for petri dishes.

A 38-year-old person with a history of intravenous drug abuse presents with malaise, fatigue, rigors, and a pan-systolic murmur best heard at the left sternal border. Based on these clinical features and risk factors, which organism is most likely to be isolated on blood culture?

Question 47

A 45-year-old patient with chronic cough, weight loss, and night sweats is suspected to have tuberculosis. A sputum sample is cultured on Löwenstein–Jensen (LJ) medium. If the organism grows, what staining property would it characteristically exhibit?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The patient's clinical symptoms (chronic cough, weight loss, night sweats) are highly suggestive of pulmonary tuberculosis caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. M. tuberculosis is characterized by its unique waxy cell wall containing high concentrations of mycolic acids. This structure makes the organism resistant to traditional Gram staining (Options A and B). Instead, it is identified using Acid-Fast staining (such as the Ziehl-Neelsen stain), where the bacteria retain the primary carbol fuchsin dye even after washing with acid-alcohol. Growth on Löwenstein-Jensen (LJ) medium, which shows rough, tough, and buff-colored colonies, confirms the diagnosis. Therefore, "acid fast" is the definitive staining characteristic for these mycobacteria. This property is essential for the rapid identification of TB in sputum samples.

Question 48

Which cellular component is exclusively present in gram-negative bacteria and not found in gram-positive organisms?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Gram-negative bacteria possess a complex cell envelope that includes an outer membrane. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), also known as endotoxin, is a major component located exclusively in the outer leaflet of this outer membrane. It is not found in gram-positive bacteria. Capsules (Option A) and Flagella (Option D) are found in both gram-positive and gram-negative species. Peptidoglycan (Option B) is a core component of the cell wall for both types, although gram-positive bacteria have a significantly thicker layer than gram-negative bacteria. LPS is clinically significant because its release into the bloodstream can trigger a massive immune response, leading to endotoxic shock and systemic inflammation. Therefore, lipopolysaccharide is the unique biochemical marker of the gram-negative cell wall.

Question 49

A patient presents to the medicine OPD with acute vomiting shortly after consuming fried rice. Based on this clinical presentation and food history, which organism is most likely responsible?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The association between consuming "fried rice" and subsequent rapid-onset vomiting is a classic clinical clue for food poisoning caused by Bacillus cereus. B. cereus is a spore-forming bacterium often found in rice. If rice is cooked and then left at room temperature, the spores can germinate and produce a heat-stable emetic toxin. Reheating (frying) the rice does not inactivate this toxin, leading to vomiting within 1 to 6 hours of ingestion. Clostridium perfringens (Option A) and the diarrheal form of B. cereus typically present with diarrhea and a longer incubation period. Staphylococcus aureus (Option C) also causes rapid-onset vomiting but is usually linked to dairy products or meats. Bacillus anthracis (Option D) causes anthrax and is not a common cause of standard food poisoning. Therefore, Bacillus cereus is the most likely pathogen given the specific rice-based history.
