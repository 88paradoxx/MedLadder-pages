Question 12

What effect does inhibition of the Na⁺/K⁺ -ATPase have on cardiac muscle function?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Inhibition of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump directly causes depolarization of cardiac muscle cells. This pump normally transports 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ into the cell per cycle, maintaining the resting membrane potential by keeping the intracellular environment relatively negative. When this pump is inhibited (as by cardiac glycosides like digoxin), Na⁺ accumulates inside the cell because it can no longer be actively expelled. This rise in intracellular Na⁺ reduces the concentration gradient that drives the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger (NCX), which normally expels Ca²⁺ using the Na⁺ gradient. As a result, intracellular Ca²⁺ also rises, and the membrane becomes less negative — i.e., depolarized — due to Na⁺ accumulation and altered ionic balance. Why other options are wrong: (A) Hyperpolarization and then depolarization is incorrect because Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase inhibition never causes an initial hyperpolarization phase. The immediate consequence is always Na⁺ accumulation leading directly to depolarization, with no preceding hyperpolarization stage involved. (C) Hyperpolarization is incorrect because hyperpolarization would require increased K⁺ efflux or Cl⁻ influx making the cell more negative. Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase inhibition does the opposite — it accumulates positive Na⁺ ions intracellularly, making the membrane potential less negative, not more. (D) Repolarization is incorrect because repolarization refers to the restoration of resting membrane potential after an action potential, driven by K⁺ efflux. Pump inhibition does not selectively activate repolarization channels; instead it disrupts the baseline ionic gradient causing sustained depolarization.

Question 13

In which intracellular structures are lysosomal enzymes normally found?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Lysosomal enzymes (hydrolases) are synthesized by ribosomes on the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) and undergo initial glycosylation within the ER lumen. They are then transported to the Golgi apparatus, where they receive the mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) tag — the critical signal that directs them to lysosomes rather than being secreted extracellularly. This entire process follows the classical secretory pathway, meaning these enzymes are always kept within membrane-bound compartments from the moment of synthesis. Why other options are wrong: (A) Cytosol is incorrect because the cytosol lacks a membrane boundary. If lysosomal enzymes were released freely into the cytosol, they would cause catastrophic self-digestion of cellular proteins. They must always remain membrane-enclosed to protect the cell. (B) Mitochondria is incorrect because mitochondria have their own distinct enzyme sets dedicated to oxidative phosphorylation and the TCA cycle. They play absolutely no role in lysosomal enzyme synthesis or storage. (D) Nucleus is incorrect because the nucleus houses genetic material and transcription machinery. Lysosomal enzymes are never trafficked there, as they exclusively follow the ER → Golgi → Lysosome secretory pathway from the point of synthesis onward.

The alveoli of the lungs are lined by two main types of epithelial cells. Type II pneumocytes (Option B) are cuboidal cells that act as the "caretakers" of the alveolus. Question 15

Which region of the digestive tract produces and secretes the enzyme enteropeptidase?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Enteropeptidase, also known as enterokinase, is a crucial enzyme for protein digestion. It is secreted by the brush border cells (enterocytes) of the duodenum. The primary role of enteropeptidase is to catalyze the conversion of the inactive pancreatic proenzyme trypsinogen into its active form, trypsin. Once trypsin is formed, it triggers a cascade of activation for other pancreatic proteases like chymotrypsin and carboxypeptidase. The stomach (Option A) secretes pepsinogen, which is activated by hydrochloric acid. The jejunum (Option C) and ileum (Option D) are primarily involved in the absorption of nutrients rather than the initiation of this specific proteolytic cascade. A deficiency in enteropeptidase can lead to severe protein malabsorption and failure to thrive. Thus, the duodenum is the critical anatomical site for the secretion of this "master switch" enzyme of protein digestion.

Question 16

Which physiological condition most strongly stimulates the release of vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Vasopressin, also known as antidiuretic hormone (ADH), is secreted by the posterior pituitary in response to two main physiological stimuli: osmotic and hemodynamic. The most sensitive trigger is a rise in plasma osmolality (not hypotonicity or hypo-osmolality as in Options A and D). However, hypotension (Option C) or a decrease in effective circulating blood volume (hypovolemia) serves as a very potent trigger for its release. When blood pressure drops significantly, baroreceptors in the heart and large arteries signal the hypothalamus to release large amounts of vasopressin. Vasopressin then acts on the V1 receptors of vascular smooth muscle to cause vasoconstriction (increasing blood pressure) and on V2 receptors in the kidneys to increase water reabsorption. Hypertension (Option B) actually inhibits vasopressin secretion to help lower blood volume. Among the choices, hypotension is the only correct hemodynamic condition that stimulates secretion.

Question 17

Which region of the brain regulates and coordinates the vomiting reflex?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The vomiting reflex is a complex process coordinated by the vomiting center located in the medulla oblongata. A critical component of this system is the Area Postrema (Option C), situated on the floor of the fourth ventricle. The Area Postrema functions as the Chemoreceptor Trigger Zone (CTZ); it lacks a functional blood-brain barrier, allowing it to detect circulating toxins, drugs, or metabolic waste products in the blood and cerebrospinal fluid. Once triggered, it sends signals to the vomiting center to initiate the emetic response. The apneustic and pneumotaxic centers (Options A and B) are located in the pons and regulate the rhythm of breathing. The Area Postrema is a primary target for antiemetic medications, making it a vital structure in clinical pharmacology and neurology.

Question 18

Which specific cells are responsible for producing and secreting renin?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Renin is a proteolytic enzyme that plays a fundamental role in the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS), which regulates blood pressure and fluid balance. It is synthesized and secreted by the juxtaglomerular (JG) cells (Option C). These specialized smooth muscle cells are located primarily in the walls of the afferent arterioles as they enter the glomerulus. JG cells function as baroreceptors that release renin in response to decreased blood pressure or sympathetic stimulation. Therefore, the JG cells are the definitive source of renin, which eventually leads to the formation of Angiotensin II and systemic vasoconstriction.

Question 20

Which of the following substances is not considered a micromineral?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Minerals are divided into macrominerals and microminerals (trace elements) based on the body's daily requirement. Macrominerals are required in amounts greater than 100 mg per day. Calcium is a macromineral (Option A); it is the most abundant mineral in the body, essential for bone structure, muscle contraction, and nerve signaling. Iodine is crucial for thyroid hormone synthesis, while selenium and copper act as essential cofactors for various enzymes. Question 21

A 36-year-old man presents with bluish-black discoloration of the sclera and pinna for the past six months and reports that his urine has turned black on standing since childhood. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The clinical features described are classic for alkaptonuria, a rare autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism. It is caused by deficiency of the enzyme homogentisate oxidase, which is involved in the degradation of the amino acids phenylalanine and tyrosine. As a result, homogentisic acid accumulates in the body. When homogentisic acid is excreted in urine and exposed to air, it oxidizes and turns the urine black—often noticed since childhood. With time, the pigment deposits in connective tissues, leading to bluish-black discoloration of the sclera and ear cartilage (pinna), a condition known as ochronosis. Phenylketonuria presents with intellectual disability and a “mousy” odor, tyrosinemia causes liver and kidney failure, and maple syrup urine disease produces urine with a burnt sugar smell. The combination of black urine on standing and ochronosis is pathognomonic of alkaptonuria.

Question 45

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) reduce gastric acid secretion by acting on which specific target?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) such as omeprazole and pantoprazole suppress gastric acid secretion by irreversibly inhibiting the H⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump, also known as the gastric proton pump. This pump is located on the apical membrane of parietal cells and represents the final common pathway for hydrochloric acid secretion in the stomach. The H⁺/K⁺ ATPase exchanges intracellular hydrogen ions (H⁺) for extracellular potassium ions (K⁺). By blocking this exchange, PPIs effectively shut down acid production regardless of the stimulus (histamine, acetylcholine, or gastrin). Why the other options are incorrect: 1. H⁺/Cl⁻ ATPase: No such pump exists. 2. Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase: Maintains membrane potential in all cells; not involved in acid secretion. 3. Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ cotransporter: Found in the loop of Henle; target of loop diuretics. Thus, H⁺/K⁺ ATPase is the specific molecular target of PPIs.

Question 46

Which drug is considered the treatment of choice for managing central diabetes insipidus?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Central Diabetes Insipidus (DI) is caused by a deficiency of antidiuretic hormone (ADH/Vasopressin) from the posterior pituitary, leading to polyuria and polydipsia. Desmopressin (dDAVP) is the preferred treatment because it is a synthetic analog of vasopressin with high selectivity for V2 receptors in the collecting ducts of the kidney. It has a longer duration of action than natural ADH and can be administered intranasally, orally, or parenterally. Insulin (Option A) is used for Diabetes Mellitus, not DI. Diazoxide (Option C) is used for hypertensive emergencies or insulinomas. Chlorpropamide (Option D) is a sulfonylurea that can sensitize the kidney to ADH in partial central DI, but it is not the first-line "preferred" treatment due to the risk of hypoglycemia. Desmopressin provides effective control of water excretion with minimal V1-mediated vasoconstrictive side effects. Therefore, Desmopressin is the gold standard for hormone replacement therapy in patients with a confirmed diagnosis of central diabetes insipidus.

Question 47

Which of the following drugs does not belong to the class of alkylating agents?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) is an antimetabolite, specifically a pyrimidine analog, not an alkylating agent. It works by inhibiting the enzyme thymidylate synthase, thereby blocking the synthesis of thymidine and disrupting DNA production. Melphalan (Option A), Cyclophosphamide (Option B), and Busulfan (Option C) are all classic alkylating agents. Alkylating agents work by attaching an alkyl group to DNA, typically at the guanine base, which leads to DNA cross-linking, strand breaks, and eventually cell apoptosis. Cyclophosphamide is a nitrogen mustard, Melphalan is used for multiple myeloma, and Busulfan is often used for chronic myeloid leukemia. While both antimetabolites and alkylating agents are used in cancer chemotherapy, they have distinct biochemical mechanisms and belong to different pharmacological classes. Because 5-FU interferes with metabolic pathways rather than directly alkylating DNA, it is the correct answer to this "not" question.

Metabolism by acetylation is a Phase II reaction catalyzed by the enzyme N-acetyltransferase (NAT). Question 50
