Question 2

In a newborn admitted to the NICU with respiratory distress and passage of meconium from the umbilicus, which anatomical structure is responsible for these findings?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The clinical presentation of meconium or fecal discharge from the umbilicus in a newborn is pathognomonic for a vitelline fistula. This condition results from a complete failure of the vitelline (omphalomesenteric) duct to obliterate during embryological development. Normally, this duct connects the midgut to the yolk sac and closes by the eighth week of gestation. Persistence of the entire duct creates a direct communication between the ileum and the umbilicus, allowing intestinal contents (meconium) to leak out. The mesonephric duct (Option A) is involved in the development of the male reproductive system. The allantois (Option B) and its remnant, the urachus (Option C), connect the bladder to the umbilicus; their persistence leads to a urachal fistula, characterized by the discharge of urine, not meconium. Therefore, the specific nature of the discharge (fecal/meconium) definitively identifies the vitelline fistula as the responsible anatomical abnormality in this infant.

Question 3

What is the anatomical term for the vertical plane that divides the body into anterior and posterior parts?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The human body is divided by standard anatomical planes to describe the orientation and location of structures. The coronal plane (Option A), also known as the frontal plane, is a vertical plane that passes through the body from side to side, effectively separating it into anterior (front) and posterior (back) sections. This plane is perpendicular to both the median and transverse planes. The transverse or axial plane (Options B and C) is a horizontal plane that divides the body into superior (upper) and inferior (lower) portions. The median or midsagittal plane (Option D) is a vertical plane that passes directly through the midline, dividing the body into equal right and left halves. Understanding these planes is fundamental for clinical examinations, surgical planning, and interpreting radiological imaging like CT and MRI. Therefore, based on the definition of dividing the body into front and back sections, the coronal plane is the correct term.

Question 4

Which type of epithelium lines the inner surface of the fallopian tube?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The fallopian tubes (uterine tubes) are lined with a specialized mucosa designed to facilitate the transport of gametes and the early embryo. This lining consists of simple ciliated columnar epithelium. These ciliated cells work in coordination with secretory (peg) cells; the cilia beat rhythmically toward the uterus to help move the secondary oocyte and subsequently the zygote. Simple columnar epithelium without cilia (Option A) is typical of the gastrointestinal tract. Pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium (Option B) is the "respiratory epithelium" found in the trachea and large bronchi. Cuboidal epithelium with microvilli (Option D) is characteristic of the proximal convoluted tubules in the kidney. The presence of cilia is the defining feature of the fallopian tube epithelium required for reproductive function. Therefore, because these cells are single-layered, tall, and possess active cilia, ciliated columnar epithelium is the accurate histological classification.

Question 5

What is the function of the biceps brachii muscle during flexion of the forearm?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

When a muscle provides the majority of the force required to perform a specific movement, it is classified as the primary mover, or agonist. In the act of elbow flexion (lifting the forearm), the biceps brachii serves as the primary mover. It contracts concentrically to pull the radius toward the humerus, effectively bending the elbow joint. A synergistic muscle (Option A), such as the brachialis, assists the primary mover in its action. A fixator muscle (Option B) stabilizes the origin of the primary mover, such as the rotator cuff muscles stabilizing the shoulder during arm movement. An antagonist muscle (Option C), in this case the triceps brachii, must relax and yield to the movement of the primary mover. Because the biceps brachii is the dominant muscle responsible for the deliberate lifting of the forearm, its role is definitively defined as the primary mover.

Question 6

Which anatomical structure is primarily responsible for preventing lateral displacement of the patella during knee flexion?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The patella has a natural tendency to dislocate laterally due to the "Q-angle" formed by the pull of the quadriceps muscles. The vastus medialis, particularly its distal horizontal fibers known as the vastus medialis obliquus (VMO), is the primary dynamic stabilizer that prevents this lateral displacement. It exerts a medial pull that keeps the patella centered within the trochlear groove of the femur during knee flexion and extension. The patellar ligament (Option B) connects the patella to the tibial tuberosity but does not provide specific medial-lateral stabilization. The gracilis (Option C) is an adductor muscle of the thigh and does not act directly on the patella. The rectus femoris (Option D) is a central quadriceps muscle that actually contributes to the lateral-pulling force on the patella. Therefore, strengthening the vastus medialis is a standard therapeutic goal for patients with patellofemoral pain syndrome or recurrent dislocations. PHYSIOLOGY

Which of the following conditions leads to a leftward shift of the oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve describes the relationship between oxygen partial pressure and hemoglobin saturation. A leftward shift indicates an increased affinity of hemoglobin for oxygen, meaning oxygen is bound more tightly and released less easily to tissues. Alkalosis (Option C), which is an increase in blood pH, is a classic cause of this leftward shift. Hypoxia (Option A) does not directly shift the curve but triggers physiological responses. Hypercarbia (increased ) (Option B), an increase in temperature, and an increase in 2,3-diphosphoglycerate (2,3-DPG) (Option D) all cause a rightward shift in the curve, decreasing oxygen affinity to facilitate delivery to tissues during high metabolic demand. This rightward movement is known as the Bohr effect. Because alkalosis increases the attraction between hemoglobin and oxygen, it is the only factor listed that definitively causes a leftward shift in the curve. BIOCHEMISTRY

Question 9

Menkes disease (kinky hair syndrome) results from a defect in the metabolism of which substance?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Menkes disease is an X-linked recessive disorder caused by a mutation in the ATP7A gene, which encodes a copper-transporting protein. This mutation results in impaired copper absorption from the intestines and its defective distribution to various tissues. Consequently, patients suffer from severe copper deficiency, which impacts the function of essential copper-dependent enzymes like lysyl oxidase and tyrosinase. Clinical hallmarks include sparse, steely, or "kinky" hair, failure to thrive, and progressive neurological deterioration. Disturbance in zinc metabolism (Option A) leads to acrodermatitis enteropathica. Iron metabolism defects (Option C) cause conditions like hemochromatosis or sideroblastic anemia. Magnesium deficiency (Option D) is typically an electrolyte imbalance rather than a primary genetic metabolic syndrome. Because the underlying pathology of Menkes syndrome is specifically the inability of cells to transport copper effectively, copper is the responsible metabolic element.

Question 10

Classical galactosemia is caused by a deficiency of the enzyme:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Classical galactosemia is an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder characterized by the inability to metabolize galactose, a sugar found in milk. The "classical" form of the disease is specifically caused by a deficiency of the enzyme galactose-1-phosphate uridyl transferase (GALT). This deficiency leads to the toxic accumulation of galactose-1-phosphate in various organs, including the liver and brain. Aldose reductase (Option A) converts excess galactose into galactitol, which contributes to cataract formation in untreated patients. Galactokinase deficiency (Option B) causes a milder form of the disease primarily characterized by early cataracts. UDP galactose-4-epimerase deficiency (Option D) is an extremely rare variant. Classical GALT deficiency presents in infancy with jaundice, hepatomegaly, failure to thrive, and sepsis if milk is not removed from the diet. Therefore, GALT is the definitive enzyme lacking in the classical presentation of this syndrome.

Question 11

Which of the following options is not a physiological function of glucagon?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Glucagon is a catabolic hormone secreted by the pancreatic alpha cells in response to low blood glucose levels. Its primary goal is to increase blood sugar by acting on the liver. Glucagon stimulates gluconeogenesis (synthesis of new glucose) (Option B) and glycogenolysis (breakdown of stored glycogen) (Option C). It also promotes ketogenesis (production of ketone bodies) (Option D) as an alternative energy source. Critically, glucagon inhibits glycolysis (Option A) in the liver to prevent the immediate consumption of the glucose it is trying to produce. By inhibiting key glycolytic enzymes like phosphofructokinase-1, glucagon ensures that glucose is exported to the systemic circulation rather than broken down into pyruvate. Therefore, while the other processes mentioned are stimulated by glucagon to maintain energy balance during fasting, glycolysis is the only pathway listed that it actively suppresses.

Question 13

During a routine health examination, a 45-year- old man inquires about where cholesterol is mainly synthesized in the body. What is the primary site of cholesterol synthesis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Cholesterol is a vital lipid synthesized in most tissues of the human body, with the liver being the primary site. The entire 27-carbon structure of cholesterol is derived solely from Acetyl-CoA. The process begins with the condensation of Acetyl-CoA molecules to form HMG-CoA, which is subsequently reduced to mevalonate by the rate-limiting enzyme HMG-CoA reductase. Pyruvate (Option B) and Oxaloacetate (Option D) are intermediates in the TCA cycle and gluconeogenesis, respectively, but are not the direct building blocks for sterol rings. Malonyl-CoA (Option C) is a critical intermediate in fatty acid synthesis rather than cholesterol synthesis. Because every carbon atom in cholesterol can be traced back to the acetate units of Acetyl-CoA, it remains the definitive primary source for the entire pathway. Understanding this source is clinically relevant as statin medications target the early steps of this Acetyl-CoA-dependent synthesis.

Question 15

Which molecule is universally known to serve as a methyl-group donor in various biochemical reactions?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

S-Adenosyl Methionine (SAM) is the universal activated methyl donor in biological systems. It is formed from the amino acid methionine and ATP. SAM provides the methyl group for hundreds of critical biochemical reactions, including the methylation of DNA, the synthesis of creatine, and the conversion of norepinephrine to epinephrine. After donating its methyl group, it is converted to S-adenosyl homocysteine. Glucose-6-phosphate (Option A) is a central intermediate in glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway. Acetyl CoA (Option B) is the primary donor of acetyl groups and the fuel for the TCA cycle. S-Adenosyl Serine (Option D) is not a standard biochemical methyl donor. SAM’s unique high-energy sulfonium bond makes the methyl group highly reactive, which is why it is the definitive "universal" donor for methylation across all kingdoms of life.

Question 16

What is the primary location of the Thermogenin protein?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Thermogenin, also known as Uncoupling Protein 1 (UCP1), is a specialized protein found in the inner mitochondrial membrane of brown adipose tissue. Its primary function is non-shivering thermogenesis, a process vital for maintaining body temperature in neonates and hibernating animals. Thermogenin works by allowing protons to leak across the inner mitochondrial membrane, bypassing ATP synthase. This collapses the proton gradient, and instead of producing ATP, the energy is released directly as heat. The Golgi complex (Option A), Endoplasmic reticulum (Option B), and Nucleus (Option D) do not contain thermogenin or participate in this specific uncoupling of the electron transport chain. Because thermogenin’s physiological role is strictly tied to the uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation, which occurs only in the mitochondria, its localization is definitively mitochondrial.
