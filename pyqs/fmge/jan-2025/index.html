Question 1

A 45-year-old woman presents with tingling in the lateral three fingers and loss of sensation at the base of the thumb on the dorsal hand. Which nerve is involved?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The patient’s symptoms of tingling in the lateral digits and loss of sensation at the dorsal base of the thumb point to the superficial branch of the radial nerve. This nerve provides cutaneous sensation to the dorsum of the hand, particularly the first web space and the dorsal aspect of the lateral three-and-a-half fingers, excluding the nail beds. The ulnar nerve (Option B) supplies the medial one-and-a-half digits and is not involved here. While the median nerve (Option C) supplies the lateral three-and-a-half digits, its distribution is primarily palmar and includes only the distal dorsal phalanges (fingertips); it does not supply the dorsal root of the thumb. The Anterior Interosseous Nerve (Option D) is a purely motor branch of the median nerve and has no cutaneous sensory distribution. Therefore, the specific loss of sensation on the dorsal aspect of the thumb base confirms radial nerve involvement.

Question 2

Which of the following is the afferent limb of the corneal reflex?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The corneal reflex is an important protective reflex of the eye that involves both a sensory (afferent) and a motor (efferent) component. The afferent limb is carried by the trigeminal nerve (cranial nerve V), specifically its ophthalmic division (V₁). This division supplies sensory innervation to the cornea and detects tactile or noxious stimuli applied to it, transmitting the impulse to the brainstem. The efferent limb of the corneal reflex is mediated by the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII), which innervates the orbicularis oculi muscle, producing bilateral blinking in response to corneal stimulation. The optic nerve (Option A) is involved in visual pathways and serves as the afferent limb of the pupillary light reflex, not the corneal reflex. The trochlear nerve (Option D) innervates the superior oblique muscle and has no role in corneal sensation or blinking. Therefore, the trigeminal nerve is the correct answer, as it provides the sensory input necessary to initiate the corneal reflex.

Question 5

Which among the following is pressure epiphysis

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

A pressure epiphysis is the articular end of a long bone that participates in joint formation and is responsible for transmitting the weight of the body. The head of the humerus is a classic example, as it forms the shoulder joint and bears mechanical load during arm movement. Other examples include the head of the femur and the lower end of the radius. Regarding the distractors, terms like the "elbow joint" (Option B) and "wrist joint" (Option C) refer to the entire articulation rather than a specific epiphysis. The sternum (Option D) is a flat bone and does not possess epiphyses in the same manner as long bones. Additionally, there are traction epiphyses (like the humeral tubercles) which serve as attachment points for muscles but do not transmit weight. Therefore, the head of the humerus specifically fits the functional definition of a pressure epiphysis.

Question 7

What is the most common defect in a ventricular septal defect?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The most common type of ventricular septal defect (VSD) is the membranous (or perimembranous) defect, accounting for approximately 80% of all cases. These defects occur in the membranous part of the interventricular septum, located below the crista supraventricularis. Muscular (trabecular) defects (Option A) are also common, particularly in infants, and may present as multiple defects known as "Swiss cheese" VSDs; they have a high rate of spontaneous closure. Inlet defects (Option C) represent about 8% of all VSDs and are frequently associated with Trisomy 21. Outlet or infundibular defects (Option D) are located below the semilunar valves (aortic and pulmonary) within the outlet septum of the right ventricle. VSDs generally result from the incomplete development or fusion of various septal components during embryonic cardiac formation. While muscular defects are prevalent, the membranous type remains the most frequently encountered in clinical practice and carries a high potential for spontaneous closure.

Question 8

The afferent limb of the corneal (wisp) reflex is carried by the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve (V1), specifically via the nasociliary nerve.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The corneal reflex (blink reflex) is a protective mechanism that triggers bilateral eyelid closure when the cornea is stimulated. The Trigeminal nerve (CN V), specifically the ophthalmic division (V1), is responsible for the afferent limb of this reflex. Sensory fibers from the cornea travel via the nasociliary branch of V1 to the trigeminal sensory nucleus in the brainstem. The Facial nerve (CN VII) (Option A) mediates the efferent limb, sending motor signals to the orbicularis oculi muscle to contract and close the eye. The Oculomotor nerve (CN III) (Option C) controls eyelid elevation and most extraocular muscles but is not part of this reflex pathway. The Abducens nerve (CN VI) (Option D) is responsible for lateral eye movement and is unrelated to the blink response. Clinically, testing this reflex is essential for assessing brainstem integrity and the function of cranial nerves V and VII.

Question 9

A histological section is shown, with an arrow indicating a large, flask-shaped cell located in the middle layer. Identify the cell marked by the arrow.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The histological section shows the cerebellar cortex, which consists of three layers: molecular, Purkinje, and granular. The marked area is a Purkinje cell, identifiable by its large, flask-shaped body arranged in a single row between the other two layers. These cells provide the only output from the cerebellar cortex and have an inhibitory effect on deep nuclei. Stellate and basket cells (Options A and B) are smaller inhibitory interneurons located in the outer molecular layer. Granular cells (Option D) are small, densely packed excitatory neurons found in the innermost granular layer. Purkinje cells are unique for their extensive dendritic branches that extend into the molecular layer. Identifying these cells is fundamental for understanding the architecture and inhibitory coordination of the cerebellum.

Question 12

Which segment of the nephron has the lowest osmolality under the influence of antidiuretic hormone (ADH)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The early distal tubule maintains the lowest osmolality in the nephron, regardless of the presence of antidiuretic hormone (ADH). This segment is functionally impermeable to water but actively reabsorbs sodium and chloride (NaCl), which further dilutes the tubular fluid, making it hypotonic. In contrast, the descending limb of the loop of Henle (Option B) is highly permeable to water, leading to a significant increase in osmolality as fluid moves into the hypertonic medulla. Under the influence of ADH, the collecting duct (Option C) and the late distal tubule (Option D) become highly permeable to water via aquaporins. This allows water to be reabsorbed along the osmotic gradient, which concentrates the urine and increases the osmolality of the fluid within these segments significantly. Therefore, the early distal tubule remains the most dilute segment because it removes solutes without allowing water to follow.

Question 13

Which of the following physiological changes occurs in muscles during contraction while exercising?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

During muscle contraction, the sarcomere shortens according to the sliding filament theory. The I-band, which consists only of thin (actin) filaments, decreases in length as these filaments are pulled toward the center of the sarcomere, increasing their overlap with the thick (myosin) filaments. The H-zone, the region containing only thick filaments, also shortens or disappears. The A-band (Option B) corresponds to the full length of the thick filaments; since the filaments themselves do not change in length, the A-band remains constant during contraction. The M-line marks the central point where thick filaments are anchored and does not increase in length (Option A); it simply becomes more prominent. Option D is incorrect because the M-line length remains stable. Therefore, the only correct observation among the choices is the shortening of the I-band as the muscle fibers actively contract.

Question 14

During spermatogenesis, which hormone is responsible for inhibiting the secretion of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Inhibin, specifically Inhibin B, is the primary hormone secreted by Sertoli cells in the testes that negatively regulates the secretion of Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH) from the anterior pituitary. This feedback mechanism is crucial for controlling the rate of spermatogenesis. While testosterone (Option A) and other androgens (Option D) also provide negative feedback, they primarily act on the hypothalamus and pituitary to inhibit the secretion of Luteinizing Hormone (LH) and Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone (GnRH), rather than FSH directly. Luteinizing Hormone (Option C) serves to stimulate the Leydig cells to produce testosterone and does not have an inhibitory role in this pathway. Therefore, Inhibin is the specific factor responsible for the targeted inhibition of FSH during the male reproductive cycle.

Question 15

A patient with a long history of chronic smoking presents with breathlessness. Pulmonary function tests reveal a reduced FEV₁%. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

A history of chronic smoking combined with an obstructive pattern on spirometry—specifically a decrease in FEV1 and a reduced FEV1/FVC ratio (<70%)—is characteristic of COPD. COPD includes chronic bronchitis and emphysema, both of which involve irreversible airflow limitation. In contrast, pulmonary fibrosis (Option B) and interstitial lung disease (Option C) are restrictive lung diseases; they typically show a normal or even increased FEV1/FVC ratio because the forced vital capacity (FVC) decreases more than the FEV1. While asthma (Option D) also causes an obstructive pattern, it is distinguished from COPD by its significant reversibility after bronchodilator use and is less directly linked to chronic smoking in late adulthood compared to COPD. Therefore, given the specific risk factor of smoking and the obstructive findings, COPD is the most likely clinical diagnosis.

Question 16

Dehydration is more severe in infants compared to adults. Which of the following is a true statement about infants?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Infants have a significantly higher percentage of total body water (TBW) compared to adults, which contributes to their increased susceptibility to severe dehydration. At birth, TBW accounts for approximately 75–80% of an infant's body weight, whereas it is only about 55–60% in adults. In the early neonatal period, the extracellular fluid (ECF) volume actually exceeds the intracellular fluid (ICF) volume (supporting Option A at birth, but not as a general lifelong rule). As the infant grows, the ICF eventually becomes the dominant compartment. Option B is incorrect for neonates but true for older children and adults. ECF and ICF are never equal (Option D). Because infants have higher metabolic rates and higher water turnover, any fluid loss represents a larger proportion of their TBW, making dehydration a pediatric emergency.

Question 17

In an experimental study, norepinephrine was administered to guinea pigs, leading to an increase in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure along with a reduction in heart rate. Which physiological mechanism best explains this response?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Norepinephrine (NE) is a potent -adrenergic agonist that causes widespread vasoconstriction, leading to a significant increase in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure. Although NE also stimulates -receptors in the heart (which would normally increase heart rate), the rapid rise in systemic blood pressure stretches the baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch. This baroreceptor stimulation triggers a reflex vagal (parasympathetic) response from the medulla, which overrides the direct cardiac effect and causes a compensatory decrease in heart rate (reflex bradycardia). Option A and B are incorrect because NE is an agonist, not a blocker. Baroreceptor inhibition (Option D) would result in a faster heart rate due to a loss of parasympathetic tone. Thus, the paradox of increased blood pressure coupled with a decreased heart rate is entirely explained by the baroreceptor reflex.

Question 18

A patient engages in strenuous exercise, skips a meal, and later consumes alcohol at a party. Alcohol intake inhibits which biochemical process, thereby increasing the risk of developing hypoglycemia?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Alcohol metabolism by alcohol dehydrogenase and acetaldehyde dehydrogenase produces an excess of NADH, significantly increasing the NADH/NAD+ ratio in the liver. This high ratio shifts metabolic reactions away from glucose production by preventing the conversion of lactate to pyruvate and malate to oxaloacetate, both of which are essential substrates for gluconeogenesis. Hypoglycemia occurs because the body cannot synthesize new glucose once glycogen stores are depleted (common after exercise or fasting). Alcohol does not directly inhibit glycogenolysis (Option B), but it becomes the primary issue when glycogen is already gone. The HMP shunt (Option C) and ketone body formation (Option D) are not the primary pathways responsible for blood glucose maintenance. In fact, alcohol metabolism can actually promote ketogenesis. Therefore, the inhibition of gluconeogenesis is the specific mechanism behind alcohol-induced fasting hypoglycemia.

Question 19

Which of the following is deficient in the mucopolysaccharidosis associated with Natowicz disease?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Natowicz Disease, also classified as Mucopolysaccharidosis Type IX (MPS IX), is an extremely rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by a deficiency of the enzyme hyaluronidase. This enzyme is responsible for the degradation of hyaluronic acid (hyaluronan); its deficiency leads to the abnormal accumulation of hyaluronic acid in various tissues. Clinical features are relatively mild compared to other MPS types and include soft tissue masses and joint stiffness. In contrast, dermatan and heparan sulfate (Options A and B) accumulate in MPS I (Hurler) and MPS II (Hunter) syndromes due to different enzyme defects. Keratan sulfate (Option C) accumulates in MPS IV (Morquio syndrome). Because Natowicz disease is specifically the "hyaluronan-accumulation" disorder, the absence of hyaluronidase makes hyaluronic acid the deficiently processed glycosaminoglycan (GAG).

Question 20

Which of the following enzymes is dependent on Vitamin C for its activity?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Prolyl hydroxylase is a key enzyme in collagen synthesis, responsible for the hydroxylation of proline residues in procollagen. This post-translational modification is essential for the stability of the collagen triple helix. The enzyme requires vitamin C (ascorbic acid) as a cofactor to keep iron (Fe²⁺) in its reduced, active state at the catalytic site. In the absence of vitamin C, prolyl hydroxylase (and lysyl hydroxylase) cannot function effectively, leading to defective collagen formation, which is the biochemical basis of scurvy. Lysyl oxidase (Option B) is also involved in collagen maturation, but it is copper-dependent, not vitamin C–dependent, and is responsible for cross-linking collagen and elastin fibers. Lysyl dehydrogenase (Option A) and hydroxyprolyl kinase (Option D) are not key enzymes in the vitamin C–dependent collagen synthesis pathway. Thus, the enzyme whose activity depends on vitamin C is prolyl hydroxylase, explaining the classic features of vitamin C deficiency such as bleeding gums and impaired wound healing.

Question 21

A 35-year-old man presents to the emergency department with blurred vision, dizziness, and a severe headache after consuming an unknown amount of homemade alcohol earlier the same day. He is disoriented on examination, and laboratory investigations show a high–anion gap metabolic acidosis. Which metabolite is primarily responsible for the toxicity in this condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The patient’s symptoms of blurred ("snowfield") vision, dizziness, and metabolic acidosis with a high anion gap after consuming homemade alcohol are classic for methanol poisoning. Methanol (Option A) itself is not directly toxic. It is metabolized by alcohol dehydrogenase to formaldehyde (Option C), which is then rapidly converted by aldehyde dehydrogenase into formic acid. Formic acid is the primary metabolite responsible for the toxicity; it inhibits cytochrome c oxidase, causing cellular hypoxia, optic neuropathy, and severe metabolic acidosis. Acetic acid (Option D) is the non-toxic product of ethanol metabolism. Formaldehyde is an intermediate but is too short-lived to be the primary driver of systemic toxicity. Treatment involves ethanol or fomepizole to block the formation of these toxic metabolites.

Question 22

Which amino acid serves as the principal buffer in blood because of its ability to both accept and donate protons at physiological pH?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Histidine is the only amino acid that functions effectively as a buffer at physiological pH (7.4). This is because its imidazole side chain has a pKa of approximately 6.0, which is close enough to the blood's pH to allow it to both accept and donate protons. Arginine (Option B) has a pKa of 12.5, which is far too high to act as a buffer in the body. Tryptophan (Option C) and Tyrosine (Option D) have minimal buffering capacity because their side chains are either non-ionizable or have pKa values (10.1 for tyrosine) that are not relevant at physiological pH. Histidine residues are particularly abundant in hemoglobin, where they play a vital role in the Bohr effect, helping to maintain blood pH while regulating oxygen delivery to tissues.

Question 23

During prolonged starvation, which hormone is predominantly secreted to help maintain blood glucose levels?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

During starvation, glucagon is the primary hormone secreted to maintain blood glucose levels. It functions by stimulating glycogenolysis (glycogen breakdown) and gluconeogenesis (glucose synthesis) in the liver, while also increasing lipolysis in adipose tissue to provide alternative fuel sources. Insulin (Option B) levels decrease during starvation to limit glucose uptake by peripheral tissues and conserve it for the brain. Amylase (Option C) is a digestive enzyme, not a metabolic hormone. Somatostatin (Option D) inhibits both insulin and glucagon but does not directly drive the glucose elevation needed during a fast. As starvation progresses past the first 24 hours, glucagon remains essential for driving hepatic gluconeogenesis as glycogen stores are depleted. Cortisol and growth hormone also eventually increase to support these long-term metabolic adaptations.

Question 24

In cases of paracetamol poisoning, N-acetylcysteine is used as an antidote. What is its mechanism of action in preventing toxicity?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Paracetamol is metabolized into a toxic intermediate called NAPQI, which is normally detoxified by glutathione. In an overdose, glutathione stores are depleted, allowing NAPQI to accumulate and cause hepatic necrosis. N-acetylcysteine (NAC) prevents this by acting as a glutathione precursor, effectively replenishing glutathione levels to facilitate detoxification. NAC does not inhibit the formation of the toxin (Option A), nor does it directly remove paracetamol from the system (Option B). It also does not neutralize liver enzymes (Option D). NAC therapy is most effective when initiated within 8 hours of ingestion but may still provide benefit up to 36 hours post-overdose. Additionally, NAC directly binds to NAPQI and enhances the non-toxic sulfation metabolic pathway. This life-saving intervention is the cornerstone of managing paracetamol-induced acute liver failure.

Question 25

What is the primary mechanism behind tissue damage in long-standing diabetes?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Chronic hyperglycemia is the fundamental mechanism driving tissue damage in long-standing diabetes. Persistent high blood glucose levels lead to several destructive pathways, including the formation of Advanced Glycation End Products (AGEs), which cause inflammation and vascular injury. While sorbitol accumulation (Option B) contributes specifically to cataracts and neuropathy through the polyol pathway, it is just one of many mechanisms triggered by hyperglycemia. Insulin resistance (Option A) and decreased insulin secretion (Option D) are the underlying causes of the diabetic state, but the resulting hyperglycemia is what actually mediates chronic complications like retinopathy, nephropathy, and atherosclerosis. Other hyperglycemia-induced pathways include the activation of Protein Kinase C (PKC) and the hexosamine pathway, both of which increase vascular permeability and oxidative stress. Thus, managing blood glucose is central to preventing systemic diabetic complications.

Question 26

KAT G mutation associated with:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Resistance to Isoniazid (INH) in Mycobacterium tuberculosis is most commonly associated with a mutation in the katG gene. INH is a prodrug that must be activated by the catalase-peroxidase enzyme, which is encoded by katG. Mutations in this gene result in an ineffective enzyme that cannot activate the drug, leading to high-level resistance. Rifampicin resistance (Option B) is associated with mutations in the rpoB gene (RNA polymerase). Levofloxacin resistance (Option C) is linked to mutations in DNA gyrase genes (gyrA and gyrB). Understanding these genetic markers is essential for interpreting rapid molecular diagnostic tests like GeneXpert or Line Probe Assays for drug-resistant tuberculosis management.

Question 27

In patients with diabetes mellitus, cataract formation occurs mainly due to the accumulation of which substance within the lens?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

In patients with diabetes, chronic hyperglycemia activates the polyol pathway, where glucose is converted into sorbitol by the enzyme aldose reductase. Sorbitol is only slowly metabolized into fructose, leading to its accumulation within the lens. Because sorbitol is osmotically active, its presence causes a significant influx of water, resulting in lens swelling and osmotic stress. This process, along with subsequent oxidative damage to lens proteins, leads to the formation of "snowflake cataracts" in younger diabetics or accelerated senile cataracts in older patients. Dulcitol and Galactitol (Options B and D) are identical substances that accumulate in cases of galactosemia, not diabetes. Therefore, sorbitol accumulation is the direct biochemical cause of lens opacification in the setting of elevated blood glucose levels. PATHOLOGY

Question 28

Which of the following enzymes is typically elevated in prostate cancer?

Acid phosphatase, specifically the prostatic acid phosphatase (PAP) isoenzyme, is characteristically elevated in patients with prostate cancer, particularly when the disease is advanced or has metastasized to the bone. Question 29

What is the usual method of collecting a sample of urine to detect UTI?

Correct answer: B

In toilet-trained individuals, the "clean-catch" midstream urine sample is the preferred method for detecting a Urinary Tract Infection (UTI). Question 30

Which of the following conditions is not reversed with the administration of atropine?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Atropine is a competitive antagonist of muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, and it effectively counteracts parasympathetic overstimulation at smooth muscle, cardiac tissue, and exocrine glands. Therefore, it is useful in conditions such as bradycardia (Option A), excessive salivation (Option C), and bronchoconstriction (Option D), which are mediated by M₂ and M₃ muscarinic receptors. However, muscle weakness caused by neuromuscular blocking agents is mediated by nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (Nm) located at the neuromuscular junction. Atropine has no action on nicotinic receptors, and thus cannot reverse skeletal muscle paralysis or weakness. Reversal of neuromuscular blockade requires an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor such as neostigmine, which increases acetylcholine levels at the neuromuscular junction to competitively overcome the blockade. Atropine is administered concurrently with neostigmine only to prevent muscarinic side effects (such as bradycardia, bronchospasm, and salivation) caused by increased acetylcholine. Therefore, atropine cannot directly improve skeletal muscle strength, as it does not act on the receptors responsible for neuromuscular transmission.

Question 31

Which drug is preferred for management of diabetes in a patient with cardiac and renal failure?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

SGLT2 inhibitors (such as empagliflozin and dapagliflozin) are the preferred drugs for managing diabetes in patients with both cardiac and renal failure because of their strong cardioprotective and renoprotective benefits, independent of glucose control. These drugs act on the proximal renal tubules to inhibit sodium–glucose cotransport, leading to glycosuria and natriuresis. This results in osmotic diuresis, reduced preload and afterload, improvement in heart failure outcomes, and slowing of chronic kidney disease progression. Pioglitazone (Option B) is contraindicated in heart failure due to fluid retention and weight gain, which can worsen cardiac function. Metformin (Option C), although first-line for type 2 diabetes, is contraindicated in advanced renal failure because of the risk of lactic acidosis. Sulfonylureas (Option D) increase the risk of prolonged hypoglycemia, especially in renal impairment due to reduced drug clearance. Thus, SGLT2 inhibitors provide the greatest overall benefit with the lowest risk in diabetic patients with concomitant heart failure and chronic kidney disease, making them the drug of choice in this scenario.

Question 32

Which adverse effect of Bleomycin is exacerbated by radiation exposure?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Bleomycin is an antitumor antibiotic known for its dose-limiting pulmonary toxicity, which can manifest as interstitial pneumonitis or progressive pulmonary fibrosis. This risk is significantly exacerbated by concurrent or prior radiation exposure, a phenomenon sometimes called "radiation recall pneumonitis". The lung tissue is particularly vulnerable because it lacks significant levels of bleomycin hydrolase, the enzyme responsible for inactivating the drug. Regarding other options, bleomycin has minimal impact on the gastrointestinal tract (Option B) and is not associated with significant neurotoxicity (Option C), unlike drugs like vincristine. It also lacks the significant hepatotoxicity (Option D) seen with many other chemotherapeutic agents. Cumulative doses exceeding 400 units and supplemental oxygen therapy also increase the risk of lung damage. Therefore, monitoring pulmonary function via is essential when combining bleomycin with radiation.

Question 33

What is the key difference between Cisplatin and Carboplatin?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

While both are platinum-based chemotherapy agents, they have distinct toxicity profiles. Cisplatin is highly nephrotoxic, often causing acute tubular necrosis, and requires aggressive intravenous hydration with mannitol or amifostine to protect the kidneys. Carboplatin, its second-generation analog, is significantly less nephrotoxic and generally does not require such intensive hydration. Regarding the other options, Cisplatin is actually more neurotoxic (Option A) and more emetogenic (causing severe nausea/vomiting, Option B) than Carboplatin. Option C is incorrect because Cisplatin remains the more neurotoxic drug of the two, often causing peripheral neuropathy and ototoxicity. Carboplatin’s main dose-limiting toxicity is myelosuppression, particularly thrombocytopenia. Therefore, the higher risk of kidney injury (nephrotoxicity) is the most critical clinical difference that necessitates different management protocols for patients receiving Cisplatin.

Question 34

A patient with rheumatoid arthritis is being treated with indomethacin and is also on an ACE inhibitor for hypertension. Which adverse effect is most likely to occur due to this drug combination?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The concurrent use of an ACE inhibitor (like enalapril) and a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) like indomethacin significantly increases the risk of hyperkalemia. ACE inhibitors reduce aldosterone secretion by blocking angiotensin II production, which leads to decreased potassium excretion. Indomethacin further worsens this by inhibiting renal prostaglandins, which normally maintain renal blood flow and support potassium secretion. The combination effectively impairs the body's ability to clear potassium, potentially leading to cardiac arrhythmias. Hypercalcemia (Option B) is not a standard side effect of either drug. Hypernatremia (Option C) is unlikely because ACE inhibitors promote mild sodium excretion (natriuresis). Hyperphosphatemia (Option D) is typically a complication of chronic kidney disease rather than a direct drug-drug interaction between these classes. Therefore, serum potassium levels must be closely monitored in patients on this combination therapy.

Question 35

A patient with a known penicillin allergy presents with fever and burning micturition, along with hearing impairment and dizziness. Which drug would be the most appropriate choice for management in this case?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The patient presents with symptoms of a urinary tract infection (UTI) and pre-existing hearing/balance issues. Given the penicillin allergy, beta-lactams like Ampicillin-Clavulanic acid (Option D) are contraindicated. Ciprofloxacin, a fluoroquinolone, is highly effective against common Gram-negative uropathogens and is safe for penicillin-allergic patients. Regarding the other choices, Amikacin (Option B) is an aminoglycoside that provides excellent UTI coverage but is notoriously ototoxic and nephrotoxic; using it in a patient who already has hearing problems and dizziness would be dangerous. Azithromycin (Option A) is a macrolide that has poor urinary tract penetration and is not a first-line agent for UTI. Ciprofloxacin offers the best balance of efficacy for burning micturition while avoiding exacerbation of the patient’s otologic symptoms. Therefore, Ciprofloxacin is the most appropriate and safest management choice in this specific clinical scenario.

Question 36

Which of the following drug dosages is not correct for the treatment of leprosy in adults?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

In the standard World Health Organization (WHO) Multi-Drug Therapy (MDT) for adult leprosy, the correct dose of Rifampicin is 600 mg administered once a month under supervision. A dose of 450 mg (Option D) is incorrect for adults; this lower dose is specifically designated for children aged 9–14 years. Regarding the correct dosages, Dapsone 100 mg (Option A) is the daily self-administered dose for both paucibacillary and multibacillary adult patients. For multibacillary cases, Clofazimine is administered as a 300 mg monthly supervised dose (Option C) combined with a 50 mg daily self-administered dose (Option B). Because the adult regimen relies on the higher 600 mg Rifampicin dose to ensure bactericidal activity against Mycobacterium leprae, 450 mg is inadequate and therefore the incorrect adult dosage in this list.

Question 37

A patient receives succinylcholine during surgery for muscle relaxation and soon develops hyperthermia, muscle rigidity, tremors, and tachycardia. What is the most appropriate treatment for this condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The clinical features of hyperthermia, muscle rigidity, tremors, and tachycardia following succinylcholine administration indicate Malignant Hyperthermia. This life-threatening genetic condition is triggered by certain volatile anesthetics and depolarizing relaxants like succinylcholine. The specific treatment and antidote is Dantrolene sodium. It works by inhibiting calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum in skeletal muscle, thereby reversing the hypermetabolic state. Neostigmine (Option A) is an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor used to reverse non-depolarizing blocks; it is contraindicated here as it can exacerbate the metabolic crisis by increasing acetylcholine. Atropine (Option C) is an anticholinergic that could worsen the condition by inhibiting sweating and increasing heart rate. Midazolam (Option D), a benzodiazepine, provides sedation but does not address the underlying calcium-mediated pathophysiology. Immediate management also involves stopping the trigger, hyperventilation with 100% oxygen, and active cooling. Immediate administration of Dantrolene is crucial to prevent severe complications and mortality.

Question 38

A patient presents with muscle weakness, diarrhea, and bradycardia and is diagnosed with organophosphate poisoning. Atropine is administered as part of the treatment. Which of the following effects would not be observed after atropine administration?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Organophosphate (OP) poisoning causes cholinergic overstimulation by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, leading to an accumulation of acetylcholine at both muscarinic and nicotinic receptors. Atropine is a muscarinic antagonist specifically used to reverse muscarinic symptoms such as bradycardia, bronchorrhea, and diarrhea. Therefore, bradycardia is "not seen" after successful atropinization; the goal of treatment is to increase the heart rate to above 80 beats/minute. Muscle weakness (Option B) is a nicotinic effect at the neuromuscular junction and is not reversed by atropine, so it remains present until oximes (like pralidoxime) are given. Diaphoresis (Option C) and nasal discharge (Option D) are muscarinic effects that were present initially but should improve or resolve after atropine administration. Successful treatment is clinically monitored by looking for signs of "atropinization," including dry axillae, clear breath sounds on auscultation, and dilated pupils.

Question 39

What is the mechanism of action of botulinum toxin?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Botulinum toxin prevents muscle contraction by inhibiting the release of acetylcholine (ACh) at the neuromuscular junction. It achieves this by cleaving SNARE proteins, such as SNAP-25, synaptobrevin, and syntaxin, which are essential for the fusion of neurotransmitter vesicles with the presynaptic membrane. This blockade leads to flaccid paralysis. Inhibiting ACh reuptake (Option B) is incorrect because ACh is typically degraded in the synapse, not reuptaken. Blocking postsynaptic receptors (Option C) describes the action of neuromuscular blockers like curare. Inhibiting acetylcholinesterase (Option D) is the mechanism for organophosphates and neostigmine, which increase ACh levels. Botulinum toxin is clinically used for treating dystonias and for cosmetic purposes (Botox) to relax muscles. Severe toxicity causes botulism, which can result in respiratory failure.

Question 40

What is the most common causative organism of prosthetic heart valve endocarditis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Staphylococcus epidermidis, a coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (CoNS), is the most common causative agent of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). It accounts for the majority of cases occurring within the first year after surgery due to its ability to form resilient biofilms on artificial materials. Regarding the distractors, S. viridans (Option A) is the most common cause of native valve endocarditis but is less frequent in prosthetic cases. S. aureus (Option B) is a highly virulent organism that causes acute endocarditis and is the second most common in PVE, but overall numbers favor S. epidermidis. "Staphylococcus" (Option D) is a genus-level term that is too non-specific for this question. Therefore, because of its unique affinity for prosthetic devices, S. epidermidis is the correct microbiological answer.

Question 41

In the diagnosis of typhus, an antigen derived from Proteus species is used. What is this type of antigen known as?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The Weil-Felix test for diagnosing typhus uses certain strains of Proteus bacteria because they share common antigens with Rickettsia. These are called heterophile antigens, defined as antigens that occur in unrelated species but are similar enough to cause cross-reactivity with the same antibodies. For example, antibodies produced during epidemic typhus agglutinate with Proteus OX19. Regarding the distractors, sequestered antigens (Option A) are hidden from the immune system (e.g., lens protein) and do not involve cross-reactivity. Superantigens (Option B) are potent immune activators like TSST-1 that bypass normal antigen presentation. Isoantigens (Option C) vary among individuals of the same species, such as blood group antigens. Because the test relies specifically on the "unrelated" cross-reaction between Proteus and Rickettsia, heterophile antigen is the correct term.

Question 42

A 39-year-old patient with HIV infection and a CD4 count of 139 cells/µL presents with altered mental status and impaired consciousness. Cerebrospinal fluid examination shows a positive finding. Which organism is most likely responsible for this presentation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The patient’s HIV status with a low CD4 count (139 cells/µL), altered sensorium, and a positive CSF India ink preparation strongly indicates Cryptococcus neoformans. This encapsulated yeast is a leading cause of opportunistic meningitis in severely immunocompromised individuals. The India ink preparation highlights the thick polysaccharide capsule, which appears as a clear halo surrounding the budding yeast cells. Aspergillus (Option B) is a filamentous fungus that primarily causes pulmonary disease and is not typical for India ink-positive meningitis. Toxoplasma (Option C) and tuberculosis (Option D) are common HIV complications but do not produce the encapsulated yeast findings seen here. Symptoms of cryptococcal meningitis include headache, fever, memory loss, and cranial nerve paresis. Other diagnostic tools include mucicarmine staining and latex agglutination tests for capsular antigen. Treatment involves an induction phase with Liposomal Amphotericin B and Flucytosine, followed by long-term maintenance therapy with Fluconazole.

Question 43

A patient is positive for HBsAg and anti-HBc IgM. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The presence of Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) along with IgM antibodies to the Hepatitis B core antigen (anti-HBc IgM) is diagnostic of acute Hepatitis B infection. HBsAg is the first marker to appear and indicates a current infection, while anti-HBc IgM specifically confirms that the infection is recent (within the last 6 months). In chronic Hepatitis B (Option B), HBsAg persists for more than 6 months, and anti-HBc IgG is present instead of IgM. The recovery phase (Option C) is defined by the disappearance of HBsAg and the appearance of anti-HBs and anti-HBc IgG. Vaccination (Option D) leads only to the development of anti-HBs antibodies; HBsAg and anti-HBc markers would both be negative in a vaccinated individual. The "window period" occurs when HBsAg has disappeared but anti-HBs has not yet appeared; in this phase, anti-HBc IgM may be the only detectable marker. Correct interpretation is vital for management and assessing infectivity.

Question 44

A farmer presents with fever and subconjunctival hemorrhage, and the microscopic agglutination test is found to be positive. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

A farmer presenting with fever, subconjunctival hemorrhage, and a positive microscopic agglutination test (MAT) points toward Leptospirosis. Leptospirosis is a zoonotic bacterial disease caused by Leptospira interrogans, often transmitted through contact with water or soil contaminated by animal urine. Clinical syndromes range from a mild anicteric illness with conjunctival suffusion to severe Weil’s disease involving jaundice and renal failure. MAT is considered the gold standard diagnostic test for detecting specific antibodies. Brucella (Option A) causes undulant fever and hepatosplenomegaly but not subconjunctival hemorrhage; it is diagnosed via the serum agglutination test. E. coli (Option C) and Staph aureus (Option D) cause diverse infections but are not associated with subconjunctival hemorrhage or a positive MAT. Early antibiotic therapy with doxycycline or penicillin is crucial for prophylaxis and treatment. Occupational exposure makes farmers and sewage workers especially vulnerable.

Question 45

A patient presents with fever, headache, vomiting, neck stiffness, and petechial rashes over the lower limbs. Which organism is most likely responsible for this presentation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The patient’s symptoms of fever, neck rigidity, headache, and vomiting combined with petechial rashes on the limbs are classic for meningococcal meningitis caused by Neisseria meningitidis. This gram-negative diplococcus is a major cause of bacterial meningitis and septicemia. Petechial or purpuric rashes occur in over 80% of cases due to endotoxin-induced vascular injury and intravascular thrombosis. Clostridium perfringens (Option B) causes gas gangrene but not meningitis. Cryptococcus neoformans (Option C) typically presents as chronic meningitis in immunocompromised patients and lacks a petechial rash. Clostridium difficile (Option D) is responsible for antibiotic-associated diarrhea. Severe meningococcal septicemia can lead to Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome, featuring massive purpura and bilateral adrenal hemorrhage. CSF analysis typically shows elevated protein, decreased glucose, and a predominance of neutrophils. Rapid diagnosis and antibiotic administration are essential to reduce mortality.

Question 46

A patient presents with a history of a thorn prick injury sustained one week earlier. Subsequently, he developed a chain of ulcers distributed along the course of the lymphatic vessels. Which organism is most likely responsible for this condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The history of a thorn prick followed by ulcerative lesions along the lymphatic drainage is characteristic of sporotrichosis. This subcutaneous infection is caused by the dimorphic fungus Sporothrix schenckii, which is commonly found on thorns and rose bushes. It typically affects gardeners and laborers, spreading through the lymphatic channels to create nodules that ulcerate. Coccidioides immitis (Option A) primarily causes a respiratory illness known as valley fever and is not associated with this specific lymphatic pattern. Aspergillus flavus (Option B) is associated with invasive aspergillosis and aflatoxin production, not trauma-induced lymphatic ulcers. Trichophyton rubrum (Option D) is a dermatophyte causing superficial skin infections like athlete’s foot rather than deep lymphatic spread. Diagnosis is often confirmed via microscopy showing characteristic "cigar-shaped" yeast cells.

Question 47

A Tzanck smear prepared from a vesicle shows multinucleated giant cells. What is the diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

A Tzanck smear showing multinucleated giant cells with "ground-glass" chromatin is pathognomonic for Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) and Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV) infections. These "Tzanck cells" result from the virus-induced fusion of infected keratinocytes. HIV (Option A) is primarily diagnosed via serological tests like ELISA or PCR and does not produce these specific cellular changes on a smear. HPV (Option C) is associated with koilocytes—epithelial cells with perinuclear halos—rather than multinucleated giant cells. EBV (Option D) is associated with "Downey cells" (atypical lymphocytes) on a peripheral blood smear, not a Tzanck smear. The Tzanck smear is a rapid bedside diagnostic tool for vesicular viral rashes, though definitive identification of the specific herpes virus may require further testing like PCR or viral culture.

Question 48

Which of the following is not caused by food adulteration?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Fluorosis is a chronic condition caused by excessive intake of fluoride, primarily through contaminated drinking water or dental products, rather than the deliberate or accidental adulteration of food. It results from high geological fluoride concentrations in groundwater. In contrast, the other options are classic examples of health conditions caused by food adulteration. Epidemic dropsy (Option B) is caused by mustard oil adulterated with Argemone oil. Neurolathyrism (Option C) results from consuming pulses adulterated with Lathyrus sativus (Kesari dal), which contains the neurotoxin BOAA. Endemic ascites (Option D) is caused by food contaminated with pyrrolizidine alkaloids from plants like Crotalaria. Since fluorosis is an environmental waterborne issue, it is the correct answer as the exception to the food-adulteration list.

Question 49

Which of the following is the correct arrangement about the levels of health care? Examination Paper January 2025 Question

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The Indian healthcare system is structured into three tiers: 1. Primary Level: Sub Centres and Primary Health Centres (PHCs) act as the first point of contact for basic curative and preventive services. 2. Secondary Level: Community Health Centres (CHCs) and District Hospitals provide specialized care and serve as referral centers for PHCs. 3. Question 50

Which of the following is not a food adulteration disease?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Fluorosis is not a food adulteration disease; rather, it is caused by the chronic ingestion of naturally occurring excess fluoride, primarily through contaminated drinking water. It leads to dental mottling and skeletal deformities. In contrast, Lathyrism (Option A) is a classic food adulteration disease caused by the consumption of Lathyrus sativus (Khesari dal) containing the neurotoxin -ODAP, which causes spastic paraparesis. Dropsy (Option C) results from mustard oil adulterated with Argemone mexicana seeds, leading to epidemic edema and heart failure. Aflatoxicosis (Option D) is caused by aflatoxins produced by Aspergillus species contaminating stored grains and nuts, which can lead to liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma.
