Question 2

Most appropriate choice for administering intravenous regional anesthesia?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Intravenous Regional Anesthesia (IVRA), also known as a Bier block, involves injecting a local anesthetic into the venous system of an isolated limb (using a double-tourniquet). Question 4

During a laryngeal surgical procedure, a patient develops loss of sensation above the vocal cords. Which nerve primarily supplies sensory innervation to this area?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The internal branch of the superior laryngeal nerve is the primary structure providing sensory innervation to the laryngeal mucosa situated above the level of the vocal folds (vocal cords). This nerve pierces the thyrohyoid membrane alongside the superior laryngeal artery to supply the vestibule of the larynx. In contrast, the recurrent laryngeal nerve (Option B) is responsible for sensory innervation below the level of the vocal cords and motor supply to all intrinsic muscles of the larynx except the cricothyroid. The glossopharyngeal nerve (Option C) provides sensory supply to the oropharynx and the posterior third of the tongue, but not the internal larynx. The external laryngeal nerve (Option D) is a purely motor nerve that innervates the cricothyroid muscle. Injury to the internal laryngeal nerve typically results in a loss of the protective cough reflex, significantly increasing the risk of aspiration. Therefore, the specific anatomical boundary mentioned—above the vocal cords—definitively identifies the superior laryngeal nerve as the responsible sensory mediator.

Question 5

A patient presents with a cystic swelling in the neck located anterior to the sternocleidomastoid muscle at the junction of its upper and middle thirds. Persistence of which embryological cleft is responsible for the formation of this cyst?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The clinical presentation describes a branchial cyst, which characteristically appears along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, most frequently at the junction of its upper and middle thirds. This cyst arises from the persistence of the second pharyngeal cleft (or cervical sinus of His). During development, the second pharyngeal arch grows downward and normally covers the second, third, and fourth clefts, which then obliterate. Failure of this obliteration results in a fluid-filled cyst. A persistent first cleft (Option A) leads to preauricular cysts or fistulae near the ear or mandible. Persistence of the third (Option C) or fourth (Option D) clefts is much rarer and usually presents lower in the neck, often associated with the pyriform sinus. Branchial cysts often contain cholesterol crystals and can become infected, requiring surgical excision. The specific location anterior to the upper sternocleidomastoid is the classic anatomical landmark for a second branchial cleft anomaly, making it the most probable embryological cause.

Question 6

A 45-year-old patient presents with gait instability, particularly noticeable when standing on one leg. On examination, standing on the left leg causes the right side of the pelvis to drop. Paralysis of which muscle group is responsible for this finding?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The physical exam finding described is a positive Trendelenburg sign. In this case, the drooping of the right pelvis when standing on the left leg indicates that the hip abductors of the stance leg (left) are paralyzed or weak. These abductors are the gluteus medius and gluteus minimus, innervated by the superior gluteal nerve. Normally, these muscles contract on the weight-bearing side to prevent the pelvis from tilting toward the unsupported side. Paralysis of the right-sided muscles (Options A and C) would cause the left pelvis to sag. The gluteus maximus (Option B) is primarily an extensor of the hip rather than an abductor, and its weakness leads to a "lurching" gait but not pelvic sagging in the coronal plane. Instability while walking, or a "waddling gait," is common when these muscles are compromised. Therefore, the sag on the right side while weight-bearing on the left side definitively localizes the pathology to the left gluteus medius and minimus.

Question 7

Describe the structural composition of the outer nose?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The structural framework of the external human nose is composed of both rigid bone and flexible cartilage. The upper one-third of the nose is supported by bone, specifically the paired nasal bones and the frontal process of the maxilla. The lower two-thirds of the nose is composed of hyaline cartilage, which includes the major alar cartilages, lateral nasal cartilages, and the cartilaginous part of the nasal septum. This arrangement provides stability at the root while allowing for flexibility and airway regulation at the tip and nostrils. Option A and Option B provide incorrect proportions for these components. Understanding this specific 1:2 ratio of bone to cartilage is essential in plastic and reconstructive surgery (rhinoplasty) and in managing nasal fractures. Fractures typically involve the upper bony bridge, whereas trauma to the lower portion often results in cartilaginous deviation or septal hematoma. Consequently, the correct anatomical description characterizes the upper third as bony and the lower two-thirds as cartilaginous.

Question 9

If a single zygote divides between 9 and 12 days after fertilization, what type of twins are most likely to result?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The classification of monozygotic twins depends entirely on the timing of zygote cleavage. When division occurs late, specifically between 9 and 12 days after fertilization, the amnion has already formed. Consequently, the twins will share a single placenta and a single amniotic sac, resulting in monochorionic monoamniotic (MoMo) twins. Cleavage before 3 days results in dichorionic diamniotic twins (Option A). Division between 4 and 8 days leads to monochorionic diamniotic twins (Option C), which is the most common type. If division occurs even later than 13 days, incomplete cleavage results in conjoined twins (Option B). MoMo pregnancies are high-risk due to the potential for umbilical cord entanglement and twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome. Understanding this embryological timeline is vital for obstetricians to provide appropriate prenatal monitoring and risk assessment. Since the amnion forms around day 8, a split occurring on day 9-12 inevitably forces the fetuses into a shared environment within the same amniotic cavity.

Question 12

What is the medical term used to describe a clavicular fracture that occurs during childbirth?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Cleidotomy is the medical term for the intentional surgical division or fracture of one or both fetal clavicles. This rare and extreme procedure is performed during difficult deliveries, specifically in cases of severe shoulder dystocia where other maneuvers have failed, or in destructive operations for a dead fetus. By fracturing the clavicle, the shoulder girdle diameter is reduced, allowing the body to pass through the birth canal. Craniotomy (Option A) involves reducing the size of the fetal head. Symphysiotomy (Option B) is a surgical procedure to widen the maternal pelvis by dividing the pubic symphysis. The Zavanelli maneuver (Option C) involves pushing the fetal head back into the uterus to perform an emergency cesarean section. Cleidotomy is generally considered a procedure of last resort due to the potential for neonatal injury, but it can be life-saving for the mother when vaginal delivery is obstructed. Thus, the specific term for fetal clavicle fracture in this context is cleidotomy.

Question 19

During which phase of the cardiac cycle does the first heart sound occur?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The first heart sound (S1), the "lub" of the "lub-dub," is produced primarily by the closure of the atrioventricular (mitral and tricuspid) valves. This occurs at the very beginning of ventricular systole, specifically during the phase of isovolumetric contraction. In this phase, the ventricles begin to contract, raising the internal pressure. As soon as the ventricular pressure exceeds the atrial pressure, the AV valves snap shut, producing S1. This sound signals the start of systole. Aortic ejection (Option B) follows this phase once the semilunar valves open. Isovolumetric relaxation (Option D) is when the second heart sound (S2) occurs due to the closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves. Rapid atrial filling (Option A) occurs during diastole. Understanding the correlation between heart sounds and the phases of the cardiac cycle is fundamental for cardiac auscultation and diagnosing valvular disorders. Therefore, S1 is definitively associated with the initiation of isovolumetric contraction.

Question 20

A 50-year-old patient with longstanding hypertension and diabetes mellitus is evaluated for renal function. In explaining the role of glomerular filtration rate (GFR), which statement correctly describes how GFR is regulated?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR) is heavily dependent on Renal Plasma Flow (RPF). Under physiological conditions, an increase in RPF increases the GFR because it maintains a high hydrostatic pressure across the glomerular capillaries and prevents the rapid rise of oncotic pressure that would otherwise oppose filtration. Afferent arteriolar constriction (Option B) or an increase in sympathetic activity (Option A) both cause a decrease in RPF and a subsequent decrease in GFR by reducing the blood entering the glomerulus. A decrease in RPF (Option D) would lead to a decrease in GFR. GFR is also regulated by autoregulatory mechanisms (tubuloglomerular feedback) and hormones like Angiotensin II. For a patient with diabetes and hypertension, maintaining an optimal GFR is vital to prevent the progression of chronic kidney disease. Understanding that RPF and GFR are generally directly proportional is a core principle of renal physiology, making Option C the only accurate statement.

Question 21

What is the underlying cause of hypoxia in a newly arrived resident in a high-altitude region?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

At high altitudes, the total barometric pressure decreases, which results in a corresponding decrease in the partial pressure of inspired oxygen (PO2). This lower PO2 in the atmosphere leads to a lower PO2 in the alveoli and consequently in the arterial blood, causing hypoxia. This is the primary physiological challenge for anyone ascending to high altitudes. Low hemoglobin levels (Option A, anemia) can cause hypoxia, but it is not the cause of altitude-related distress; in fact, the body eventually increases hemoglobin as an adaptation. Low blood lactate (Option C) is not a cause of hypoxia. High partial pressure of CO2 (Option D) would indicate hypoventilation, but at high altitudes, residents typically hyperventilate to compensate for the low oxygen, leading to low CO2 levels. Understanding this fundamental relationship between atmospheric pressure and PO2 is essential for grasping the pathophysiology of acute mountain sickness and the subsequent process of acclimatization.

Question 22

While examining a patient’s jugular venous pulse, a physician observes a prominent V wave in the waveform. Which clinical condition is most commonly associated with this finding?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The 'v' wave in the jugular venous pulse (JVP) waveform corresponds to atrial filling while the tricuspid valve is closed during ventricular systole. A prominent (large) 'v' wave is a classic physical sign of tricuspid regurgitation. In this condition, blood leaks backward from the right ventricle into the right atrium during systole, significantly increasing the pressure and volume in the atrium and producing an exaggerated 'v' wave. Aortic regurgitation (Option B) affects the left side of the heart and does not directly produce JVP changes. Tricuspid stenosis (Option C) would result in a prominent 'a' wave due to forceful atrial contraction against a narrowed valve. Atrial fibrillation (Option D) is characterized by the absence of the 'a' wave. Bedside assessment of the JVP waveform is a vital diagnostic skill in cardiology, allowing the clinician to differentiate between various right-sided heart pathologies. Thus, a giant 'v' wave is pathognomonic for severe tricuspid regurgitation.

Question 25

A 65-year-old man undergoing evaluation for suspected renal dysfunction has his creatinine clearance measured. Which statement best describes the significance and interpretation of creatinine clearance?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Creatinine clearance is a widely used clinical measure to estimate the Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR). It is considered comparable to inulin clearance, which is the "gold standard" for measuring GFR. Inulin is perfect because it is freely filtered but neither reabsorbed nor secreted. Creatinine is also freely filtered and not reabsorbed; however, a small amount is actively secreted by the renal tubules. This secretion causes creatinine clearance to slightly overestimate the true GFR (by about 10-20%). Despite this, it is far more practical for clinical use than inulin, which requires a continuous infusion. It is not "superior" (Option C) in accuracy, nor "inferior" (Option B) in clinical utility. It is not identical to renal plasma flow (Option D), which is much higher. For most clinical purposes, creatinine clearance provides a sufficiently accurate estimate of kidney function. Consequently, it remains the standard-of-care proxy for GFR in everyday medical practice.

Question 29

Hemoglobin with iron in ferric form is :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Normally, the iron present in hemoglobin is in the ferrous (Fe²⁺) state, which allows reversible binding of oxygen. Methemoglobin is an abnormal form of hemoglobin in which the iron is oxidized to the ferric (Fe³⁺) state. Ferric iron cannot bind oxygen, and its presence also increases the oxygen affinity of the remaining ferrous heme groups, thereby impairing oxygen release to tissues. As a result, methemoglobinemia causes functional hypoxia, often associated with cyanosis and chocolate-colored blood that does not improve with supplemental oxygen. HbA (Option B), fetal hemoglobin HbF (Option C), and HbS (Option D) all normally contain iron in the ferrous state to support oxygen transport. Methemoglobinemia may be congenital (due to enzyme defects) or acquired following exposure to oxidizing agents such as nitrates, nitrites, sulfonamides, or local anesthetics. Treatment involves methylene blue, which reduces ferric iron back to the ferrous form.

Question 30

A 6-year-old child presents with hypoglycemia, hepatomegaly, and growth retardation, raising suspicion of a glycogen storage disorder. Deficiency of which enzyme is characteristically seen in Von Gierke’s disease?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Von Gierke's disease (Glycogen Storage Disease Type I) is caused by a deficiency of the enzyme Glucose-6-phosphatase. This enzyme is the final common step in both gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis, responsible for converting glucose-6-phosphate into free glucose in the liver. Its deficiency prevents the liver from releasing glucose into the blood, leading to severe fasting hypoglycemia. The trapped glycogen leads to massive hepatomegaly. Other features include lactic acidosis, hyperuricemia, and growth retardation. Branching enzyme deficiency (Option B) causes GSD IV (Andersen disease). Debranching enzyme deficiency (Option C) causes GSD III (Cori disease), which is milder than Type I. Muscle glycogen phosphorylase deficiency (Option D) causes GSD V (McArdle disease), which affects muscles but not the liver. Treatment for Von Gierke's focuses on maintaining blood glucose levels through frequent feedings and uncooked cornstarch. This enzyme defect is a classic example of an inborn error of metabolism affecting carbohydrate homeostasis.

Question 31

Ascorbic acid (Vitamin C) is essential for the maturation of collagen. This post-translational modification is necessary for the formation of stable hydrogen bonds that hold the collagen triple helix together. Question 32

While studying metabolic pathways and their intracellular locations, which metabolic pathway is known to occur exclusively in the cytoplasm?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Glycolysis is the metabolic pathway that converts glucose into pyruvate, and it occurs exclusively within the cytoplasm of all human cells. Every enzyme required for this ten-step process is located in the cytosol. Beta oxidation of fatty acids (Option A) and the TCA (Krebs) cycle (Option B) occur exclusively within the mitochondrial matrix. The urea cycle (Option D) is unique because it is split between the mitochondria (the first two steps) and the cytoplasm (the remaining steps). Other purely cytoplasmic pathways include the HMP shunt and fatty acid synthesis. Understanding the sub-cellular compartmentalization of these pathways is essential for grasping how the cell regulates metabolic flux; for example, the separation of fatty acid synthesis (cytoplasm) from beta-oxidation (mitochondria) prevents a futile cycle. Therefore, glycolysis stands out as the fundamental energy-generating pathway that does not require mitochondrial involvement for its initial steps.

NO plays a critical role in regulating blood pressure, inhibiting platelet aggregation, and acting as a neurotransmitter. Tryptophan (Option A) is the precursor for serotonin and melatonin. Understanding the arginine-NO pathway is fundamental to cardiovascular physiology and pharmacology. Thus, arginine serves as the unique and indispensable nitrogen source for the production of this essential gaseous mediator.

Question 35

Which of the following elements is not considered a microelement (trace element) required in very small quantities for normal human health?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

In human nutrition, elements are classified based on the required daily intake. Calcium is classified as a macroelement (or macromineral) because the body requires it in large amounts (typically ) for bone structure, muscle contraction, and nerve signaling. In contrast, Copper (Option A), Zinc (Option B), and Selenium (Option D) are all microelements (trace elements). Question 37

Which class of enzymes uses molecular oxygen as the hydrogen acceptor, resulting in the formation of water?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

In the mitochondrial electron transport chain, Cytochrome c oxidase (Complex IV) is the terminal enzyme responsible for the final reduction of oxygen. It transfers electrons to molecular oxygen, which then combines with hydrogen ions (protons) to generate water. This is the fundamental oxygen-consuming step of aerobic respiration. Catalase (Option A) breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, but does not use molecular oxygen as a hydrogen recipient. Superoxide dismutase (Option B) converts superoxide radicals into oxygen and hydrogen peroxide. Pyruvate dehydrogenase (Option D) is a mitochondrial enzyme that links glycolysis to the TCA cycle but is not part of the respiratory chain's oxygen reduction. Inhibition of cytochrome c oxidase by poisons like cyanide or carbon monoxide is lethal because it completely halts ATP production. Understanding this specific role of Complex IV is essential for grasping how the body utilizes inhaled oxygen to drive cellular energy production.

Question 40

Which drug is known to elevate the levels of uric acid in urine?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Probenecid is a uricosuric agent, meaning its primary mechanism of action is to elevate the levels of uric acid in the urine. It achieves this by inhibiting the reabsorption of uric acid in the proximal convoluted tubules of the kidney, thereby increasing its renal excretion and lowering the concentration of uric acid in the blood. In contrast, Allopurinol (Option D) and Febuxostat (Option B) are xanthine oxidase inhibitors that lower blood uric acid by decreasing its production; consequently, they actually reduce the total amount of uric acid excreted in the urine over time. Colchicine (Option A) is an anti-inflammatory drug used for acute gout flares and does not significantly affect uric acid levels in the blood or urine. Probenecid is used for chronic gout management but is contraindicated in patients with a history of renal stones, as the high urinary uric acid concentration can promote stone formation. Understanding this "production vs. excretion" distinction is vital for choosing the appropriate gout therapy.

Question 44

Identify the type of inhibition shown in the graph.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The given graph is a Lineweaver–Burk plot (1/V vs 1/[S]), commonly used to analyze enzyme inhibition. In the image, both the inhibited (red) and uninhibited (black) lines intersect at the same point on the X-axis, indicating that Km (Michaelis constant) remains unchanged. Why Other Options Are Wrong (A) Competitive inhibition: Km increases (lines intersect on Y-axis), not X-axis. (B) Allosteric inhibition: General term, not specific to this LB plot pattern. (C) Uncompetitive inhibition: Produces parallel lines, not intersecting ones.

Question 50

In a patient with suspected gastrointestinal infection, which culture medium is commonly used in the laboratory to differentiate lactose- fermenting bacteria from non-lactose fermenters?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

MacConkey agar is a specialized differential and selective culture medium widely used in microbiology. It contains bile salts and crystal violet, which inhibit the growth of gram-positive bacteria, making it selective for gram-negative organisms (like Enterobacteriaceae). Crucially, it contains the sugar lactose and a neutral red pH indicator. Bacteria that are lactose fermenters (e.g., E. coli) produce acid, which turns the colonies pink. Identifying the lactose fermentation pattern on MacConkey agar is the foundational first step in the laboratory identification of aerobic gram-negative bacilli, helping clinicians narrow down the etiological agent of a gastrointestinal or urinary tract infection .
