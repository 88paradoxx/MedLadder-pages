Question 1

Identify the tarsal bone marked by the arrow in the given X-ray of the right foot.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The arrow points to the navicular bone, a wedge-shaped tarsal bone situated on the medial aspect of the midfoot. It is anatomically located between the talus (proximally) and the three cuneiform bones (distally). On a dorsoplantar or oblique X-ray, its position on the medial side is highly distinctive. The cuboid bone (Option B) is located on the lateral aspect of the foot. The intermediate cuneiform (Option C) and lateral cuneiform (Option D) are located distal to the navicular and articulate with the metatarsals. Accurate identification of midfoot tarsal bones is essential for diagnosing fractures and dislocations in trauma cases. Based on the medial position between the talar head and the cuneiforms, the bone indicated is the navicular.

Question 2

What is the embryological cause of this anomaly?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The anomaly shown is a unicornuate uterus, which results from the developmental failure of one paramesonephric (Müllerian) duct. Normally, the two paramesonephric ducts must fuse at the midline to form the uterus, cervix, and upper vagina. If one duct fails to develop or fuse properly with its counterpart, it results in a single-sided uterine cavity, often accompanied by a small rudimentary horn. Non-fusion of the mesonephric ducts (Option A) would affect male reproductive anatomy, such as the vas deferens. "Metanephric duct" (Option C) is an incorrect term, as the metanephros forms the kidneys, not the uterus. Complete agenesis (Option D), known as Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser syndrome, would result in the total absence of the uterus and upper vagina. Therefore, the partial failure of Müllerian duct development is the correct embryological cause.

Question 3

A 46-year-old man is diagnosed with a tumor involving the left lobe of the liver, and a left- sided hemihepatectomy is planned. Which lobes of the liver are removed during this surgical procedure?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Functional liver anatomy is based on Couinaud’s segments, which are determined by vascular supply and biliary drainage. A left hemihepatectomy involves the surgical resection of the entire functional left lobe, which consists of segments II, III, and IV. Segment I (the caudate lobe) is functionally distinct and is typically not resected in a standard hemihepatectomy unless specifically indicated (Option B). Segments V, VI, VII, and VIII (Option C) comprise the functional right lobe of the liver. Option D is incorrect because Segment V is part of the right lobe. Therefore, segments 2, 3, and 4 are the correct anatomical targets for a left hemihepatectomy.

Question 4

Which of the following veins are involved in the formation of esophageal varices?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Esophageal varices result from portal hypertension, usually due to liver cirrhosis. When portal blood flow is obstructed, portosystemic anastomoses form at specific sites. At the lower end of the esophagus, the portal circulation (represented by the left gastric vein) meets the systemic circulation (represented by the esophageal veins draining into the azygos system). Increased pressure in the left gastric vein causes the submucosal esophageal veins to become dilated and tortuous, forming varices. The right gastric vein (Option B) drains the lesser curvature but is not the primary contributor to lower esophageal varices. The left gastroepiploic vein (Option C) and right gastroepiploic vein (Option D) drain the greater curvature into the splenic and superior mesenteric veins, respectively, and are not part of the esophageal portosystemic shunt. Therefore, the left gastric vein is the key portal vessel involved.

Question 5

Injury to which part causes difficulty in dorsiflexion of the foot?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

In the provided image, point B identifies the common peroneal nerve as it courses superficially around the neck of the fibula. This nerve is highly susceptible to injury at this site. It branches into the deep and superficial peroneal nerves, with the deep peroneal nerve responsible for innervating the tibialis anterior muscle. This muscle is the primary driver of dorsiflexion (lifting the foot upward). An injury at point B results in "foot drop," where the patient cannot dorsiflex the foot. The sciatic nerve (Point A) would cause much more extensive leg weakness. The tibial nerve (Point C) innervates the posterior compartment, and its injury would cause difficulty in plantarflexion (pointing the foot down). While the deep peroneal nerve (Point D) also causes foot drop, the injury at the fibular head (B) is the more classic clinical site.

Question 6

Which structure is formed by the structure indicated by the arrow?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The arrow identifies the notochord, an embryonic midline structure derived from mesoderm that serves as the primitive axial skeleton. While it mostly disappears during development, its remnant persists as the nucleus pulposus, the gelatinous core of the adult intervertebral disc. The annulus fibrosus (Option A) is the tough outer ring of the intervertebral disc, but it originates from the sclerotome portion of the mesoderm rather than the notochord itself. The neural tube (Option C) is formed from ectoderm; although its formation is induced by signals from the notochord, it is not a direct derivative. The septum transversum (Option D) is another mesoderm-derived structure that contributes to the formation of the diaphragm and is unrelated to the notochordal pathway. Therefore, the notochord's specific adult anatomical legacy is the creation of the nucleus pulposus, which functions to absorb spinal compression.

Question 7

A diabetic worker sustains a stab injury to the central part of his palm. Three days later, he develops swelling, severe pain, and inability to extend the middle and ring fingers. Pus collection in a palmar space is suspected. Which palmar space is most likely involved?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

A central palm stab injury followed by swelling, pain, and restricted movement of the middle and ring fingers indicates an infection of the midpalmar space (labeled B). This deep anatomical compartment is bounded anteriorly by the palmar aponeurosis and posteriorly by the metacarpals. The hypothenar space (Option A) is located on the ulnar side and primarily involves the little finger. The thenar space (Option C) is situated on the radial side, typically associated with infections of the thumb and index finger. The dorsal subaponeurotic space (Option D) is located on the back of the hand rather than the palm. Because the injury was central and the symptoms affect the third and fourth digits, the midpalmar space is the specific site of pus accumulation. Hand infections in diabetic patients are particularly serious due to impaired healing and increased susceptibility to deep-space abscesses.

Question 8

Which is the narrowest part of the adult laryngeal airway?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

In the adult larynx, the glottis—specifically the rima glottidis, which is the narrow opening between the true vocal cords—represents the narrowest part of the airway. This site is critical for voice production and regulating airflow. The supraglottic region (Option A), which contains the epiglottis and false cords, is wider than the glottic opening. The subglottic region (Option C) is located below the vocal cords; while it is the narrowest part of the larynx in infants and children, it is wider than the glottis in adults. The trachea (Option D) is a significantly larger tubular structure and is much wider than any part of the laryngeal apparatus. Understanding these anatomical differences is vital for intubation and airway management, as the glottis is the primary point of resistance in adults, whereas the subglottis is the limiting factor in pediatric patients.

Question 9

The upper part of the uncinate process commonly attaches to?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The uncinate process is a thin, curved bony hook belonging to the ethmoid bone that forms part of the lateral nasal wall. Its upper part commonly extends to attach to the middle turbinate (middle nasal concha). This structure is essential in forming the drainage pathway for the frontal and maxillary sinuses through the ostiomeatal complex. The superior turbinate (Option B) is located higher in the nasal cavity than the uncinate process, so there is no anatomical attachment between them. The inferior turbinate (Option C) is a separate bone located much lower in the nasal passage. The bulla ethmoidalis (Option D) is an ethmoid air cell situated behind the uncinate process; while they form the boundaries of the hiatus semilunaris inferior, they do not serve as an attachment site for one another. Thus, the middle turbinate is the standard attachment point.

The posterior tibial artery passes behind the medial malleolus within the tarsal tunnel. Question 11

A patient is brought to the emergency department after a road traffic accident with a head injury. He is conscious, but when questioned about the incident, his responses are irrelevant despite fluent speech. Which area of the brain is most likely to be injured?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The patient's presentation of fluent but irrelevant speech characterizes Wernicke's aphasia, also known as receptive aphasia. This condition results from a lesion in Wernicke's area, located in the superior temporal gyrus of the dominant hemisphere. These patients can produce speech with normal rate and rhythm, but they lack comprehension of language, leading to "word salad" answers. A lesion in the pre-central gyrus (Option A) would cause motor weakness or paralysis on the contralateral side rather than a language disorder. Damage to the inferior frontal gyrus (Option B) causes Broca's aphasia, where speech is non-fluent and effortful, though comprehension is relatively preserved. Brain stem injuries (Option D) would typically involve severe motor/sensory deficits or altered consciousness rather than an isolated fluent aphasia. Thus, the disconnect between speech production and meaningful content localizes the injury to the superior temporal gyrus. PHYSIOLOGY

Question 12

A 35-year-old man wakes up after sleeping with his arm hanging over a chair and complains of pain and sensory symptoms. In this condition, which sequence correctly represents the order of susceptibility of different nerve fiber types to compression?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The clinical scenario describes "Saturday night palsy," a compression injury of the radial nerve. Nerve fibers have different sensitivities to external stressors based on their size and myelination. When subjected to pressure or mechanical compression, large myelinated fibers are the most susceptible, following the order: A > B > C. Fiber type A (specifically Alpha) is the most sensitive to pressure, while Type C is the least. In contrast, if the stressor were local anesthesia, the order would be C > B > A, as small unmyelinated fibers are blocked most easily. If the stressor were hypoxia, Type B fibers would be the most susceptible. Because this patient experienced mechanical compression by draping his arm over a chair, the largest and most metabolically demanding fibers (Type A) are affected first, making Option B the correct hierarchy.

Question 13

A woman from Delhi travels to Ladakh, a high- altitude area, and soon after arrival develops breathlessness, headache, and lightheadedness. What is the primary underlying mechanism responsible for these symptoms?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

At high altitudes, the partial pressure of oxygen is lower, which stimulates peripheral chemoreceptors to increase the rate of pulmonary ventilation (hyperventilation). This compensatory mechanism results in an excessive removal of carbon dioxide (CO2) from the blood. The loss of CO2, which acts as an acid in the body, causes the blood pH to rise, leading to respiratory alkalosis. Respiratory acidosis (Option A) occurs with hypoventilation, where CO2 is retained, not at high altitudes. Metabolic alkalosis (Option B) is typically caused by loss of stomach acid from vomiting or diuretic use. Metabolic acidosis (Option D) is associated with conditions like renal failure, diarrhea, or diabetic ketoacidosis. Therefore, the acute hyperventilation in response to hypoxia specifically drives a respiratory alkalotic state until the kidneys compensate by excreting excess bicarbonate over several days.

Question 14

For testing blood clotting/ coagulation parameters, blood is collected in which color- coded vacutainer tube?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

For the assessment of coagulation parameters, such as Prothrombin Time (PT) and Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT), blood must be collected in a light blue vacutainer. This tube contains sodium citrate, which acts as a reversible calcium-chelating anticoagulant. Citrate is preferred because it preserves the fragile coagulation factors necessary for accurate laboratory testing. Red tubes (Option A) contain no anticoagulant and are used for serum studies. Green tubes (Option B) contain heparin and are utilized for arterial blood gases or osmotic fragility tests. Gray tubes (Option D) contain sodium fluoride to inhibit glycolysis for accurate blood glucose measurement. Therefore, the blue-coded vacutainer is the standard requirement for any clotting or intrinsic/extrinsic pathway study.

Question 15

In malignant hyperthermia, a mutation affecting the ryanodine receptor (RYR) triggers a severe reaction to certain anesthetic agents. What is the primary ionic imbalance resulting from this receptor defect?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Malignant hyperthermia is a pharmacogenetic disorder of skeletal muscle. It is triggered by volatile anesthetics (like halothane) or succinylcholine in individuals with a mutation in the RYR1 gene. This mutation results in a hypersensitive ryanodine receptor, which causes a massive, uncontrolled release of calcium ions from the sarcoplasmic reticulum into the sarcoplasm. This excess calcium drives sustained muscle contraction and a state of hypermetabolism, leading to muscle rigidity, extreme tachycardia, and a rapid rise in body temperature. Sodium influx (Option A) and chloride efflux (Option C) are not the primary defects in this condition. Potassium uptake (Option B) is not actively regulated by the sarcoplasmic reticulum in this pathway; in fact, potassium is typically released out of cells during rhabdomyolysis. Therefore, aberrant calcium signaling is the central physiological mechanism.

Question 16

What is the primary protein that binds to thyroxine?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) are poorly soluble in water and must be transported through the bloodstream bound to carrier proteins to regulate their availability to tissues. Thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG) is the primary and most specific transport protein for these hormones. It possesses a high affinity for thyroid hormones and is responsible for carrying the vast majority of T4 in circulation. Follistatin (Option A) is unrelated to thyroid transport; it primarily binds and inhibits activins. Transthyretin (Option B), or prealbumin, binds a much smaller fraction of T4 and serves a secondary role, mainly within the cerebrospinal fluid. Transferrin (Option D) is specifically dedicated to the transport of iron in the blood and has no role in thyroid hormone metabolism. Therefore, TBG remains the most significant systemic carrier of thyroxine, ensuring its stability and controlled delivery to target organs throughout the body.

Question 17

Which among the following best describes 'Neuropraxia'?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Neuropraxia represents the mildest form of nerve injury according to Seddon's classification. It involves a temporary physiological conduction block caused by focal demyelination (damage to the nerve sheath) while the underlying axon remains completely intact. Because the axon is not severed, there is no Wallerian degeneration, and recovery is typically complete within six weeks once the sheath heals. Axonotmesis (Option B) involves a damaged axon within an intact connective tissue framework, leading to distal degeneration and a longer recovery period. Neurotmesis (Option C) is the most severe grade, characterized by complete disruption of both the axon and the supporting nerve sheaths; spontaneous recovery is impossible in these cases and surgical repair is mandated. Therefore, neuropraxia is specifically defined by the preservation of axonal continuity despite localized myelin dysfunction, leading to a favorable prognosis.

Question 18

As a neuron's diameter increases, what effect manifests?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The diameter of an axon is a major determinant of how rapidly an action potential can travel. A larger axonal diameter results in a significantly lower internal (axial) resistance to current flow. This reduced resistance allows local electrical currents to spread more efficiently and quickly along the interior of the axon, enabling faster depolarization of the adjacent membrane segments. Consequently, larger neurons exhibit a higher conduction velocity. This principle is vital in motor neurons where high-speed signal transmission is required. Option B is incorrect as velocity increases with size. Membrane resistance (Option C) per unit length actually decreases with larger surface areas, but the reduction in axial resistance dominates the change in speed. Action potential amplitude (Option D) is determined by ionic equilibrium potentials rather than physical axon size. Thus, diameter and speed are directly proportional.

Question 19

Which among the following hormones, in a lactating mother, is responsible for the growth of breast tissue?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

In a lactating mother, prolactin is the primary hormone responsible for stimulating the growth, development, and maintenance of the milk-producing lobuloalveolar tissue in the breast. It acts on lactotrophs to promote the hyperplasia of mammary glands necessary for milk synthesis. Oxytocin (Option A) is responsible for the milk ejection reflex (let-down) but does not cause tissue growth. Estrogen (Option B) stimulates ductal growth and overall breast enlargement during puberty and pregnancy but is not the specific driver of the alveolar milk-producing tissue growth during established lactation. Progesterone (Option D) supports alveolar development in preparation for milk production during pregnancy but its levels drop significantly after delivery to allow for the full action of prolactin. Therefore, prolactin is the essential hormone for both the development of productive breast tissue and actual milk synthesis in the postnatal period. BIOCHEMISTRY

Question 20

A patient with a pituitary adenoma compressing the optic chiasma presents with visual field loss. Which specific visual field defect is most likely to be observed in this patient?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The optic chiasm contains the crossing fibers originating from the nasal retina of both eyes. These nasal retinal fibers are responsible for detecting the temporal (outer) fields of vision. A pituitary adenoma typically enlarges and compresses the central portion of the chiasm from below. This pressure disrupts the crossing nasal fibers, leading to a loss of the temporal visual fields in both eyes, a condition known as bitemporal hemianopia. Homonymous hemianopia (Option C) and homonymous anopia (Option B) result from lesions in the optic tract or radiations, where corresponding halves of the visual field are lost (e.g., both right fields). Quadrantanopia (Option D) occurs with partial lesions of the optic radiations in the temporal or parietal lobes. Thus, bitemporal field loss is the classic sign of a chiasmal lesion.

Question 21

A man presents with high VLDL and chylomicrons, with eruptive xanthomas. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The presentation of elevated VLDL and chylomicrons signifies extremely high triglyceride levels. This profile is characteristic of Familial Hypertriglyceridemia (Type IV Hyperlipoproteinemia). The massive triglyceride elevation results in "eruptive xanthomas," which are small, yellowish skin papules appearing suddenly on the skin. This condition also carries a significant risk of acute pancreatitis. Familial hypercholesterolemia (Option D) involves isolated high LDL and total cholesterol, typically presenting with tendon xanthomas rather than eruptive ones. Familial dysbetalipoproteinemia (Option B) involves high IDL and would show palmar xanthomas. Familial hyperlipidemia (Option A) is a general term rather than the specific biochemical entity described. Because the eruptive xanthomas and the specific elevation of triglyceride-rich VLDL and chylomicrons are described, Familial Hypertriglyceridemia is the most accurate clinical diagnosis. Management focuses on fibrates and lifestyle changes.

Question 22

A breastfed infant presents with lethargy, hepatomegaly, and cataract formation. Deficiency of which enzyme is most likely responsible for this clinical presentation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The clinical triad of lethargy, hepatomegaly, and cataracts shortly after starting breastfeeding is diagnostic of classic galactosemia. This inborn error of metabolism is caused by a deficiency in Galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase (GALT/GALPUT). The deficiency leads to a toxic accumulation of galactose-1-phosphate in the liver and galactitol in the lens of the eye. Untreated cases risk severe mental retardation, E. coli sepsis, and liver failure. Galactokinase deficiency (Option A) causes isolated cataracts without the severe hepatic or systemic symptoms seen here. Aldolase B deficiency (Option C) results in hereditary fructose intolerance, which presents after the introduction of fruit or formula containing sucrose rather than breastfeeding. Fructokinase deficiency (Option D) is a benign condition known as essential fructosuria. Management of classic galactosemia requires the immediate and permanent withdrawal of galactose and lactose from the infant's diet.

Question 23

Which vitamin deficiency can lead to lactic acidosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Thiamine (Vitamin B1) serves as an essential cofactor for the enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase. This enzyme is responsible for converting pyruvate into acetyl-CoA to enter the Krebs cycle. In states of thiamine deficiency, this conversion is blocked, causing pyruvate to accumulate and shunt toward the production of lactate. This metabolic shift results in lactic acidosis, which is commonly seen in severe thiamine deficiency syndromes like beriberi and Wernicke's encephalopathy. Riboflavin (Option B) is involved in the electron transport chain but its deficiency does not typically drive lactic acid production. Niacin (Option C) is a precursor for NAD, and its deficiency leads to pellagra. Biotin (Option D) is a cofactor for carboxylases; while its absence causes metabolic issues, it is not the primary cause of lactic acidosis. Thus, thiamine is the specific vitamin required to prevent the toxic buildup of lactate.

Question 24

Which of the following enzymes is responsible for the immortality of cancer cells?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Telomeres are protective caps at the ends of chromosomes that shorten with each cell division, eventually leading to cellular senescence or death. Telomerase is the enzyme responsible for maintaining telomere length by adding repetitive sequences back onto the chromosome ends. While telomerase activity is low in most adult somatic cells, its overexpression in cancer cells allows them to bypass the normal aging process and divide indefinitely. Topoisomerase (Option B) manages DNA structural tension by inducing strand breaks during replication. Helicase (Option C) unwinds the double helix to allow access for replication machinery. RNA polymerase (Option D) is essential for synthesizing RNA during transcription but does not directly influence the replicative lifespan of the cell. Consequently, the activation of telomerase is the fundamental mechanism that confers "immortality" upon malignant cells, facilitating uncontrolled tumor growth.

Question 25

Which of the following acts as a major intracellular antioxidant and helps in detoxifying reactive oxygen species?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Glutathione is the primary intracellular antioxidant responsible for neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS). It is a tripeptide composed of glutamate, cysteine, and glycine. Glutathione detoxifies ROS by donating electrons, converting harmful radicals into less reactive compounds, and is also essential for regenerating other antioxidants like vitamins C and E. While other enzymes are involved in the antioxidant defense, they have more specific roles: Superoxide dismutase (Option C) specifically converts superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide. Catalase (Option B) then converts that hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. Peroxidase (Option D) breaks down peroxides using reducing agents, often utilizing glutathione as a donor. Because glutathione acts as the central hub for electron donation and cellular redox balance, it is classified as the major intracellular antioxidant.

Question 26

Which of the following is pro-apoptotic?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Apoptosis is regulated by the Bcl-2 family of proteins, which act as a rheostat for cell survival. These proteins are divided into anti-apoptotic and pro-apoptotic members. Bax (along with Bak and Bok) is a pro-apoptotic member. It functions by promoting mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), which allows the release of cytochrome c into the cytoplasm to activate the caspase cascade. A useful mnemonic is "Bax and Bak Break" the membrane. Bcl-2 (Option A), Bcl-xL (Option B), and Mcl-1 (Option C) are all anti-apoptotic proteins. They work to preserve mitochondrial membrane integrity and prevent the release of death signals, effectively promoting cell survival. Therefore, Bax is the specific protein among the options that actively triggers the programmed cell death pathway.

Question 27

A 2-year-old child presents with fever and lymphadenopathy. Histopathological examination (H&E) shows hallmark cells, and a diagnosis of anaplastic large cell lymphoma is made. Which chromosomal translocation is most commonly associated with this condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Anaplastic Large Cell Lymphoma (ALCL) is highly associated with the t(2;5)(p23;q35) chromosomal translocation. This translocation results in the fusion of the nucleophosmin (NPM) gene on chromosome 5 with the anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) gene on chromosome 2. The resulting NPM-ALK fusion protein leads to the constitutive activation of ALK tyrosine kinase, which drives malignant cell proliferation. Histologically, ALCL is identified by "hallmark cells"—large pleomorphic cells with characteristic horseshoe-shaped nuclei. Option A (t(8;14)) and Option B (t(8;22)) are associated with Burkitt lymphoma, involving the MYC gene. Option D (t(14;18)) is the hallmark of follicular lymphoma, involving BCL2 overexpression. Therefore, the t(2;5) translocation is the definitive genetic finding for ALK-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma.

Question 28

A 6-month-old infant presents with lethargy, vomiting, and poor feeding following a period of prolonged fasting. Laboratory evaluation shows hypoglycemia with low ketone levels and mild hepatomegaly, suggesting a disorder of fat metabolism. What is the most likely underlying diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

This presentation is the classic clinical hallmark of MCAD deficiency, the most common fatty acid beta-oxidation defect. Normally, during fasting, the body switches to fat oxidation to produce energy and ketones. In MCAD deficiency, the inability to break down medium-chain fats leads to hypoketotic hypoglycemia and fat accumulation in the liver (mild hepatomegaly). Her's disease (Option A) causes fasting hypoglycemia and hepatomegaly, but ketone production remains normal because fat oxidation is intact. Hereditary fructose intolerance (Option C) occurs after fructose ingestion, not fasting. Von Gierke disease (Option D) presents with severe hypoglycemia and lactic acidosis, but ketones are typically normal or even high rather than low. Thus, the specific failure to produce ketones during a hypoglycemic fasting state identifies MCAD deficiency.

Question 29

A 2-month-old infant presents with poor feeding, vomiting, and lethargy. Laboratory investigations reveal hyperammonemia along with increased urinary orotic acid levels. Which enzyme deficiency is most likely responsible for this condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Hyperammonemia combined with elevated urinary orotic acid is diagnostic of Ornithine Transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency, also known as Type 2 Hyperammonemia. This X-linked disorder prevents the conversion of carbamoyl phosphate and ornithine into citrulline within the mitochondria. The resulting buildup of carbamoyl phosphate shunts into the pyrimidine synthesis pathway, leading to high orotic acid levels. Argininosuccinate lyase deficiency (Option D) causes hyperammonemia with elevated citrulline/argininosuccinate rather than orotic acid. Thus, OTC deficiency is the only condition matching this metabolic profile.

Question 31

A patient has a long arm span, hypermobile joints and hypermobile lens. What is the defective protein?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The symptoms of a long arm span relative to height, joint hypermobility, and ectopia lentis (lens subluxation) are classic for Marfan syndrome. Question 32

A patient with multiple colonic polyps is diagnosed with colorectal carcinoma and has a strong family history suggestive of Hereditary Non-Polyposis Colorectal Cancer (HNPCC). Which DNA repair mechanism is most likely defective in this condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Hereditary Non-Polyposis Colorectal Cancer (HNPCC), also known as Lynch syndrome, is caused by a defect in the DNA mismatch repair (MMR) system. Mutations in genes like hMSH2 (the most common) or hMSH1 prevent the correction of base-pairing errors during replication, leading to microsatellite instability and a high risk of malignancy. Nucleotide excision repair (Option A) fixes bulky lesions like those from UV radiation and is defective in xeroderma pigmentosum. Base excision repair (Option C) corrects small, non-bulky lesions caused by oxidation. Double-strand break repair (Option D) handles major chromosomal breaks and is linked to conditions like Ataxia-Telangiectasia or BRCA mutations. Because HNPCC is the specific diagnostic entity described, the mismatch repair pathway is the definitive biochemical defect. This highlights the importance of genetic screening for patients with early-onset colorectal cancers and significant family histories.

Question 33

Tau protein inclusions are involved in:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Alzheimer's disease is pathologically characterized by the accumulation of two specific proteins: amyloid-beta (Aβ) and tau. Amyloid-beta forms extracellular plaques, while hyperphosphorylated tau protein aggregates into intracellular neurofibrillary tangles within neurons. This proteinopathy leads to significant neuronal loss and cortical atrophy. Huntington's disease (Option B) involves the accumulation of mutant huntingtin protein, not tau. Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) (Option C) is typically linked to SOD1 mutations or TDP-43 inclusions rather than tau. CNS Lymphoma (Option D) is a malignancy of B-cells and is not associated with neurodegenerative protein inclusions. The presence of tau tangles specifically contributes to the cytoskeletal disruption and subsequent dementia seen in Alzheimer's patients. Microscopic visualization of these tangles remains a hallmark for post-mortem confirmation of the disease.

Question 34

In Barrett's oesophagus, which of the following epithelial transformations occurs?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Barrett's oesophagus is a condition where the normal stratified squamous epithelium of the lower oesophagus is replaced by intestinal-type columnar epithelium. This process, known as intestinal metaplasia, occurs as a complication of chronic Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD). The histological diagnosis requires the presence of goblet cells, which contain mucous vacuoles that appear blue with Alcian blue staining. Identifying this transformation is vital because it significantly increases the risk of developing oesophageal adenocarcinoma. Option A describes the squamous metaplasia seen in the bronchi of smokers. Option C is seen in the bladder under chronic irritation. Option D is not characteristic of oesophageal pathology. Endoscopically, Barrett's appears as red, velvety mucosa extending upward from the gastro-oesophageal junction.

Question 35

A patient with renal disease undergoes a kidney biopsy. Congo red staining of the tissue reveals deposits exhibiting apple-green birefringence under polarized light. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The demonstration of extracellular proteinaceous deposits that exhibit apple-green birefringence when stained with Congo red and viewed under polarized light is the pathognomonic finding for amyloidosis. In the context of renal disease, this often signifies AA (secondary) amyloidosis resulting from chronic inflammation. Under standard light microscopy, these deposits appear as amorphous, eosinophilic substances. Option B (Minimal change disease) shows normal glomeruli on light microscopy and foot process effacement on electron microscopy, but no amyloid deposits. Option C (Diabetic nephropathy) is characterized by nodular glomerulosclerosis (Kimmelstiel-Wilson lesions). Option D (Membranous nephropathy) features a thickened basement membrane with subepithelial "spikes". Neither diabetes nor membranous disease shows the specific staining properties of amyloid. Thus, the Congo red staining property definitively confirms the presence of amyloid fibrils in the tissue.

Question 36

A 25-year-old man presents with headaches, visual disturbances, and hypertension. MRI of the brain reveals a cerebellar hemangioblastoma, and abdominal imaging shows multiple renal cysts along with a solid renal mass suggestive of renal cell carcinoma. There is a family history of similar findings, as his father died of a brain tumor in his 40s. Which chromosomal abnormality is most likely associated with this condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

This patient’s presentation of cerebellar hemangioblastomas and renal cell carcinoma (RCC), along with a family history of similar findings, is diagnostic of Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease. VHL is an autosomal dominant condition caused by a mutation or deletion in the VHL tumor suppressor gene located on chromosome 3p25-26. A deletion on 13q (Option A) results in the loss of the RB1 gene, causing retinoblastoma. A deletion on 17p (Option B) involves the loss of TP53, leading to Li-Fraumeni syndrome. Trisomy 12 (Option D) is a common cytogenetic abnormality found in chronic lymphocytic leukemia. The specific combination of central nervous system hemangioblastomas and clear cell renal carcinoma is pathognomonic for a defect on the short arm of chromosome 3.

Question 37

Mutation in which of the following genes is most commonly associated with medullary thyroid carcinoma?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is a neuroendocrine tumor arising from parafollicular C cells, and it is most commonly associated with mutations in the RET proto-oncogene. These mutations are found in both sporadic cases and familial syndromes such as Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia type 2 (MEN 2). RAS mutations (Option A) are more typically seen in follicular thyroid adenomas and carcinomas. BRAF mutations (Option B) are the most common genetic alterations found in papillary thyroid carcinoma. TP53 mutations (Option D) are strongly associated with highly aggressive anaplastic thyroid carcinoma. Because RET activation is the primary driver of MTC, genetic testing for this mutation is essential for managing patients and screening their relatives.

Question 38

Hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC), also known as Lynch syndrome, results from mutations in which category of genes?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Lynch syndrome (HNPCC) is caused by germline mutations in DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes, such as MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, or PMS2. A failure in these proteins prevents the correction of base-pairing errors during replication, leading to microsatellite instability (MSI) and a high risk of colorectal and extracolonic cancers. The APC gene (Option A) is a tumor suppressor mutated in Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP). The MYC proto-oncogene (Option C) is involved in Burkitt lymphoma. TERT1 mutations (Option D) affect telomerase activity and are linked to other familial cancers but not HNPCC. Therefore, the hallmark of Lynch syndrome is a defect in the MMR system, resulting in the characteristic MSI observed in these tumors.

Question 39

Basophilic stippling of red blood cells is most characteristically seen in which of the following conditions?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Basophilic stippling (punctate basophilia) is the presence of multiple blue pinhead dots in red blood cells, representing the pathological accumulation of ribosomes. This is a hallmark haematological finding in chronic lead toxicity (plumbism). Lead inhibits the enzyme 5-pyrimidine nucleotidase, preventing the normal degradation of ribosomes. Iron deficiency anaemia (Option A) typically presents with microcytic, hypochromic cells but without stippling. Megaloblastic anaemia (Option C) features macrocytosis and hypersegmented neutrophils. Hereditary spherocytosis (Option D) is characterized by small, dense spherocytes and an increased MCHC. While basophilic stippling can occasionally be seen in thalassemia and sideroblastic anemia, it remains most pathognomonic for lead poisoning in a diagnostic context.

Question 40

A 58-year-old man with a history of multiple sexual partners presents with anorexia and jaundice. Liver biopsy reveals hepatocytes with a characteristic ground-glass appearance. What is the most probable diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The biopsy finding of ground-glass hepatocytes in a patient with clinical jaundice and risk factors like multiple sexual partners is pathognomonic for chronic Hepatitis B infection. This ground-glass appearance is caused by the massive accumulation of Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) within the smooth endoplasmic reticulum of the infected cells. Hepatitis A (Option A) is typically transmitted via the feco-oral route and shows hepatocyte ballooning rather than ground-glass cells. Hepatitis C (Option C) histology usually features lymphoid aggregates in the portal tracts and steatosis. In Hepatitis D (Option D), any observed ground-glass cells are actually a result of the co-existing HBV component. Therefore, ground-glass opacity is the definitive histological signature of chronic HBV infection.

Question 41

A diabetic man presents with seizures. Radiological imaging reveals a lesion, and histopathological examination shows liquefactive necrosis. What is the most important underlying mechanism responsible for this finding?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Liquefactive necrosis is the standard type of necrosis observed in central nervous system (brain) tissue following infection or ischemia. The primary mechanism responsible is enzymatic degradation. Because the brain is rich in lipids and lacks a supportive fibrous stroma, the hydrolytic lysosomal enzymes released by activated microglia and infiltrating neutrophils quickly digest the dead tissue into a liquid mass. While ischemia (Option C) triggers the process, the actual transformation into liquefactive necrosis is due to the enzymatic action rather than the lack of blood flow itself. Mechanical injury (Option B) causes hemorrhage or trauma but not the classic histological pattern of liquefaction. Increased intracranial pressure (Option D) leads to herniation but not this specific type of necrosis. Thus, enzymatic digestion is the critical driver of the liquefactive process.

Question 42

In a population with a mean blood glucose level of 105 mg/dL and a standard deviation of 10 mg/dL, assuming a normal distribution, what range of blood glucose values would be expected to include approximately 95% of individuals?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

In a normal distribution (Gaussian distribution), approximately 95% of all data points fall within ±2 standard deviations (SD) from the mean (more precisely ±1.96 SD). Given a mean of 105 mg/dL and an SD of 10 mg/dL, the calculation for the 95% range is: Mean ± (2 × SD) = 105 ± (2 × 10) = 105 ± 20. This results in a range of 85 to 125 mg/dL. Option B (101–110) covers a much narrower range of only ±0.5 SD. Option C (90–125) is an asymmetric range and does not follow the empirical rule. Option D (95–115) represents only ±1 SD from the mean, which accounts for approximately 68% of the population rather than 95%. Therefore, 85–125 mg/dL is the statistically correct range for a 95% confidence interval.

Question 43

A patient with hypertension, tachycardia, and early renal involvement is started on an angiotensin receptor blocker (telmisartan). By which mechanism does this drug most effectively help in reducing heart rate and blood pressure?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Telmisartan is an Angiotensin II Receptor Blocker (ARB) that acts as a selective competitive antagonist at AT1 receptors. Its primary mechanism for lowering blood pressure is through potent vasodilation, which results in a decrease in peripheral vascular resistance. By reducing systemic pressure, ARBs can also lead to a secondary reduction in reflex sympathetic activation, which helps improve tachycardia. ARBs do not directly decrease cardiac output (Option B), as they lack significant negative inotropic properties. They are not loop diuretics (Option C); these belong to an entirely different drug class. Option D (beta-1 receptor blockade) is the mechanism of beta-blockers like propranolol, not ARBs. Telmisartan is particularly useful in patients with renal involvement because it reduces intraglomerular pressure, providing a renoprotective effect.

Question 44

Which medication is preferred for preoperative blood pressure control in a patient diagnosed with pheochromocytoma?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The standard of care for the preoperative management of pheochromocytoma is the establishment of an effective alpha-adrenergic blockade to control hypertension and prevent a hypertensive crisis during surgery. Phenoxybenzamine, a long-acting, non-selective, and irreversible alpha-blocker, is the classic drug preferred for this purpose. Crucially, the alpha-blockade must be established first before any beta-blockers are added. Propranolol (Option A) should only be introduced after alpha-blockade to manage tachycardia, as giving a beta-blocker alone can cause "unopposed alpha stimulation," leading to dangerous blood pressure spikes. Prazosin (Option C) is a selective alpha-1 blocker that can be used but is not the traditional standard. Labetalol (Option D) blocks both alpha and beta receptors but is not used as the initial drug because its beta-blocking component is much stronger than its alpha-blocking effect.

Question 45

A woman being treated with cetirizine for allergic rhinitis complains of excessive drowsiness. Which alternative antihistamine would be most suitable due to its minimal sedative effects?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Cetirizine is a second-generation antihistamine that is generally non-sedating, but it can cause sleepiness in sensitive individuals. For such patients, Fexofenadine is the best alternative. Fexofenadine is also a second-generation agent but has even lower penetration into the central nervous system, resulting in minimal to no sedative effects. Hydroxyzine (Option A), Diphenhydramine (Option B), and Promethazine (Option D) are all first-generation H1 receptor antagonists. These drugs are highly lipophilic, cross the blood-brain barrier easily, and are well-known for their potent sedative and hypnotic properties, making them entirely inappropriate as non-drowsy alternatives. Therefore, for a patient requiring clear daytime cognitive function while managing allergies, fexofenadine is the standard clinical choice.

Question 46

Which of the following drugs acts by inhibiting the catechol-O-methyltransferase enzyme?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Tolcapone is a medication used as adjunct therapy in Parkinson’s disease. Its specific mechanism of action is the inhibition of the catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT) enzyme. This enzyme is responsible for the metabolic breakdown of catecholamines, including levodopa and dopamine; inhibiting it prolongs the duration of action of these catechols in the CNS and peripheral tissues. Amantadine (Option A) works by increasing dopamine release and inhibiting its reuptake. Rotigotine (Option B) is a dopamine receptor agonist. Selegiline (Option D) is a selective monoamine oxidase-B (MAO-B) inhibitor that prevents the breakdown of dopamine within the brain. Because only tolcapone targets the COMT pathway, it is the correct answer for this biochemical mechanism. Note that tolcapone requires regular monitoring due to the risk of hepatotoxicity.

Question 47

A patient presents with blackish discoloration of the nasal cavity and posterior buccal mucosa and has a history of prolonged high-dose steroid use. What is the most appropriate drug for treating this condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The presence of black necrotic tissue in the nasal and oral cavities of an immunosuppressed patient (high-dose steroid user) is highly diagnostic of invasive mucormycosis. This is a surgical and medical emergency. The standard first-line antifungal agent for mucormycosis is systemic Amphotericin B (preferably in its lipid formulation). Amphotericin B is a potent broad-spectrum fungicidal drug that remains the only agent with reliable efficacy against the Mucorales fungi. Fluconazole (Option A) and Itraconazole (Option C) are not effective against these aggressive fungi and should never be used as primary therapy for this condition. Griseofulvin (Option D) is only used for superficial skin infections (dermatophytes). Management must include both immediate administration of Amphotericin B and urgent surgical debridement of the necrotic tissue to prevent intracranial extension.

Question 48

A patient is brought to the emergency department with clinical features suggestive of opioid poisoning. What is the antidote of choice in this situation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Opioid poisoning classically presents with a triad of unconsciousness, respiratory depression, and pinpoint pupils (miosis). The definitive antidote of choice is Naloxone. Naloxone is a competitive opioid receptor antagonist that rapidly displaces opioids from their receptors, reversing the life-threatening respiratory depression within minutes of intravenous administration. Flumazenil (Option A) is the specific antagonist for benzodiazepine overdose, not opioids. Atropine (Option B) is an anticholinergic used for organophosphate poisoning or bradycardia. Physostigmine (Option D) is used for reversing anticholinergic toxicity, such as an atropine overdose. Because Naloxone specifically and rapidly targets the opioid pathway, it is the mandated intervention in any suspected narcotic overdose emergency.

Question 49

What is Zileuton's mechanism of action?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Zileuton is a pharmacological agent used primarily for the maintenance treatment of asthma. Its specific mechanism of action is the inhibition of 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX). This enzyme is responsible for the first step in converting arachidonic acid into inflammatory leukotrienes (LTB4, LTC4, LTD4, LTE4). By blocking this pathway, Zileuton effectively reduces the bronchoconstriction and airway inflammation that drive asthma symptoms. Cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibitors (Option A), such as NSAIDs, target the prostaglandin pathway rather than the leukotriene pathway. 5-phosphodiesterase inhibitors (Option C), like sildenafil, are used for erectile dysfunction or pulmonary hypertension. Preventing mast cell degranulation (Option D) is the mechanism of mast cell stabilizers like cromolyn sodium. Therefore, 5-LOX inhibition is the unique identifying action of Zileuton.

Question 50

A patient with hypertension remains poorly controlled despite treatment with antihypertensive medications, including a diuretic. Laboratory evaluation reveals hypokalemia. What should be the next step in the management of this patient?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Hypertension that remains resistant to standard therapy (including diuretics) and is accompanied by spontaneous hypokalemia is highly suggestive of primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome). In this condition, an adrenal adenoma or hyperplasia secretes excess aldosterone, which forces the kidneys to reabsorb sodium (raising blood pressure) and excrete potassium (causing hypokalemia). The most appropriate treatment is Spironolactone, which is an aldosterone receptor antagonist. It directly blocks the action of excess aldosterone, helping to both lower blood pressure and correct the low potassium levels. KCl (Option A) would only temporarily fix the potassium levels without addressing the underlying hypertensive cause. Torasemide (Option C) and Hydrochlorothiazide (Option D) are diuretics that increase potassium loss and would significantly worsen the patient's hypokalemia.
