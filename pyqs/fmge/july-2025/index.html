Question 12

A 35-year-old man wakes up after sleeping with his arm hanging over a chair and complains of pain and sensory symptoms. In this condition, which sequence correctly represents the order of susceptibility of different nerve fiber types to compression?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The clinical scenario describes "Saturday night palsy," a compression injury of the radial nerve. Nerve fibers have different sensitivities to external stressors based on their size and myelination. When subjected to pressure or mechanical compression, large myelinated fibers are the most susceptible, following the order: A > B > C. Fiber type A (specifically Alpha) is the most sensitive to pressure, while Type C is the least. Question 13

A woman from Delhi travels to Ladakh, a high- altitude area, and soon after arrival develops breathlessness, headache, and lightheadedness. What is the primary underlying mechanism responsible for these symptoms?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

At high altitudes, the partial pressure of oxygen is lower, which stimulates peripheral chemoreceptors to increase the rate of pulmonary ventilation (hyperventilation). This compensatory mechanism results in an excessive removal of carbon dioxide (CO2) from the blood. The loss of CO2, which acts as an acid in the body, causes the blood pH to rise, leading to respiratory alkalosis. Respiratory acidosis (Option A) occurs with hypoventilation, where CO2 is retained, not at high altitudes. Metabolic alkalosis (Option B) is typically caused by loss of stomach acid from vomiting or diuretic use. Metabolic acidosis (Option D) is associated with conditions like renal failure, diarrhea, or diabetic ketoacidosis. Therefore, the acute hyperventilation in response to hypoxia specifically drives a respiratory alkalotic state until the kidneys compensate by excreting excess bicarbonate over several days.

Malignant hyperthermia is a pharmacogenetic disorder of skeletal muscle. It is triggered by volatile anesthetics (like halothane) or succinylcholine in individuals with a mutation in the RYR1 gene. Question 17

Which among the following best describes 'Neuropraxia'?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Neuropraxia represents the mildest form of nerve injury according to Seddon's classification. It involves a temporary physiological conduction block caused by focal demyelination (damage to the nerve sheath) while the underlying axon remains completely intact. Because the axon is not severed, there is no Wallerian degeneration, and recovery is typically complete within six weeks once the sheath heals. Axonotmesis (Option B) involves a damaged axon within an intact connective tissue framework, leading to distal degeneration and a longer recovery period. Neurotmesis (Option C) is the most severe grade, characterized by complete disruption of both the axon and the supporting nerve sheaths; spontaneous recovery is impossible in these cases and surgical repair is mandated. Therefore, neuropraxia is specifically defined by the preservation of axonal continuity despite localized myelin dysfunction, leading to a favorable prognosis.

Question 18

As a neuron's diameter increases, what effect manifests?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The diameter of an axon is a major determinant of how rapidly an action potential can travel. A larger axonal diameter results in a significantly lower internal (axial) resistance to current flow. This reduced resistance allows local electrical currents to spread more efficiently and quickly along the interior of the axon, enabling faster depolarization of the adjacent membrane segments. Consequently, larger neurons exhibit a higher conduction velocity. This principle is vital in motor neurons where high-speed signal transmission is required. Option B is incorrect as velocity increases with size. Membrane resistance (Option C) per unit length actually decreases with larger surface areas, but the reduction in axial resistance dominates the change in speed. Action potential amplitude (Option D) is determined by ionic equilibrium potentials rather than physical axon size. Thus, diameter and speed are directly proportional.

Question 23

Which vitamin deficiency can lead to lactic acidosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Thiamine (Vitamin B1) serves as an essential cofactor for the enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase. This enzyme is responsible for converting pyruvate into acetyl-CoA to enter the Krebs cycle. In states of thiamine deficiency, this conversion is blocked, causing pyruvate to accumulate and shunt toward the production of lactate. This metabolic shift results in lactic acidosis, which is commonly seen in severe thiamine deficiency syndromes like beriberi and Wernicke's encephalopathy. Riboflavin (Option B) is involved in the electron transport chain but its deficiency does not typically drive lactic acid production. Niacin (Option C) is a precursor for NAD, and its deficiency leads to pellagra. Biotin (Option D) is a cofactor for carboxylases; while its absence causes metabolic issues, it is not the primary cause of lactic acidosis. Thus, thiamine is the specific vitamin required to prevent the toxic buildup of lactate.

Question 24

Telomeres are protective caps at the ends of chromosomes that shorten with each cell division, eventually leading to cellular senescence or death. Telomerase is the enzyme responsible for maintaining telomere length by adding repetitive sequences back onto the chromosome ends. While telomerase activity is low in most adult somatic cells, its overexpression in cancer cells allows them to bypass the normal aging process and divide indefinitely. Topoisomerase (Option B) manages DNA structural tension by inducing strand breaks during replication. Helicase (Option C) unwinds the double helix to allow access for replication machinery. RNA polymerase (Option D) is essential for synthesizing RNA during transcription but does not directly influence the replicative lifespan of the cell. Consequently, the activation of telomerase is the fundamental mechanism that confers "immortality" upon malignant cells, facilitating uncontrolled tumor growth.

Question 25

Glutathione is the primary intracellular antioxidant responsible for neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS). It is a tripeptide composed of glutamate, cysteine, and glycine.
