Question 22

Which ion plays the primary role in establishing the resting membrane potential?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The resting membrane potential (RMP) of a neuron, typically around -70 mV, is primarily established by the differential permeability of the cell membrane to various ions and the concentration gradients of these ions. At rest, the neuronal membrane is significantly more permeable to potassium ions () than to other ions like sodium () or chloride . This high permeability is due to the presence of numerous open leak potassium channels. Because the concentration of is much higher inside the cell than outside, ions tend to diffuse out of the cell down their concentration gradient. As these positively charged ions leave the cell, they make the inside of the membrane negative relative to the outside. This outflow continues until the electrical gradient pulling back into the cell balances the concentration gradient pushing it out, establishing the equilibrium potential for potassium, which is very close to the RMP.

Question 26

In a 35-year-old patient with intravascular hemolysis, the serum potassium level rises to 5.5 mEq/L. What effect will this have on the resting membrane potential in this patient?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The resting membrane potential (RMP) is primarily established by the potassium (K+) concentration gradient across the cell membrane, with a higher concentration inside the cell. The RMP is negative due to the constant efflux of positive K+ ions. Intravascular hemolysis releases K+ from red blood cells, causing hyperkalemia (increased extracellular K+). This reduces the concentration gradient between the intracellular and extracellular compartments. According to the Nernst and Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equations, a smaller K+ gradient reduces the driving force for K+ to exit the cell. With less positive charge leaving the cell, the interior becomes less negative, meaning the RMP shifts to a more positive value (e.g., from -70mV to -60mV). This depolarization moves the RMP closer to the threshold for firing an action potential, initially increasing excitability.

Question 30

Which of the following components is involved in the baroreceptor reflex?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The baroreceptor reflex is a crucial homeostatic mechanism for the short-term regulation of blood pressure. It operates on the principle of negative feedback. Baroreceptors are stretch receptors located in the walls of the aortic arch and the carotid sinuses. When blood pressure rises, these receptors are stretched, and they increase their firing rate to the cardiovascular centers in the brainstem. In response, the brainstem initiates a reflex that increases parasympathetic (vagal) activity and decreases sympathetic activity. This leads to a decrease in heart rate, cardiac contractility, and peripheral vascular resistance, which in turn lowers the blood pressure back towards its set point. Conversely, a drop in blood pressure reduces baroreceptor firing, triggering an increase in sympathetic activity to raise the pressure. This corrective action that opposes the initial stimulus is the definition of a negative feedback loop.

Question 33

A patient presents with rice-water stools. The toxin acts on which receptor in the intestine?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The clinical presentation of profuse, voluminous rice-watery stools is the hallmark of cholera, an infection caused by Vibrio cholerae. The disease is mediated by the powerful cholera toxin. The cholera toxin is an A-B type toxin, composed of one A (active) subunit and five B (binding) subunits. The B subunits are responsible for binding the toxin to the surface of the intestinal epithelial cells (enterocytes). The specific receptor on the host cell membrane to which the B subunits bind is the GM1 ganglioside. Once bound, the A subunit is internalized into the cell. It then acts as an ADP-ribosyltransferase, permanently activating the Gs alpha subunit of the adenylate cyclase enzyme. This leads to a massive, unregulated increase in intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP), which in turn causes the hypersecretion of chloride, sodium, and water into the intestinal lumen, resulting in severe secretory diarrhea.

Question 34

Hemiballismus is associated with a lesion of which nucleus?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Hemiballismus is a rare hyperkinetic movement disorder characterized by involuntary, violent, flinging movements of the limbs on one side of the body (contralateral to the lesion). The classic cause of hemiballismus is a lesion, most commonly a lacunar stroke or hemorrhage, affecting the subthalamic nucleus (STN). Question 35

Through which secondary messenger system do β₂-adrenergic receptors exert their effects?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Beta-2 adrenergic receptors are a type of G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR). Specifically, they are coupled to a stimulatory G-protein (Gs). Adenylate cyclase then catalyzes the conversion of ATP into the second messenger, cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP). Question 40

A young patient presents with muscle spasms, tingling in the hands and feet, seizures, and episodes of breathing difficulty due to laryngospasm, and laboratory investigations show an electrolyte abnormality. What is the most likely underlying cause of these symptoms?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Question 42

The ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) ratio describes the relationship between alveolar ventilation (V) and pulmonary capillary blood flow (Q).
