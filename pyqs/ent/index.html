What is the dose of diphtheria antitoxin?

The administration of Diphtheria Antitoxin (DAT) is a critical, life-saving intervention for the treatment of clinical diphtheria. Its purpose is to neutralize the circulating diphtheria toxin before it can bind to and damage host cells, particularly in the heart and nervous system. The dose of DAT is not based on the patient's weight but is determined by the clinical severity and duration of the illness. The recommended doses are as follows :For pharyngeal or laryngeal disease of less than 48 hours duration : 20,000 to 40,000 units.For nasopharyngeal disease : 40,000 to 60,000 units.For extensive disease of more than 3 days duration or with significant swelling (bull neck) : 80,000 to 120,000 units.Therefore, the therapeutic dose range falls within 10,000 to 100,000 IU (or even higher), making option B the correct range. The antitoxin should be administered as soon as the clinical diagnosis is suspected, without waiting for laboratory confirmation.

In stable epidemiological conditions (where incidence, duration, and population size are relatively constant), there is a well-established relationship between prevalence, incidence, and the duration of a disease. Prevalence refers to the total number of existing cases (new and old) in a population at a specific point in time, while incidence refers to the rate of new cases occurring over a period. The formula that connects these three variables is :Prevalence = Incidence × Average Duration of the Disease This formula is applicable for chronic diseases where the prevalence is low (typically less than 10%).Given the data in the question : Incidence = 4 cases per 1000 population per year.Average Duration = 2 years.Prevalence = (4 / 1000) × 2 = 8 / 1000.Therefore, the prevalence of the disease is estimated to be 8 per 1000 of the population. This means that at any given time, 8 out of every 1000 people in the population are expected to have the disease.

Question 6

What should the couple protection rate be for the Net Reproduction Rate (NRR) to equal 1?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The Net Reproduction Rate (NRR) is a demographic indicator that represents the average number of daughters that would be born to a woman (or a group of women) if she passed through her lifetime conforming to the age-specific fertility and mortality rates of a given year. An NRR of 1 signifies that each generation of mothers is producing exactly enough daughters to replace itself, leading to a stable population size over the long term (zero population growth). To achieve this replacement-level fertility, a certain proportion of couples in the reproductive age group must be using contraception. The Couple Protection Rate (CPR) is the percentage of eligible couples effectively protected against childbirth by one or another approved method of family planning. It has been estimated that a CPR of approximately 60% is required for a population to achieve an NRR of 1. This is a crucial target for national family planning programs aiming for population stabilization.

Question 8

What is the most appropriate method to display disease incidence over time?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The best graphical method for representing a trend or the change in a continuous variable over time is a line diagram (or line graph). To represent the incidence of a disease over time, the time periods (e.g., years, months, weeks) are plotted on the x-axis, and the corresponding incidence rates are plotted on the y-axis. The data points are then connected by a line. This visual representation makes it very easy to see the trend in the disease's occurrence—whether it is increasing, decreasing, or showing cyclical patterns over the specified period. A histogram (A) is used to display the frequency distribution of a single continuous variable. A bar diagram (D) is used to compare frequencies or means of different categorical groups. A scatter diagram (C) is used to show the relationship between two different continuous variables.

Question 10

In a diabetes mellitus (DM) screening program involving 1,000 individuals, 90 people tested positive on the screening test. However, a confirmatory gold-standard test showed that 100 individuals actually had the disease. What is the sensitivity of the screening test?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Sensitivity is a measure of a screening test's ability to correctly identify individuals who truly have the disease. It is calculated as the proportion of true positives among all individuals who actually have the disease. The formula for sensitivity is :Sensitivity = (True Positives) / (True Positives + False Negatives) In this problem : The total number of people who truly have the disease (as determined by the gold standard test) is 100. This is the denominator (True Positives + False Negatives).The number of people who tested positive with the screening test is 90. This represents the True Positives.We can infer that 10 people who had the disease were missed by the screening test (100 total diseased - 90 who tested positive = 10 False Negatives).Therefore, the sensitivity of the screening test is calculated as :Sensitivity = 90 / (90 + 10) = 90 / 100.This means the test correctly identifies 90% of all people who actually have diabetes mellitus.

Question 11

Which drug is used for mass chemoprophylaxis in meningococcal meningitis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Chemoprophylaxis for meningococcal meningitis is recommended for close contacts of a person with the disease to eradicate nasopharyngeal carriage of Neisseria meningitidis and prevent secondary cases. In situations of a large-scale outbreak, mass chemoprophylaxis may be considered. The drugs of choice for this purpose must be effective, easy to administer, and safe for large populations. Rifampicin has historically been a primary agent for mass chemoprophylaxis. It is highly effective at eradicating nasopharyngeal carriage and is given orally for two days. However, it has several contraindications (e.g., pregnancy, liver disease) and can lead to resistance. For this reason, other agents like ciprofloxacin (single oral dose) or ceftriaxone (single intramuscular injection) are now often preferred, especially for individual contacts. Nevertheless, rifampicin remains a recognized and effective option for mass prophylaxis. Penicillin is used for treatment, not prophylaxis, as it does not reliably eliminate the carrier state.

Question 13

Kala-azar occurs in all endemic regions except which one?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Kala-azar, or visceral leishmaniasis, is a vector-borne parasitic disease caused by Leishmania donovani and transmitted by sandflies. In India, the disease has historically been highly endemic in specific states in the eastern part of the country. The most heavily affected states, which account for the vast majority of cases in India, are Bihar, Jharkhand, West Bengal, and the eastern districts of Uttar Pradesh. These four states are the focus of the national Kala-azar elimination program. While sporadic cases may occur elsewhere, Assam, located in the northeastern region of India, is not considered a traditional endemic area for Kala-azar. The epidemiology of the disease is very focal, depending on the presence of the specific sandfly vector (Phlebotomus argentipes) and other environmental factors that are concentrated in the plains of the states mentioned above.

Question 15

Pasteurization is carried out at what temperature and for what duration?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Pasteurization is a process of heat treatment used to kill pathogenic microorganisms in foods and beverages, such as milk, to make them safe for consumption and to extend their shelf life. There are several standard time-temperature combinations for pasteurizing milk. One of the original methods is the Holder method, or low-temperature long-time (LTLT) pasteurization. Option (C) is close to the HTST method but has the wrong time. The other options list incorrect time-temperature combinations.

Question 17

When a susceptible person develops a disease within the incubation period after exposure to the primary case, what is this called?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The Secondary Attack Rate (SAR) is a measure of the infectivity or contagiousness of a disease and is used to evaluate control measures. It is defined as the number of new cases of a disease that occur among the susceptible contacts of a known primary case, within one incubation period of the disease. The formula is :SAR = (Number of new cases among contacts / Total number of susceptible contacts) × 100.This rate specifically measures the spread of a disease from the initial case (primary case) to those who were directly exposed to that case. It is a useful metric for investigating disease outbreaks in closed settings like households, schools, or barracks. The primary attack rate refers to the rate of disease among all those exposed during an outbreak. The case fatality rate is the proportion of cases that result in death.

Question 18

What is the name of the new online RNTCP software used to monitor the TB control programme?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

NIKSHAY (derived from Ni for end and Kshay for TB) is a web-based and mobile-based health information management system developed and implemented by the Government of India for the National Tuberculosis Elimination Program (NTEP), formerly known as RNTCP. It was launched in 2012 to comprehensively monitor and manage TB patients and control program activities across the country. NIKSHAY serves as a unified interface for all stakeholders, including public and private healthcare providers, laboratories, and program managers. It is used for the mandatory notification of all TB cases, tracking patients from diagnosis through treatment completion, managing drug supplies, and monitoring treatment adherence. The system also integrates features for direct benefit transfers (Nikshay Poshan Yojana) to support patients' nutritional needs. It is a critical digital tool in India's strategy to eliminate tuberculosis by 2025, enabling real-time surveillance and improving the quality of patient care and program management.

Question 20

78-year-old man with diabetes mellitus and hypertension presents with epistaxis. Initial measures, including nasal packing, fail to control the bleeding. Which artery should be ligated to effectively manage the bleeding?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

In cases of severe posterior epistaxis (nasal bleeding) that are refractory to conservative measures like nasal packing, surgical intervention is often required. The vast majority of significant posterior bleeds originate from the rich vascular network on the lateral nasal wall. The primary blood supply to this area comes from the sphenopalatine artery (SPA), which is the terminal branch of the internal maxillary artery. The SPA enters the nasal cavity through the sphenopalatine foramen and supplies most of the posterior nasal septum and lateral nasal wall. Endoscopic surgical ligation or cauterization of the sphenopalatine artery is the modern gold-standard procedure for controlling intractable posterior epistaxis. While the anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries (B, C) also contribute to the nasal blood supply, they are responsible for bleeding from the superior aspect of the nose and are ligated less commonly.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

This clinical presentation is classic for Juvenile Nasopharyngeal Angiofibroma (JNA). This is a benign but locally aggressive and highly vascular tumor that occurs almost exclusively in adolescent males. The cardinal symptoms are unilateral nasal obstruction and recurrent, profuse, spontaneous epistaxis. Question 25

An impaired gag reflex results from damage to which cranial nerves?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The gag reflex is a brainstem reflex that involves two primary cranial nerves. The afferent (sensory) limb of the reflex arc is mediated by the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX), which carries sensory information from the posterior tongue, pharynx, and soft palate. The efferent (motor) limb is primarily carried by the vagus nerve (CN X), which innervates the muscles of the soft palate and pharynx, causing them to contract and elevate during the gag response. A lesion affecting either the sensory input (CN IX) or the motor output (CN X) will result in an impaired or absent gag reflex. Therefore, the combination of CN IX and CN X is correct. The other nerve pairings are not involved in this specific reflex : CN V (sensation to anterior face), CN VI (lateral rectus muscle), CN VII (facial expression), CN VIII (hearing/balance), CN XI (sternocleidomastoid/trapezius).

Question 28

A child who has undergone tonsillectomy develops postoperative bleeding while resting in the ward. What is the most appropriate immediate step in management?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Post-tonsillectomy hemorrhage is a serious and potentially life-threatening complication that requires immediate and definitive management. While minor oozing can sometimes be managed conservatively, active bleeding in a child warrants returning the patient to the operating theater (OT). Once in the OT and under general anesthesia, the first and most crucial step is to remove all blood clots from the tonsillar fossa. A clot can mask the underlying bleeding vessel and prevent it from retracting and constricting. After the clot is evacuated, the specific bleeding point can be identified and controlled, usually by cauterization or re-ligation with sutures. Simple pressure packing is often insufficient for arterial bleeding.

Question 32

Which of the following features is not typical of tuberculous otitis media?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Tuberculous otitis media is a rare form of chronic suppurative otitis media caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It has several classic clinical features that distinguish it from the more common pyogenic forms. One of its most defining characteristics is a painless, profuse, and persistent otorrhea (ear discharge). The lack of pain, despite significant tissue destruction, is a major clinical clue. Other classic findings include the presence of multiple perforations of the tympanic membrane which may later coalesce, pale and sluggish granulations in the middle ear cleft, and severe destruction of the ossicular chain, leading to a significant conductive hearing loss. Facial nerve palsy is also more common than in typical otitis media. Therefore, painful otorrhea is the feature that is not characteristic; the discharge is classically described as painless.

Question 33

Partial or complete closure of the nasal cavity is performed as a treatment for which condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Atrophic rhinitis (ozena) is a chronic nasal condition characterized by progressive atrophy of the nasal mucosa and turbinate bones, leading to a paradoxically wide nasal cavity filled with foul-smelling crusts. The goal of surgical treatment is to narrow the abnormally wide nasal cavity to reduce crust formation and improve mucosal function. Young's operation is a surgical procedure that involves creating mucocutaneous flaps to achieve a complete closure of the nostril. The nostril is kept closed for several months to a year, allowing the nasal mucosa to rest, regenerate, and reverse some of the atrophic changes. A modified version of the procedure involves only a partial closure. These surgical narrowing procedures are specific to the management of severe, recalcitrant atrophic rhinitis and are not used for other forms of rhinitis like allergic or vasomotor rhinitis.

Question 34

What is the name given to the occipitofrontal view on an X-ray of the paranasal sinuses (PNS)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Plain X-rays for evaluating the paranasal sinuses involve specific projections to visualize the different sinuses without overlap. The occipitofrontal view, more commonly known as the Caldwell view, is a standard projection used for this purpose. It is taken with the patient's nose and forehead pressed against the film cassette and the X-ray beam angled at approximately 15 degrees caudally. This specific angulation projects the dense petrous ridges below the floor of the maxillary sinuses, providing an excellent, unobstructed view of the frontal sinuses and the ethmoid sinuses. In contrast, the Water's view (occipitomental view) is designed to provide the best visualization of the maxillary sinuses. The Towne's view is used for imaging the occipital bone and petrous pyramids.

Question 35

What is the term used for widening of the cartilaginous portion of the external auditory canal?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

A meatoplasty is a surgical procedure specifically designed to widen the meatus, or opening, of the external auditory canal. It is often performed in conjunction with mastoidectomy or other middle ear surgeries to improve aeration and drainage, allow for easier post-operative cleaning and inspection of the mastoid cavity, and prevent the accumulation of debris that can lead to recurrent infections.
