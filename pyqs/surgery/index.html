Question 1

A 2-year-old child presents to the emergency department with respiratory distress. Chest X-ray shows hyperinflation of the right lung with a normal left lung. Which of the following statements is correct?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The clinical history of sudden onset respiratory distress in a toddler, combined with the X-ray finding of unilateral hyperinflation, is highly suggestive of an inhaled foreign body in the bronchus. The foreign body can act as a ball-valve, allowing air to enter the lung during inspiration but trapping it during expiration, leading to air trapping and hyperinflation on the affected side. A key physical finding on auscultation would be focal wheezing or decreased air entry over the affected lung area (A). A complete airway obstruction (C) would lead to atelectasis (collapse), not hyperinflation. The treatment of choice (TOC) for removing a foreign body from the airway in a child is rigid bronchoscopy, not flexible bronchoscopy (B), as the rigid scope provides a better airway, superior optics, and larger channels for the retrieval instruments.

Question 5

Inspiratory stridor is most commonly associated with which type of lesion?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Stridor is a high-pitched, harsh sound produced by turbulent airflow through a partially obstructed airway. The character of the stridor can help to localize the level of the obstruction. Inspiratory stridor is characteristic of an extrathoracic airway obstruction, specifically at or above the level of the vocal cords (the glottis). Lesions in the supraglottic region, such as epiglottitis or laryngomalacia, are classic causes. During inspiration, the negative pressure generated in the airway tends to collapse the floppy supraglottic tissues inward, causing the obstruction and the characteristic inspiratory sound. In contrast, an intrathoracic obstruction (e.g., in the trachea or bronchus) typically causes expiratory stridor or wheezing, as the airway narrows during the positive pressure of expiration. A fixed obstruction, such as subglottic stenosis, often causes biphasic stridor (heard during both inspiration and expiration).

Question 9

What is the indication for a tracheostomy?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

A tracheostomy is a surgical procedure to create an opening into the trachea (windpipe) through the neck to establish a direct airway. The indications for tracheostomy can be broadly grouped into three categories : upper airway obstruction, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and poor pulmonary toilet (inability to clear secretions). A foreign body obstructing the airway at or above the level of the larynx that cannot be removed by other means (like the Heimlich maneuver or direct laryngoscopy) is an absolute indication for an emergent surgical airway. Tracheomalacia (C), or weakness of the tracheal cartilage, can be a complication of or an indication for tracheostomy, but obstruction is a more direct and acute reason. Vocal cord (A) and pharynx (B) replacement are not indications for this procedure.

Question 14

Blow out fracture of orbit most commonly involves:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

A blowout fracture is a specific type of orbital fracture that is caused by a sudden increase in intraorbital pressure from a blunt, non-penetrating trauma to the globe, such as from a fist or a ball. The force is transmitted to the orbital contents, and the pressure causes a fracture of one of the thin walls of the orbit, while the stronger orbital rim remains intact. The orbital floor (the roof of the maxillary sinus) is the most common site to be involved in a blowout fracture. The second most common site is the medial wall (the lamina papyracea of the ethmoid bone). These two walls are the thinnest and weakest parts of the bony orbit. The lateral wall and the roof are much thicker and stronger and are fractured much less frequently by this mechanism. A fracture of the floor can lead to entrapment of the inferior rectus muscle, causing restricted upward gaze, and enophthalmos (sunken eye).

Question 16

In which condition is 100-day glaucoma observed?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Neovascular glaucoma (NVG) is a severe, secondary form of glaucoma characterized by the growth of new, abnormal blood vessels (neovascularization) over the iris (rubeosis iridis) and into the trabecular meshwork in the anterior chamber angle. These vessels obstruct the outflow of aqueous humor, leading to a rapid and often dramatic rise in intraocular pressure. The most common cause of NVG is severe, widespread retinal ischemia. The ischemic retina releases vasoproliferative factors, primarily Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF), which stimulate the neovascularization. The two main underlying conditions that cause this level of ischemia are proliferative diabetic retinopathy and ischemic Central Retinal Vein Occlusion (CRVO). Following an ischemic CRVO, NVG typically develops after a latent period, classically around 3 months, or approximately 100 days, earning it the historical name 100-day glaucoma.

Question 26

A patient with a known diagnosis of anal carcinoma presents for follow-up with a mass near the anal opening. Which group of lymph nodes is most likely to be involved?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The lymphatic drainage of the anal canal is divided by the dentate (pectinate) line. Tumors located below the dentate line, which includes the anal margin and perianal skin, drain primarily to the superficial inguinal lymph nodes. This is because this lower portion of the anal canal is derived from the ectoderm and shares the same lymphatic drainage pathway as the skin of the lower trunk, perineum, and lower limbs. Since the question describes a mass "near the anal opening," it implies a lesion in this lower region. In contrast, tumors located above the dentate line drain upwards along the superior rectal vessels to the perirectal and then to the internal iliac lymph nodes (Option D). The external iliac nodes (Option C) become involved later. Therefore, for a distal anal canal carcinoma, the superficial inguinal lymph nodes are the first and most likely echelon of nodes to be involved.

After surgical resection of an oral malignancy, adjuvant (postoperative) radiotherapy is given to reduce the risk of locoregional recurrence. The decision is based on pathological risk factors. Question 36

Direct inguinal hernia which part is weak

A direct inguinal hernia occurs when abdominal contents protrude directly through a weakness in the posterior wall of the inguinal canal, medial to the inferior epigastric vessels. Chronic alcohol consumption leads to a significant increase in the hepatic NADH:NAD+ ratio due to the metabolism of ethanol. This altered redox state has several metabolic consequences. Question 86

In a patient who has undergone surgery for a parathyroid adenoma, what postoperative finding or test is used to confirm that the diseased parathyroid gland has been successfully and correctly removed?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

During a minimally invasive parathyroidectomy for a single adenoma, it is crucial to confirm that the hypersecreting gland has been successfully removed before concluding the operation. This is done using an intraoperative parathyroid hormone (IOPTH) assay. Parathyroid hormone (PTH) has a very short half-life of only 3 to 5 minutes. This property allows for a rapid assessment of surgical success. A baseline PTH level is drawn before the adenoma is excised. After the suspected adenoma is removed, another blood sample is drawn, typically at 5 minutes and 10 minutes post-excision. The most widely accepted criterion for a successful parathyroidectomy (known as the Miami criterion) is a greater than 50% reduction in the PTH level from the highest pre-excision baseline level at the 10-minute post-excision sample. A successful drop confirms that the source of the excess PTH has been removed.

Question 89

What is the most common pancreatic endocrine neoplasm?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PNETs) are rare tumors that can be functional (hormone-secreting) or non-functional. Among the functional PNETs, the most common type is the insulinoma. Insulinomas are tumors of the pancreatic beta cells that autonomously secrete excessive amounts of insulin. This leads to inappropriate hyperinsulinemia, which causes recurrent episodes of hypoglycemia, particularly during fasting or after exercise. The clinical presentation is characterized by Whipple's triad : symptoms of hypoglycemia (e.g., confusion, sweating, palpitations), a documented low plasma glucose level during the episode, and relief of symptoms upon administration of glucose. The vast majority (>90%) of insulinomas are benign and solitary. Gastrinomas are the second most common functional PNET, but they are more often located in the duodenum than the pancreas itself.

Question 95

The most common type of gallstone is :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Gallstones are classified into three main types: cholesterol stones, pigment stones, and mixed stones. The most common type worldwide is cholesterol stones, particularly in Western populations. These stones are primarily composed of cholesterol monohydrate crystals and form when bile becomes supersaturated with cholesterol. Factors that predispose to cholesterol stone formation include obesity, female sex, multiparity, advancing age, rapid weight loss, and estrogen exposure.Pure cholesterol stones are typically yellowish and radiolucent on X-ray. Although mixed stones (containing cholesterol along with calcium salts and bile pigments) are also common, they are generally considered variants of cholesterol stones.Pigment stones (including calcium bilirubinate stones) are more common in patients with chronic hemolysis, biliary infection, or cirrhosis and are less common overall compared to cholesterol stones.
