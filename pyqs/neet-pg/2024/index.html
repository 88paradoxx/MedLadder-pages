Question 1

What is the condition or stage of the oocyte after ovulation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

During fetal life, primary oocytes begin meiosis but are arrested in prophase I. This state persists until puberty. Just prior to ovulation, a surge of Luteinizing Hormone (LH) triggers the completion of meiosis I, resulting in an unequal division that produces a large secondary oocyte and a small first polar body. The secondary oocyte then immediately enters meiosis II but is arrested in metaphase II. It is this secondary oocyte, halted at metaphase II, that is expelled from the ovary during ovulation. The meiotic process will only resume and complete if the oocyte is successfully fertilized by a sperm. Therefore, the oocyte available for fertilization post-ovulation is a secondary oocyte arrested in metaphase II. Option A describes the pre-pubertal state, while options B and C are incorrect stages of meiotic arrest.

Question 2

Fracture at which site affects the longitudinal growth of a bone?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The longitudinal growth of long bones in children and adolescents occurs at the epiphyseal plate, also known as the growth plate or physis. This cartilaginous plate is situated between the epiphysis (the end of the bone) and the metaphysis. Growth occurs through a process of endochondral ossification, where cartilage cells within the plate proliferate, mature, and are eventually replaced by bone, thereby elongating the bone shaft (diaphysis). A fracture that involves the epiphyseal plate (a Salter-Harris fracture) can disrupt this delicate process. Damage to the proliferating chondrocytes can lead to premature cessation of growth, resulting in limb length discrepancy or angular deformities. Fractures of the diaphysis (Option B), epiphysis (Option C), or metaphysis (Option D) that do not involve the growth plate do not typically interfere with the bone's longitudinal growth potential.

Question 4

In an alcoholic patient who develops a wide-based gait and nystagmus following a road traffic accident, which cerebellar lobe is most likely involved?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The clinical signs of a wide-based gait (gait ataxia) and nystagmus are indicative of cerebellar dysfunction. In the context of chronic alcoholism, the most commonly affected part of the cerebellum is the anterior lobe, particularly its superior vermian portion. This area is responsible for coordinating movements and regulating posture of the limbs, especially the legs. Its degeneration due to the neurotoxic effects of alcohol leads to the characteristic unsteady, wide-based gait as the patient attempts to increase their stability. While the RTA is the acute event, the patient's underlying chronic alcoholism makes the anterior lobe particularly vulnerable and likely the source of these neurological signs. The flocculonodular lobe (Option A) is associated with truncal ataxia, while the dentate nucleus (Option B) is a deep nucleus involved in motor planning. Question 5

Correct answer: A

Horner syndrome results from a disruption of the oculosympathetic pathway, leading to a triad of miosis, ptosis, and anhidrosis. The ptosis seen in this syndrome is typically mild (1-2 mm). Question 7

A singer has difficulty producing high-pitched sounds, and examination reveals bowing of the right vocal cord. Injury to which muscle is most likely responsible for this finding?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The pitch of the voice is determined by the tension and length of the vocal cords. To produce high-pitched sounds, the vocal cords must be tensed and elongated. This action is the primary function of the cricothyroid muscle. It is the only intrinsic laryngeal muscle innervated by the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve. Contraction of the cricothyroid muscle tilts the thyroid cartilage forward, thereby stretching the vocal cords. Paralysis of this muscle, as suggested by the bowing of the right vocal cord and the inability to reach high pitches, indicates a lesion of the right external superior laryngeal nerve. The posterior cricoarytenoid (Option A) abducts (opens) the cords, the lateral cricoarytenoid (Option B) adducts (closes) them, and the thyroarytenoid (Option D) shortens and relaxes the cords, lowering the pitch.

Question 9

The membrane covering an omphalocele is formed from which embryological layer or tissue?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

An omphalocele is characterized by the herniation of abdominal viscera through a midline defect at the umbilicus, with the contents being covered by a protective sac. This sac is a bilaminar structure. The outer layer of the sac is composed of amnion, which is the innermost membrane of the placenta that encloses the embryo. The inner layer of the sac is the peritoneum. Between these two layers is Wharton's jelly. This covering distinguishes it from gastroschisis, where the herniated viscera are exposed directly to the amniotic fluid without a sac. The chorion (Option B) is the outermost fetal membrane and is not involved. Mesoderm (Option C) and endoderm (Option D) are embryonic germ layers that give rise to the organs themselves but not the specific outer covering of the omphalocele sac.

Question 10

In a patient who has fecal matter discharging from the umbilicus, what is the most probable diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The discharge of fecal matter from the umbilicus indicates a patent connection between the intestine and the umbilicus, known as a vitelline fistula or omphalomesenteric duct fistula. This duct normally connects the midgut to the yolk sac in the embryo and obliterates by the seventh week of gestation. A persistent remnant of this duct on the intestinal side is known as Meckel's diverticulum, which is a type of ileal diverticulum. When the entire duct remains patent, it forms a fistula. Therefore, the underlying pathology for a vitelline fistula is a persistent ileal diverticulum connected to the umbilicus. A urachal fistula (Option A) connects the bladder to the umbilicus and would present with urine discharge. Omphalocele (Option C) and gastroschisis (Option D) are congenital abdominal wall defects, not fistulas presenting with discharge in this manner.

Question 13

In a patient with sciatica who has tenderness of the hamstring muscles, which nerve supplies a hybrid muscle that is partially spared in this condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Sciatica occurs due to irritation or compression of the sciatic nerve, which commonly causes pain along the posterior thigh and may affect the hamstring muscles. Most hamstring muscles—semitendinosus, semimembranosus, and the long head of biceps femoris—are supplied by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve.However, the biceps femoris is considered a hybrid muscle because its two heads receive different nerve supplies. The long head of the biceps femoris is supplied by the tibial division of the sciatic nerve, while the short head of the biceps femoris is supplied by the common peroneal (fibular) division of the sciatic nerve.In cases of sciatica affecting the tibial component, the muscles innervated by the tibial part may become painful or weak. However, the short head of the biceps femoris, supplied by the common peroneal nerve, may remain partially functional. Because of this dual innervation, the muscle is described as hybrid and can be partially spared in certain sciatic nerve injuries.Therefore, the nerve responsible for supplying the spared portion of this hybrid muscle is the common peroneal nerve.

Question 15

Which anatomical location is the most frequent site for a congenital diaphragmatic hernia?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH) is a birth defect where there is an opening in the diaphragm, allowing abdominal organs to move into the chest cavity. The vast majority of CDH cases occur in the posterolateral region of the diaphragm. This type is known as a Bochdalek hernia, and it results from the failure of the pleuroperitoneal fold to close during fetal development. Bochdalek hernias account for about 85-90% of all CDH cases and are most common on the left side. Anterolateral defects (Option D), known as Morgagni hernias, are much less frequent. Hernias through the central tendon (Option A) or esophageal hiatus (part of the crura, Option C) are also rare compared to the posterolateral location. The herniation can impede lung development, leading to life-threatening pulmonary hypoplasia.

This combination of symptoms is classic for a lesion of the median nerve, typically at the level of the wrist (e.g., carpal tunnel syndrome). Question 22

By which mechanism does caffeine interfere with normal sleep?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Caffeine's primary mechanism for promoting wakefulness is its action as a competitive antagonist at adenosine receptors, specifically the A1 and A2A subtypes. Adenosine is a neuro-motor that accumulates in the brain throughout the day, promoting sleepiness by inhibiting wakefulness-promoting neurons. This leads to increased neuronal activity and the release of other neurotransmitters like dopamine and norepinephrine, resulting in heightened alertness and a delay in sleep onset. Question 23

Which ion plays the primary role in establishing the resting membrane potential?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The resting membrane potential (RMP) of a neuron, typically around -70 mV, is primarily established by the differential permeability of the cell membrane to various ions and the concentration gradients of these ions. At rest, the neuronal membrane is significantly more permeable to potassium ions () than to other ions like sodium () or chloride . This high permeability is due to the presence of numerous open leak potassium channels. Because the concentration of is much higher inside the cell than outside, ions tend to diffuse out of the cell down their concentration gradient. As these positively charged ions leave the cell, they make the inside of the membrane negative relative to the outside. This outflow continues until the electrical gradient pulling back into the cell balances the concentration gradient pushing it out, establishing the equilibrium potential for potassium, which is very close to the RMP.

Sunburn causes tissue damage and inflammation, which leads to peripheral sensitization of nerve endings. Question 29

In a patient with phenylketonuria who has normal phenylalanine hydroxylase activity, deficiency of which substance is responsible for the condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Classic phenylketonuria (PKU) is caused by a deficiency of the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH), which converts phenylalanine to tyrosine. However, a small percentage of patients with hyperphenylalaninemia have normal PAH levels. This is known as atypical or malignant PKU. In these cases, the defect lies in the synthesis or recycling of the essential cofactor for PAH, tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4). The enzyme dihydrobiopterin reductase (DHPR) is responsible for regenerating BH4 from its oxidized form, dihydrobiopterin (BH2). A deficiency in DHPR leads to a lack of functional BH4, which not only impairs the PAH enzyme but also tyrosine hydroxylase and tryptophan hydroxylase, which are required for the synthesis of neurotransmitters like dopamine and serotonin. This results in a more severe neurological phenotype than classic PKU.

Question 31

In a patient receiving phenobarbitone therapy, which complex of the electron transport chain is inhibited by this drug?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Barbiturates, such as phenobarbitone and amobarbital, are known inhibitors of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Similar to rotenone, they act by blocking the electron transfer within Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase). They specifically inhibit the transfer of electrons from the iron-sulfur centers within Complex I to ubiquinone (coenzyme Q). This inhibition disrupts the flow of electrons originating from NADH, thereby reducing the proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane and severely impairing ATP synthesis through oxidative phosphorylation. This action contributes to the central nervous system depression seen with barbiturate overdose. The other complexes (II, III, and IV) are not directly inhibited by phenobarbitone, although the overall function of the chain is compromised due to the block at Complex I.

Question 32

Which of the following substances are metabolites produced during methanol poisoning?

Show answer and explanation

Question 34

The presence of xanthomas over the Achilles tendon in a patient is most suggestive of which underlying diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The presence of tendon xanthomas, particularly on the Achilles tendon or extensor tendons of the hands, is a pathognomonic sign of Familial Hypercholesterolemia (FH). FH is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder caused by mutations in the LDL receptor gene, leading to impaired clearance of Low-Density Lipoprotein (LDL) from the circulation. This results in markedly elevated levels of LDL cholesterol from birth. Question 38

A patient’s relatives inform the treating doctor via social media that the urinary coproporphyrin test is positive. What is the most likely underlying cause or diagnosis suggested by this finding?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The presence of elevated urinary coproporphyrin III is a key biochemical marker for lead poisoning. Lead interferes with the heme synthesis pathway by inhibiting several key enzymes. One of these enzymes is coproporphyrinogen oxidase. Inhibition of this enzyme leads to the accumulation of its substrate, coproporphyrinogen III, which is then oxidized to coproporphyrin III and excreted in the urine in large amounts. Another enzyme inhibited by lead is ALA dehydratase, leading to increased urinary aminolevulinic acid (ALA), which is an even more sensitive marker. Therefore, a positive urinary coproporphyrin test, especially in a context with potential exposure, should raise strong suspicion for lead poisoning. Asbestosis (Option B) and silicosis (Option C) are pneumoconioses and do not cause this biochemical abnormality. Mercury poisoning (Option D) has different toxic manifestations.

Question 41

A patient develops a skin rash that worsens with exposure to sunlight. Which DNA repair mechanism is defective or involved in this condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Extreme photosensitivity, a skin rash exaggerated by sun exposure, is the hallmark symptom of Xeroderma Pigmentosum (XP). This is a rare autosomal recessive genetic disorder where the ability to repair DNA damage caused by ultraviolet (UV) radiation is deficient. UV light primarily causes the formation of pyrimidine dimers (specifically, thymine dimers) in DNA, which distort the double helix and block replication and transcription. The primary mechanism for repairing such bulky, helix-distorting lesions is Nucleotide Excision Repair (NER). In XP, one of the several proteins involved in the NER pathway is defective. This failure to repair UV-induced damage leads to a massively increased risk of skin cancers. Base excision repair (Option B) corrects single-base damage. Mismatch repair (Option C) corrects errors made during DNA replication. Double-stranded break repair (Option D) fixes breaks in both DNA strands.

Question 49

Which specific antibody is most commonly associated with celiac disease?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The serological diagnosis of Celiac Disease relies on the detection of highly specific autoantibodies. The most sensitive and specific antibody test currently used is the IgA anti-tissue transglutaminase (anti-TTG) antibody test. Tissue transglutaminase is the autoantigen in celiac disease; it deamidates gliadin (a component of gluten), which increases its immunogenicity and triggers the autoimmune response. IgA anti-endomysial antibodies (EMA) are also highly specific but the test is more subjective. While anti-gliadin antibodies (AGA) (Option D) were used in the past, they have lower sensitivity and specificity and are no longer recommended for primary diagnosis. ANCA (Option B) is associated with vasculitides like granulomatosis with polyangiitis. ASCA (Option C) is associated with Crohn's disease. Therefore, anti-TTG is the premier antibody marker for celiac disease.

Question 50

In a patient with thalassemia, which viral infection is known to target erythroid progenitor cells and can precipitate aplastic anemia?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Patients with chronic hemolytic anemias, such as thalassemia and sickle cell disease, rely on a hyperactive bone marrow to compensate for the rapid destruction of red blood cells. Parvovirus B19 has a unique tropism for erythroid progenitor cells (the precursors to red blood cells) in the bone marrow. Infection with this virus leads to the lysis of these progenitor cells, causing a temporary cessation of red blood cell production (erythropoiesis). In a person with normal red cell survival, this 7-10 day pause is clinically insignificant. However, in a thalassemia patient with a shortened red cell lifespan, this abrupt halt in production, known as a transient aplastic crisis, leads to a rapid and severe drop in hemoglobin levels, which can be life-threatening.
