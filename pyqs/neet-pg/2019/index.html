Question 1

Thrombosis of the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) is associated with which syndrome?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Wallenberg syndrome, also known as the lateral medullary syndrome, is a neurological condition caused by an ischemic stroke in the lateral part of the medulla oblongata in the brainstem. The most common vascular cause for this infarct is an occlusion of the intracranial portion of the vertebral artery, followed by its largest branch, the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA). This stroke leads to a characteristic pattern of neurological deficits due to damage to specific tracts and nuclei. Key features include vertigo, nystagmus, and ataxia (damage to the inferior cerebellar peduncle and vestibular nuclei); dysphagia and hoarseness (damage to the nucleus ambiguus); and a distinctive crossed sensory loss. The patient experiences loss of pain and temperature sensation on the ipsilateral (same) side of the face (spinal trigeminal nucleus) and the contralateral (opposite) side of the body (spinothalamic tract).

Question 2

In which organ is the Space of Disse located?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The Space of Disse, or the perisinusoidal space, is a unique anatomical location found within the liver. It is the microscopic space situated between the basolateral surface of the hepatocytes (liver cells) and the fenestrated endothelial lining of the liver sinusoids (specialized capillaries). This space is filled with blood plasma that filters through the fenestrations of the sinusoidal endothelium. The Space of Disse is a critical site for the metabolic exchange between the blood and the hepatocytes; plasma solutes can directly contact the microvilli of the hepatocytes, allowing for efficient uptake of nutrients and secretion of proteins like albumin. It also contains the hepatic stellate cells (also known as Ito cells), which are responsible for storing Vitamin A and are involved in the pathogenesis of liver fibrosis. This perisinusoidal space is a feature unique to the liver's microarchitecture.

Question 4

The cholecystocaval line, also known as Cantlie’s line, is an imaginary anatomical line used to divide the liver into functional right and left lobes. Question 7

Damage to which structure results in contralateral loss of pain and temperature sensation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The sensations of pain and temperature are transmitted from the periphery to the brain via the spinothalamic tract, which is a key component of the anterolateral system. The first-order neurons have their cell bodies in the dorsal root ganglion and enter the spinal cord. They synapse in the dorsal horn with second-order neurons. The axons of these second-order neurons immediately decussate (cross over) to the opposite side of the spinal cord within one or two spinal segments. They then ascend in the contralateral white matter as the lateral spinothalamic tract. Because these fibers cross at the level of the spinal cord, an injury to the lateral spinothalamic tract on one side will result in a loss of pain and temperature sensation on the contralateral (opposite) side of the body, beginning a few segments below the level of the lesion. The fasciculus gracilis and cuneatus (dorsal columns) carry ipsilateral proprioception and fine touch.

Question 9

The ducts of Bellini are found in which organ?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The ducts of Bellini, also known as the papillary ducts, are anatomical structures found within the kidney. They represent the most distal part of the collecting duct system. The collecting ducts from multiple nephrons progressively merge in the renal medulla to form larger and larger ducts. The final, large-caliber ducts that are formed just before the collecting system opens into the minor calyx at the tip of the renal papilla are called the ducts of Bellini. These ducts are lined by tall columnar epithelium and are responsible for channeling the final, concentrated urine from the renal medulla into the renal pelvis. They are the terminal conduits of the nephron and play a final role in concentrating the urine under the influence of antidiuretic hormone (ADH).

Question 11

Which joint is responsible for allowing the head to move from side to side (left to right)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The rotational movement of the head, such as shaking the head no from left to right, occurs primarily at the atlanto-axial joint. This is a complex of three synovial joints between the first cervical vertebra (the atlas, C1) and the second cervical vertebra (the axis, C2). The main component responsible for rotation is the pivot joint between the dens (odontoid process) of the axis and the anterior arch of the atlas. This arrangement allows the atlas, which carries the skull, to pivot or rotate around the dens of the axis. Approximately 50% of all cervical spine rotation occurs at this specific joint. In contrast, the atlanto-occipital joint (B), between the atlas and the occipital condyles of the skull, is primarily responsible for flexion and extension of the head (the yes motion).

Question 12

Which nerve supplies the submandibular gland?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The secretomotor innervation of the submandibular and sublingual salivary glands follows a complex parasympathetic pathway. The preganglionic parasympathetic fibers originate from the superior salivatory nucleus in the brainstem and travel with the facial nerve (CN VII). These fibers then branch off as the chorda tympani nerve, which joins the lingual nerve (a branch of the mandibular nerve, CN V3) in the infratemporal fossa. The preganglionic fibers travel along the lingual nerve until they synapse in the submandibular ganglion, which is suspended from the lingual nerve itself. Postganglionic fibers then arise from this ganglion and directly supply the submandibular and sublingual glands, stimulating saliva secretion. Therefore, while the original fibers are from the facial nerve, the lingual nerve is the final carrier of these secretomotor fibers to the gland and its ganglion.

Question 14

The right coronary artery originates from which structure?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The coronary arteries, which supply blood to the heart muscle itself, are the first branches of the aorta. They originate from the aortic sinuses (also known as the sinuses of Valsalva), which are three small dilations at the root of the ascending aorta, located just superior to the cusps of the aortic valve. There are three aortic sinuses : the right, the left, and the posterior (non-coronary) sinus. The right coronary artery (RCA) arises from the right aortic sinus (also called the anterior aortic sinus). The left coronary artery (LCA) arises from the left aortic sinus (also called the left posterior aortic sinus). The posterior aortic sinus is typically non-coronary, meaning no coronary artery arises from it.

Question 15

Bainbridge reflex causes which of the following?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The Bainbridge reflex, also known as the atrial reflex, is a physiological mechanism that results in an increase in heart rate in response to an increase in venous return and right atrial pressure. When venous return to the heart increases, the walls of the right atrium are stretched. This stretching stimulates low-pressure baroreceptors (stretch receptors) located in the atrial walls. The afferent signals from these receptors travel via the vagus nerve to the cardiovascular center in the medulla oblongata. The medullary centers then respond by decreasing parasympathetic (vagal) tone and increasing sympathetic tone to the sinoatrial (SA) node. This results in a reflexive tachycardia, or an increased heart rate. This reflex helps to prevent the pooling of blood in the venous system by accelerating the heart's pumping action to match the increased venous return.

Question 17

Which hormone is mainly released after day 14 of the menstrual cycle and influences the endometrium?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The menstrual cycle is divided into the follicular and luteal phases. Ovulation, triggered by the Luteinizing Hormone (LH) surge, occurs around day 14. Following ovulation, the remnant of the ovarian follicle transforms into the corpus luteum. The corpus luteum is responsible for secreting large amounts of progesterone, which becomes the dominant hormone during the second half of the cycle (the luteal phase, from day 14 to 28). Progesterone's primary role is to act on the endometrium, which has already been thickened by estrogen during the follicular phase. Progesterone induces secretory changes in the endometrial glands, increases vascularization, and makes the endometrium receptive to implantation of a fertilized embryo. If pregnancy does not occur, the corpus luteum degenerates, progesterone levels fall, and menstruation begins.

Question 19

Functional residual capacity (FRC) is the volume of air left in the lungs after which respiratory event?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Functional Residual Capacity (FRC) is a standard lung volume that represents the amount of air remaining in the lungs at the end of a normal, quiet expiration. It is the equilibrium point of the respiratory system, where the outward elastic recoil of the chest wall is exactly balanced by the inward elastic recoil of the lungs. The FRC is not a single volume but is the sum of two other lung volumes : the Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV) and the Residual Volume (RV). FRC is a physiologically important volume because it acts as a buffer, ensuring that gas exchange in the alveoli can continue at a relatively constant rate throughout the respiratory cycle, even during expiration. The volume of air remaining after a forceful expiration (C) is the Residual Volume.

Question 20

Polyuria is defined as a urine output exceeding which value?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Polyuria is a clinical term for the passage of an abnormally large volume of urine over a 24-hour period. While various absolute volume definitions exist (e.g., >3 liters per day for an adult), a more precise, weight-based definition is often used, particularly in pediatrics and for comparing individuals of different sizes. Polyuria is generally defined as a urine output of more than 40 to 50 milliliters per kilogram of body weight per 24 hours. For a typical 70 kg adult, this corresponds to a urine volume of 2.8 to 3.5 liters per day. This excessive urine output is a hallmark symptom of conditions like diabetes mellitus (due to osmotic diuresis) and diabetes insipidus (due to a lack of or insensitivity to antidiuretic hormone). Among the options provided, >50 ml/kg is a commonly cited threshold.

Question 25

C-peptide is present in which of the following situations?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Insulin is synthesized in the beta cells of the pancreas as a single polypeptide chain called proinsulin. The proinsulin molecule consists of the A chain and B chain of insulin, which are linked together by a connecting peptide, known as the C-peptide. For insulin to become active, this proinsulin molecule must be cleaved. Within the secretory granules of the beta cell, enzymes cleave off the C-peptide, leaving the A and B chains linked by disulfide bonds to form the mature insulin molecule. When the beta cell is stimulated to release insulin into the bloodstream, it co-secretes both mature insulin and the C-peptide in equimolar amounts (one molecule of C-peptide for every one molecule of insulin). Because C-peptide is co-secreted but is not cleared by the liver like insulin, its measurement in the blood is a valuable clinical tool for assessing endogenous insulin production.

Question 30

Which of the following statements is true regarding decorticate rigidity?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Decorticate rigidity (or posturing) is a stereotypical posture that occurs with specific types of severe brain injury. It is caused by a lesion in the cerebral hemispheres that is superior to the red nucleus in the midbrain. This lesion disconnects the cerebral cortex from the brainstem. The posture is characterized by flexion of the upper limbs (arms flexed and adducted, wrists and fingers flexed) and extension of the lower limbs. This occurs because the rubrospinal tract, which originates from the red nucleus and facilitates upper limb flexion, is left intact and unopposed by cortical input. The vestibulospinal tract causes extension in all limbs, but its effect on the upper limbs is overridden by the rubrospinal tract. In decerebrate rigidity, the lesion is below the red nucleus, so both upper and lower limbs are extended. Decerebrate rigidity is generally considered a more severe injury, and the associated extensor rigidity is more pronounced than in decorticate posturing.

Question 31

Zewellger syndrome is due to the absence of :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Zellweger syndrome is the most severe of a group of genetic disorders known as peroxisome biogenesis disorders (PBDs). It is an autosomal recessive condition caused by mutations in PEX genes, which encode proteins (peroxins) that are essential for the normal formation and assembly of peroxisomes. Peroxisomes are vital cellular organelles that are responsible for several key metabolic functions, including the beta-oxidation of very long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs), the synthesis of plasmalogens (essential components of myelin), and the metabolism of branched-chain fatty acids. The absence or dysfunction of peroxisomes in Zellweger syndrome leads to the accumulation of toxic VLCFAs and other metabolites in various tissues, particularly the brain, liver, and kidneys. This results in a devastating multisystem disease characterized by severe neurological abnormalities, craniofacial dysmorphism, and liver dysfunction, which is typically fatal in the first year of life.

Question 34

All are true regarding mitochondrial DNA, EXCEPT :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The statement that all respiratory proteins are synthesized within the mitochondria is false. While mitochondria possess their own small, circular, double-stranded genome (mtDNA) and the machinery to transcribe and translate it, this genome is very limited. Human mtDNA encodes only 13 proteins, all of which are essential subunits of the electron transport chain complexes (I, III, IV) and ATP synthase. However, the vast majority of mitochondrial proteins, including the other ~70 subunits of the respiratory chain complexes and all the proteins involved in mtDNA replication, transcription, translation, and metabolism, are encoded by genes in the nuclear DNA. These proteins are synthesized on cytoplasmic ribosomes and then imported into the mitochondria. Therefore, the assembly and function of the respiratory chain require a coordinated effort between both the mitochondrial and nuclear genomes. The other statements are true : mtDNA is inherited almost exclusively from the mother, and it has a high mutation rate compared to nuclear DNA.

Question 36

Hyperammonaemia inhibits the TCA cycle by depleting :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Hyperammonemia, an excess of ammonia in the blood, is highly toxic to the central nervous system and is the primary cause of hepatic encephalopathy. One of the main mechanisms of ammonia's neurotoxicity is its interference with the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle in brain astrocytes. In the brain, excess ammonia is detoxified by being combined with glutamate to form glutamine, a reaction catalyzed by glutamine synthetase. To replenish the glutamate used in this reaction, another enzyme, glutamate dehydrogenase, combines ammonia with the TCA cycle intermediate α-ketoglutarate. In a state of severe hyperammonemia, this reaction is driven strongly in the direction of glutamate synthesis, which effectively depletes the brain's pool of α-ketoglutarate. The loss of this critical TCA cycle intermediate impairs the cycle's function, leading to a deficit in cellular energy (ATP) production and contributing significantly to the neurological dysfunction seen in hepatic encephalopathy.

Question 42

True about type 1 diabetes mellitus :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The most common type of gallstones found in Western populations are cholesterol gallstones. These form when the bile in the gallbladder becomes supersaturated with cholesterol. Normally, cholesterol is kept soluble in bile through the formation of micelles with bile salts and phospholipids. When the concentration of cholesterol exceeds the solubilizing capacity of the bile, it can no longer remain in solution and begins to precipitate out. This initial step involves the nucleation of cholesterol molecules from the supersaturated bile. These molecules then aggregate and grow into macroscopic stones. The chemical form of the cholesterol that precipitates is anhydrous cholesterol, which then rapidly converts to stable crystalline cholesterol monohydrate. These plate-like crystals are the fundamental building blocks of cholesterol gallstones. Examination of the stones reveals these characteristic cholesterol monohydrate crystals.

Question 45

Collagen present in skin is

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Collagen is the most abundant protein in the human body and is the main structural component of connective tissues. There are many different types of collagen, each with a specific tissue distribution and function. The skin, particularly its dermal layer, is composed predominantly of Type I collagen. Type I collagen is a fibrillar collagen that forms thick, strong fibers, providing the dermis with its immense tensile strength and structural integrity. Type III collagen (reticular fibers) is also present in the skin, particularly in the papillary dermis and around blood vessels, but Type I is the major component (making up about 80% of dermal collagen). Type II collagen (A) is the principal collagen found in cartilage. Type IV collagen (D) is a non-fibrillar, network-forming collagen that is the primary structural component of all basement membranes, including the dermal-epidermal junction in the skin.

Question 46

Which of the following molecules functions as a ribozyme?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

A ribozyme is a ribonucleic acid (RNA) molecule that possesses catalytic activity, meaning it can function as an enzyme. While most biological catalysts are proteins, certain RNA molecules can also catalyze chemical reactions. The most prominent and fundamental example of a ribozyme is the large ribosomal RNA (rRNA) component of the ribosome. The crucial step of protein synthesis, the formation of a peptide bond between amino acids, is catalyzed not by a ribosomal protein but by the peptidyl transferase center located within the23S (in prokaryotes) or28S (in eukaryotes) rRNA of the large ribosomal subunit. This discovery that RNA could act as a catalyst was a major breakthrough in molecular biology and led to the RNA world hypothesis. The other options are all protein enzymes : poly(A) polymerase, ribonuclease, and transpeptidase.

Question 47

Type I hyperlipoproteinemia is characterized by which feature?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Type I hyperlipoproteinemia, also known as familial chylomicronemia syndrome, is a rare autosomal recessive disorder of lipid metabolism. The condition is characterized by a massive accumulation of chylomicrons in the plasma. This is caused by a deficiency in the enzyme lipoprotein lipase (LPL) or its cofactor, apolipoprotein C-II. LPL is the enzyme located on the surface of capillary endothelial cells that is responsible for breaking down the triglycerides within chylomicrons and VLDL, allowing for the uptake of fatty acids by peripheral tissues. In its absence, chylomicrons, which are synthesized in the intestine to transport dietary fat, cannot be cleared from the circulation. This leads to extremely high triglyceride levels (hypertriglyceridemia), eruptive xanthomas, and a high risk of recurrent acute pancreatitis. A classic sign is a creamy, supernatant layer that forms when a blood sample is left to stand.

Question 50

Glanzmann thrombasthenia results from a defect in which component?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Glanzmann thrombasthenia is a rare, autosomal recessive bleeding disorder characterized by a qualitative or quantitative defect in platelet function. The underlying molecular pathology is a deficiency or dysfunction of the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (GpIIb/IIIa) receptor on the surface of platelets. This receptor is an integrin that, upon platelet activation, undergoes a conformational change and becomes the binding site for fibrinogen. Fibrinogen then acts as a bridge, cross-linking adjacent platelets to one another. This cross-linking is the essential final step in the formation of a stable platelet aggregate and primary hemostatic plug. In Glanzmann thrombasthenia, the absence of functional GpIIb/IIIa receptors means that platelets can adhere to injured endothelium but cannot aggregate. This leads to a severe bleeding tendency, typically manifesting as mucocutaneous bleeding such as epistaxis, gingival bleeding, and menorrhagia. A decreased GpIb (A) is seen in Bernard-Soulier syndrome, while anti-GpIIb/IIIa antibodies (C) are found in immune thrombocytopenic purpura.
