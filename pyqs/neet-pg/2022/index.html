Question 1

Which of the following components is involved in the baroreceptor reflex?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The baroreceptor reflex is a crucial homeostatic mechanism for the short-term regulation of blood pressure. It operates on the principle of negative feedback. Baroreceptors are stretch receptors located in the walls of the aortic arch and the carotid sinuses. When blood pressure rises, these receptors are stretched, and they increase their firing rate to the cardiovascular centers in the brainstem. In response, the brainstem initiates a reflex that increases parasympathetic (vagal) activity and decreases sympathetic activity. This leads to a decrease in heart rate, cardiac contractility, and peripheral vascular resistance, which in turn lowers the blood pressure back towards its set point. Conversely, a drop in blood pressure reduces baroreceptor firing, triggering an increase in sympathetic activity to raise the pressure. This corrective action that opposes the initial stimulus is the definition of a negative feedback loop.

Question 2

Which of the following factors will reduce extracellular potassium levels?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Epinephrine (adrenaline) and other beta-2 adrenergic agonists play a significant role in potassium homeostasis by promoting the shift of potassium from the extracellular fluid into the intracellular compartment. Epinephrine activates the Na+/K+-ATPase pump on the cell membrane, primarily in skeletal muscle cells. This activation increases the uptake of potassium into the cells, thereby lowering the concentration of potassium in the extracellular fluid (plasma). This mechanism is physiologically important for preventing dangerous hyperkalemia, especially after exercise when potassium is released from contracting muscles. Insulin also has a similar effect of shifting potassium into cells. Glucagon (A) can cause a transient increase in extracellular potassium. Lactic acidosis (C) causes potassium to shift out of cells, leading to hyperkalemia. Atropine (B) does not have a direct effect on potassium distribution.

Question 4

A patient presents with rice-water stools. The toxin acts on which receptor in the intestine?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The clinical presentation of profuse, voluminous rice-watery stools is the hallmark of cholera, an infection caused by Vibrio cholerae. The disease is mediated by the powerful cholera toxin. The cholera toxin is an A-B type toxin, composed of one A (active) subunit and five B (binding) subunits. The B subunits are responsible for binding the toxin to the surface of the intestinal epithelial cells (enterocytes). The specific receptor on the host cell membrane to which the B subunits bind is the GM1 ganglioside. Once bound, the A subunit is internalized into the cell. It then acts as an ADP-ribosyltransferase, permanently activating the Gs alpha subunit of the adenylate cyclase enzyme. This leads to a massive, unregulated increase in intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP), which in turn causes the hypersecretion of chloride, sodium, and water into the intestinal lumen, resulting in severe secretory diarrhea.

Question 5

Hemiballismus is associated with a lesion of which nucleus?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Hemiballismus is a rare hyperkinetic movement disorder characterized by involuntary, violent, flinging movements of the limbs on one side of the body (contralateral to the lesion). The classic cause of hemiballismus is a lesion, most commonly a lacunar stroke or hemorrhage, affecting the subthalamic nucleus (STN). The STN is a key component of the basal ganglia's indirect pathway, which normally functions to inhibit unwanted movements. A lesion in the STN disrupts this pathway, leading to reduced inhibitory output from the basal ganglia to the thalamus. This disinhibition of the thalamocortical pathway results in the release of the uncontrolled, large-amplitude, ballistic movements that are characteristic of the disorder. While other basal ganglia nuclei are involved in motor control, the subthalamic nucleus is the specific anatomical structure most directly linked to hemiballismus.

Question 8

A patient with tuberculosis on isoniazid therapy for six months presents with tingling and paresthesia. Deficiency of which vitamin is the most likely cause?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

A well-known side effect of the anti-tuberculosis drug isoniazid (INH) is a dose-dependent peripheral neuropathy, which presents with symptoms like tingling, numbness, and paresthesias in a glove and stocking distribution. This neurotoxicity occurs because isoniazid structurally resembles vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) and interferes with its metabolism. INH increases the excretion of vitamin B6 and inhibits the enzyme pyridoxine phosphokinase, which is necessary to convert pyridoxine into its active form, pyridoxal-5'-phosphate (PLP). PLP is an essential cofactor for numerous enzymatic reactions, including the synthesis of neurotransmitters. The resulting deficiency of active vitamin B6 leads to the development of peripheral neuropathy. To prevent this complication, it is standard practice to co-administer prophylactic pyridoxine supplementation to all patients receiving isoniazid therapy.

Question 9

Which of the following enzyme activities measured in red blood cells can be used to diagnose vitamin B₂ deficiency?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The functional status of several vitamins can be assessed by measuring the activity of a vitamin-dependent enzyme in red blood cells (RBCs), both with and without the addition of the vitamin's active coenzyme form. For Vitamin B2 (riboflavin), the key enzyme is erythrocyte glutathione reductase (EGR). This enzyme is crucial for reducing oxidized glutathione (GSSG) to its reduced form (GSH), which is essential for protecting the cell against oxidative damage. EGR requires flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), the active coenzyme form of riboflavin, to function. In a riboflavin-deficient individual, the EGR enzyme will be undersaturated with FAD, and its basal activity will be low. When FAD is added to the assay in vitro, the enzyme activity increases significantly. The extent of this stimulation (the EGR activity coefficient or EGRAC) is a sensitive and specific functional marker of riboflavin status. Transketolase (B) is used to assess thiamine (B1) status.

Question 12

Primaquine can precipitate hemolytic anemia in individuals with a specific enzyme deficiency. This enzyme is associated with which metabolic pathway?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The antimalarial drug primaquine is an oxidizing agent. In individuals with a deficiency of the enzyme glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), administration of primaquine can trigger an acute hemolytic crisis. G6PD is the rate-limiting enzyme of the Hexose Monophosphate (HMP) shunt, also known as the pentose phosphate pathway. The primary function of the HMP shunt in red blood cells is to produce NADPH. NADPH is essential for maintaining a supply of reduced glutathione (GSH) via the enzyme glutathione reductase. Reduced glutathione is a critical antioxidant that protects the red blood cell from oxidative damage. In G6PD-deficient individuals, NADPH production is impaired. When exposed to an oxidizing drug like primaquine, the red cells cannot cope with the oxidative stress, leading to hemoglobin denaturation (Heinz bodies) and subsequent hemolysis.

Question 16

Which of the following acts as an inhibitor of Complex IV ?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The mitochondrial electron transport chain consists of four main protein complexes. Complex IV, also known as cytochrome c oxidase, is the final enzyme in this chain. It is responsible for transferring electrons from cytochrome c to molecular oxygen, reducing it to water. This step is crucial for the entire process of oxidative phosphorylation. Cyanide is a potent and rapidly acting poison that specifically and irreversibly binds to the ferric iron () in the heme a3 component of cytochrome c oxidase. This binding completely blocks the transfer of electrons to oxygen, effectively halting the electron transport chain and cellular respiration. This leads to a rapid and catastrophic drop in ATP production, causing cytotoxic hypoxia and death. Antimycin (D) inhibits Complex III. Rotenone and barbiturates inhibit Complex I.

Question 17

What is the mechanism of action of botulinum toxin?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Botulinum toxin, produced by the bacterium Clostridium botulinum, is one of the most potent neurotoxins known. Its mechanism of action is to produce a flaccid paralysis by acting at the presynaptic terminal of the neuromuscular junction. The toxin is a zinc endopeptidase that specifically cleaves proteins of the SNARE complex. These SNARE proteins (such as SNAP-25, synaptobrevin, and syntaxin) are essential for the docking and fusion of synaptic vesicles with the presynaptic membrane. By destroying these proteins, the botulinum toxin prevents the release of acetylcholine from the axon terminals of lower motor neurons into the synaptic cleft. Without acetylcholine to bind to the nicotinic receptors on the muscle fiber, muscle contraction cannot occur, resulting in paralysis. It does not inhibit the receptor itself (A), affect reuptake (B), or inhibit synthesis (D).

Question 19

What is a single-nucleotide change that results in the formation of a stop codon called?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

A point mutation (C) is a general term for a change in a single nucleotide base within the DNA sequence. There are several types of point mutations based on their effect on the resulting protein. A nonsense mutation is a specific type of point mutation where the change in a single nucleotide results in the original amino acid-coding codon being converted into one of the three stop codons (UAA, UAG, or UGA). This causes the premature termination of translation, leading to the production of a truncated, and usually non-functional, protein. A missense mutation (A) results in a different amino acid being incorporated. A silent mutation (D) is a change in the nucleotide sequence that does not change the resulting amino acid, due to the redundancy of the genetic code.

Question 20

Warburg effect is used by cancer cells because-

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The Warburg effect, or aerobic glycolysis, describes the phenomenon where cancer cells preferentially metabolize glucose through glycolysis, converting it to lactate, even in the presence of ample oxygen. This is metabolically inefficient for ATP production; oxidative phosphorylation yields far more ATP per molecule of glucose (so A is incorrect). However, this metabolic shift is advantageous for rapidly proliferating cells. The high rate of glycolysis, while yielding less ATP, shunts glucose-derived carbon intermediates into various anabolic pathways. These intermediates serve as essential building blocks (elements) for the synthesis of nucleotides (for DNA/RNA), lipids (for membranes), and amino acids, which are all required to build new cells. This redirection of metabolism from energy production to biosynthesis is crucial for supporting the high proliferative demands of cancer cells.

Question 21

All of the following statements regarding neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are true except which one?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs) are web-like structures released by neutrophils as a defense mechanism against pathogens. These NETs are composed of a backbone of decondensed nuclear DNA, which is studded with various antimicrobial proteins from the neutrophil's granules, such as histones, elastase, and myeloperoxidase (so D is true). The primary function of NETs is to trap and kill extracellular pathogens like bacteria (B) and fungi, preventing their dissemination. This process, called NETosis, is a distinct form of cell death. NETs are prominently observed in various inflammatory and infectious conditions, including sepsis (A). The DNA that forms the scaffold of NETs is derived from the nucleus, not from the mitochondria (so C is the incorrect statement). Mitochondrial DNA may be released during other forms of cell death but is not the primary component of the NET scaffold.

Question 25

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Autosomal recessive (AR) inheritance means that a person must inherit two copies of the mutated gene (one from each parent) to be affected by the disorder. Parents are typically asymptomatic carriers. Cystic fibrosis is a classic example of an autosomal recessive disease, caused by mutations in the CFTR gene. In contrast, the other options are autosomal dominant (AD) disorders. In AD inheritance, inheriting just one copy of the mutated gene from one parent is sufficient to cause the disease. Huntington disease (B), achondroplasia (C), and Treacher Collins syndrome (D) are all well-known examples of autosomal dominant conditions. They typically show vertical transmission in a pedigree (appearing in every generation), whereas AR disorders like cystic fibrosis often appear in siblings with unaffected parents (horizontal transmission).

Question 35

Question 39

A 5-year-old patient with asthma on theophylline therapy presents with frequent and increased urination. What is the most likely mechanism by which theophylline causes diuresis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Theophylline is a methylxanthine used as a bronchodilator in asthma and COPD. Besides its effects on the airways, it also has a mild diuretic effect. The primary mechanism for this diuresis is its action as a non-selective antagonist of adenosine receptors. Specifically, antagonism of the A1 adenosine receptor in the kidney has two main effects that promote diuresis. First, it causes afferent arteriolar vasodilation, which increases the glomerular filtration rate (GFR). Second, it inhibits sodium reabsorption in the proximal convoluted tubule. The combination of an increased GFR and reduced proximal sodium reabsorption leads to an increased delivery of fluid and solute to the distal parts of the nephron, resulting in diuresis. The Na+K+2Cl- blocker (C) is the mechanism of loop diuretics, and the Na+Cl- blocker (D) is the mechanism of thiazide diuretics.

Question 41

A patient receiving cisplatin therapy presents with multiple episodes of vomiting. After administration of an antiemetic, he develops Parkinsonian features. Which antiemetic drug is most likely responsible for these symptoms?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The development of Parkinson's-like features, also known as extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) such as dystonia, akathisia, and pseudoparkinsonism, is a well-known side effect of drugs that block dopamine D2 receptors in the central nervous system. Metoclopramide is an antiemetic that acts as a central and peripheral D2 receptor antagonist. Because it readily crosses the blood-brain barrier, it can block D2 receptors in the basal ganglia, disrupting the normal balance of dopamine and acetylcholine and leading to these movement disorders. Cisplatin is a highly emetogenic chemotherapy agent, and metoclopramide is an effective antiemetic, but this side effect limits its use. Domperidone (B) is also a D2 antagonist but does not cross the blood-brain barrier as readily, so it has a much lower risk of causing EPS. Ondansetron (C) is a 5-HT3 antagonist and does not cause EPS.

Correct answer: B

This question describes the classic microbiological features of Talaromyces marneffei (formerly Penicillium marneffei). This fungus is a significant opportunistic pathogen in immuno compromised individuals, particularly HIV-positive patients in Southeast Asia. It is thermally dimorphic, growing as a mold at room temperature (25-27°C) and converting to a yeast-like form at body temperature (37°C). The most distinctive feature is that the mold form produces a characteristic, water-soluble red pigment that diffuses into the culture medium. Question 44

Which of the following hypolipidemic drugs works by inhibiting PCSK-?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Evolocumab is a monoclonal antibody and a member of the drug class known as PCSK9 inhibitors. PCSK9 (Proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9) is a protein that binds to LDL receptors on the surface of liver cells. This binding promotes the degradation of the LDL receptors inside the cell, which reduces the number of receptors available to clear LDL cholesterol from the blood. By binding to and inhibiting PCSK9, evolocumab prevents it from destroying LDL receptors. This leads to a significant increase in the number of LDL receptors on the hepatocyte surface, which in turn enhances the clearance of LDL cholesterol from the circulation, dramatically lowering LDL-C levels. Atorvastatin (A) is a statin that inhibits HMG-CoA reductase. Ezetimibe (C) inhibits cholesterol absorption at the brush border of the small intestine. Fenofibrate (D) is a fibrate that primarily works by activating PPAR-alpha.

Question 45

Which of the following agents acts as a growth hormone receptor antagonist?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Pegvisomant is a unique medication used in the treatment of acromegaly, a condition of excess growth hormone (GH). Its mechanism of action is as a growth hormone receptor antagonist. It is a pegylated, genetically engineered variant of human GH that binds to the GH receptor on cell surfaces but does not activate it. By competitively blocking the binding of endogenous GH to its receptors, pegvisomant prevents the synthesis and secretion of Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 (IGF-1), which is the primary mediator of GH's growth-promoting and metabolic effects. This leads to a reduction in IGF-1 levels and an improvement in the clinical signs and symptoms of acromegaly. Mecasermin (B) is recombinant IGF-1. Tesamorelin (C) is a GHRH analogue. Bromocriptine (D) is a dopamine agonist that can sometimes lower GH levels but is not a receptor antagonist.
