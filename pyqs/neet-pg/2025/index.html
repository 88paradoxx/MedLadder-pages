Question 1

What are the contents of Umbilical cord?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The umbilical cord is the lifeline connecting the fetus to the placenta. In a normal, fully developed umbilical cord, there are three blood vessels encased in a gelatinous substance called Wharton's jelly. These vessels are two umbilical arteries and one umbilical vein. The two umbilical arteries carry deoxygenated blood and waste products away from the fetus to the placenta. The single umbilical vein carries oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood from the placenta to the fetus. Initially, there are two umbilical veins, but the right umbilical vein typically obliterates early in fetal development, leaving only the left one to persist. The presence of only a single umbilical artery instead of two can sometimes be associated with other congenital anomalies.

Question 3

A girl presents with a foot injury following a history of forceful eversion of the foot. Which structure is most likely to be affected?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Ankle sprains are common injuries, but the ligaments affected depend on the mechanism of injury. The vast majority of ankle sprains (over 85%) are inversion injuries, where the foot rolls inward, stressing the ligaments on the lateral (outer) side of the ankle. These are the anterior talofibular ligament (A), the posterior talofibular ligament (C), and the calcaneofibular ligament (D). In contrast, a forceful eversion injury, where the foot is twisted outward, puts stress on the ligaments on the medial (inner) side of the ankle. The primary stabilizer on the medial side is the deltoid ligament. This is a very strong, fan-shaped ligament complex that connects the medial malleolus of the tibia to the talus, calcaneus, and navicular bones. A significant eversion force is required to sprain or tear the deltoid ligament.

Question 4

A patient presents with muscle wasting, hypotonia, and weakness of the left hand, with impaired fine motor skills such as kneading dough. Which anatomical structure is most likely affected?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The patient's symptoms—muscle wasting (atrophy), reduced tone (hypotonia/flaccidity), and weakness—are the classic signs of a lower motor neuron (LMN) lesion. LMNs are the final common pathway connecting the central nervous system to the muscles. The cell bodies of the LMNs that innervate the limbs are located in the anterior (ventral) horn of the spinal cord. Since the symptoms are confined to the left hand, the lesion must be affecting the anterior horn cells in the cervical spinal cord that supply the muscles of that hand. The corticospinal tracts decussate (cross over) in the medulla. Therefore, a lesion in the right cerebral hemisphere would cause deficits on the left side of the body. While a pyramidal or internal capsule lesion (B, C, D) would cause left-sided weakness, these are all upper motor neuron lesions, which would result in spasticity and hyperreflexia, not atrophy and reduced tone.

The marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve has a clinically important and vulnerable course. Question 6

Anti-mitochondrial antibody (AMA) is most commonly seen in :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Anti-mitochondrial antibodies (AMAs) are the serological hallmark of Primary Biliary Cholangitis (PBC), an autoimmune liver disease characterized by the chronic, progressive destruction of the small intrahepatic bile ducts. AMAs are present in approximately 90-95% of patients with PBC and have a very high specificity for the disease. The primary target of these autoantibodies is the E2 component of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC-E2), located on the inner mitochondrial membrane. The presence of AMA, particularly at a high titer, in a patient with a cholestatic pattern of liver enzyme elevation (predominantly elevated alkaline phosphatase) is virtually diagnostic of PBC. In contrast, autoimmune hepatitis (A) is associated with anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA) and anti-smooth muscle antibodies (ASMA). Primary sclerosing cholangitis (C) is often associated with perinuclear anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (p-ANCA).

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The key to this diagnosis is the specific histopathological findings described. Question 19

A 68-year-old man presents with bleeding, and peripheral blood smear reveals the presence of a faggot cell. Which chromosomal translocation is most commonly associated with this finding?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The patient's presentation with bleeding suggests a coagulopathy, a common feature of a specific type of acute leukemia. The key diagnostic finding is the presence of a "faggot cell" on the peripheral blood smear. A faggot cell is a pathognomonic finding in Acute Promyelocytic Leukemia (APL), also known as AML M3. It is a malignant promyelocyte whose cytoplasm is packed with numerous Auer rods, which are abnormal, needle-like azurophilic granules, often arranged in bundles or "faggots." APL is a medical emergency due to its strong association with life-threatening disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). The defining genetic abnormality in virtually all cases of APL is the specific chromosomal translocation t(15;17)(q24;q21). This translocation creates a fusion gene between the PML gene on chromosome 15 and the retinoic acid receptor-alpha (RARA) gene on chromosome 17, which is the underlying driver of the disease.

Question 22

In Hurler syndrome, which glycosaminoglycans accumulate in tissues and lead to organ enlargement?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Hurler syndrome, also known as Mucopolysaccharidosis Type I (MPS I), is an autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder. It is caused by a deficiency of the enzyme alpha-L-iduronidase. This enzyme is essential for the stepwise degradation of two specific glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), also known as mucopolysaccharides. In the absence of this enzyme, its substrates, heparan sulfate and dermatan sulfate, cannot be broken down and progressively accumulate within the lysosomes of cells throughout the body. This lysosomal storage leads to cellular dysfunction, organomegaly (hepatosplenomegaly), skeletal deformities (dysostosis multiplex), corneal clouding, and progressive neurological deterioration. Accumulation of keratan sulfate (A, B, D) is characteristic of Morquio syndrome (MPS IV).

Question 25

A patient has elevated phenylalanine levels despite normal phenylalanine hydroxylase activity. Deficiency of which cofactor is most likely responsible for this finding?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) catalyzes the conversion of phenylalanine to tyrosine. This reaction requires a specific cofactor, tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4). In classic Phenylketonuria (PKU), the PAH enzyme itself is deficient. However, a less common cause of hyperphenylalaninemia is a defect in the synthesis or recycling of the BH4 cofactor. This is known as malignant PKU. In these cases, the PAH enzyme protein is normal, but it cannot function due to the lack of its essential cofactor. BH4 is also a required cofactor for the enzymes tyrosine hydroxylase and tryptophan hydroxylase, which are involved in the synthesis of the neurotransmitters dopamine and serotonin. Therefore, BH4 deficiency leads not only to high phenylalanine levels but also to a severe neurological disease that does not respond to a low-phenylalanine diet alone.

Question 26

A patient with vitamin B12 deficiency presents with anemia, a positive Romberg sign, and elevated homocysteine levels. Which amino acid is most likely deficient?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The conversion of the amino acid homocysteine to methionine is a crucial step in one-carbon metabolism. This reaction is catalyzed by the enzyme methionine synthase. This enzyme requires Vitamin B12 (in the form of methylcobalamin) as an essential cofactor. In Vitamin B12 deficiency, the activity of methionine synthase is impaired. This leads to two major biochemical consequences : a buildup of the substrate, homocysteine, in the blood (elevated homocysteine), and a deficiency of the product, methionine. Methionine is an essential amino acid required for protein synthesis and for the production of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the universal methyl donor for numerous critical methylation reactions in the body, including the synthesis of myelin. The neurological symptoms of B12 deficiency (like the positive Romberg sign, indicating proprioceptive loss) may be related to this impaired methylation.

Question 28

A frameshift mutation caused by insertion of a nucleotide at the 4th position of a 900-base mRNA occurs. What is the most likely consequence of this mutation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The genetic code is read in non-overlapping triplets of nucleotides called codons. A frameshift mutation occurs when the number of inserted or deleted nucleotides is not a multiple of three. In this case, a single nucleotide is inserted at position 4. This is highly disruptive because it shifts the reading frame for the entire downstream sequence of the mRNA. The insertion at position 4 will alter the second codon (bases 4, 5, 6) and every subsequent codon thereafter. This will lead to the incorporation of a completely different sequence of amino acids from that point onward, resulting in a complete change in the protein product. Furthermore, a frameshift mutation often introduces a premature stop codon, which would lead to the truncation of the protein. The final protein will be completely different and almost certainly non-functional.

Question 30

A patient presents with orange-colored tonsils, a triglyceride level of 140 mg/dL, and markedly reduced HDL cholesterol (5 mg/dL). What is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The combination of pathognomonic clinical and laboratory findings is diagnostic of Tangier disease. This is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by a severe deficiency or absence of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol in the blood. The underlying genetic defect is in the ABCA1 gene, which codes for a transporter that is essential for the efflux of cholesterol from peripheral cells to form HDL particles. The hallmark laboratory finding is extremely low HDL levels (often <5 mg/dL) with normal or low LDL and moderately elevated triglycerides. The clinical hallmark is the deposition of cholesteryl esters in the reticuloendothelial system, which causes enlarged, orange-yellow colored tonsils, as well as hepatosplenomegaly and a peripheral neuropathy. Familial hypercholesterolemia (A) involves high LDL, and Type I hyperlipoproteinemia (C) involves extremely high triglycerides.

Question 35

A patient ascends to a high altitude of 700 meters and develops headache, nausea, dyspnea, and dizziness. What physiological mechanism best explains the development of these symptoms?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The symptoms described are characteristic of Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS), which occurs due to rapid ascent to high altitudes without adequate acclimatization. The primary trigger for AMS is the reduced partial pressure of oxygen in the inspired air, leading to systemic hypobaric hypoxia. The brain is particularly sensitive to low oxygen levels. In response to this hypoxia, the cerebral blood vessels undergo vasodilation to increase blood flow and oxygen delivery to the brain tissue. This vasodilation leads to increased intracranial pressure and vascular leakage (cerebral edema), which manifests clinically as a headache, the hallmark symptom of AMS. Other symptoms like nausea, dizziness, and dyspnea are also related to the body's response to hypoxia. In contrast, hypoxia causes pulmonary vasoconstriction (Option B), not systemic vasoconstriction. The initial respiratory response is hyperventilation, leading to respiratory alkalosis, not metabolic acidosis (Option C).

Question 37

What type of acid–base disturbance is expected in a person trekking at high altitude as a result of hyperventilation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

At high altitudes, the partial pressure of oxygen in the ambient air is low, leading to systemic hypoxia. The peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid and aortic bodies) detect this drop in arterial PO₂ and stimulate the respiratory center in the brainstem to increase the rate and depth of breathing. This response, known as hyperventilation, is a compensatory mechanism to increase oxygen intake. However, this increased ventilation also leads to excessive exhalation or "blowing off" of carbon dioxide (CO₂). Since CO₂ dissolves in blood to form carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), its excessive loss leads to a decrease in the partial pressure of arterial CO₂ (PaCO₂) and a decrease in hydrogen ion concentration, causing the pH of the blood to rise. This condition, characterized by a primary decrease in PaCO₂ and an elevated pH, is defined as respiratory alkalosis.

Question 39

A patient presents with fatigue, muscle cramps, and an intense craving for salt. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The combination of fatigue, muscle cramps, and a specific, strong craving for salt is highly suggestive of Addison's disease, or primary adrenal insufficiency. In this condition, the adrenal cortex fails to produce adequate amounts of cortisol and aldosterone. Aldosterone deficiency is the key to these specific symptoms. Lack of aldosterone leads to excessive sodium and water loss in the urine (natriuresis) and retention of potassium (hyperkalemia). The resulting hyponatremia and volume depletion cause hypotension, fatigue, and muscle cramps. The body's physiological response to the severe sodium deficit manifests as an intense craving for salt. Conn's disease (Option B) is primary hyperaldosteronism, which would cause sodium retention and hypertension. Cushing's disease (Option C) involves excess cortisol and would also cause hypertension. Hypopituitarism (Option D) can cause secondary adrenal insufficiency, but the salt craving is most characteristic of primary (aldosterone-deficient) failure.

Question 40

The Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012 is a comprehensive law in India designed to protect children from offenses of sexual assault, sexual harassment, and pornography. Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

This question addresses the legal concept of statutory rape. Under Indian law, specifically the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012, the age of consent for sexual activity is 18 years. Any sexual act involving a person below the age of 18 is considered a criminal offense, regardless of whether the minor agreed to it. The law presumes that a person under 18 is incapable of giving valid, informed consent to sexual activity. Therefore, the girl's statement that the act was consensual is legally irrelevant. The consent is invalid as a matter of law because she is a minor. The absence of physical injuries also does not negate the crime. The act itself constitutes a sexual offense under the POCSO Act.

Question 46

A patient with a history of hypertension presents with renal calculi and is started on hydrochlorothiazide. Which statement correctly explains the rationale for using this drug?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The primary reason hydrochlorothiazide, a thiazide diuretic, is prescribed for patients with recurrent calcium-containing renal stones is its effect on renal calcium handling. Thiazides act on the distal convoluted tubule of the nephron, where they inhibit the Na⁺-Cl⁻ cotransporter. This action leads to an indirect increase in the reabsorption of calcium from the tubular fluid back into the blood, resulting in decreased calcium excretion in the urine (hypocalciuria). By lowering the concentration of calcium in the urine, thiazides reduce the risk of calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate stone formation. Therefore, statement (B) is the correct explanation for its therapeutic use in this context. Statement (A) is the opposite of the drug's effect. Statement (D), decreased citrate excretion (hypocitraturia), is a potential adverse effect of thiazides (often due to diuretic-induced hypokalemia), and it would actually increase the risk of stone formation, not prevent it. Citrate is a natural inhibitor of stone formation.

Question 47

A patient with HIV is newly diagnosed with multidrug-resistant tuberculosis. What is the appropriate treatment regimen, and which parameters should be monitored during therapy?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

For patients with multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB), which is defined as resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampicin, standard first-line drugs are ineffective. Current WHO guidelines recommend shorter, all-oral regimens. The BPaLM regimen is one such highly effective 6-month regimen consisting of Bedaquiline, Pretomanid, Linezolid, and Moxifloxacin. This regimen has shown high efficacy rates in treating MDR-TB. When treating a co-infected patient with HIV and TB, a critical consideration is the timing of antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation. Starting ART can lead to a rapid recovery of the immune system, which can then mount an exaggerated inflammatory response to the TB antigens. This phenomenon is known as Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome (IRIS), which can cause a paradoxical worsening of symptoms. Therefore, patients must be closely monitored for signs of IRIS. The other options list incorrect or outdated regimens for MDR-TB.

Question 49

Benralizumab exerts its therapeutic effect by targeting which specific receptor?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Benralizumab is a monoclonal antibody specifically designed as a targeted therapy for severe eosinophilic asthma. Its mechanism of action involves binding to the alpha subunit of the interleukin-5 (IL-5) receptor, which is expressed on the surface of eosinophils and basophils. Unlike other anti-eosinophilic drugs that target the IL-5 ligand itself (like mepolizumab and reslizumab), benralizumab targets the receptor. By binding to the IL-5 receptor α, it prevents IL-5 from binding and activating the cell. More importantly, this binding marks the eosinophil for rapid destruction by natural killer cells through a process called antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC). This leads to a profound and rapid depletion of eosinophils from the blood and tissues. The other cytokines listed are involved in different inflammatory pathways : IL-4 (Option B) in atopy, IL-1 (Option C) in innate immunity, and TNF-α (Option D) in systemic inflammation.
