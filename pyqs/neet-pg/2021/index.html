Question 2

Which blood vessel is responsible for carrying deoxygenated blood from the fetus back to the placenta?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

In fetal circulation, the roles of the umbilical arteries and vein are reversed compared to systemic circulation in an adult. The umbilical artery, of which there are two, carries deoxygenated blood and waste products from the fetus to the placenta for oxygenation and removal. Conversely, the single umbilical vein (B) carries highly oxygenated and nutrient-rich blood from the placenta to the fetus. The ductus venosus (C) is a shunt that allows a portion of this oxygenated blood from the umbilical vein to bypass the fetal liver and go directly to the inferior vena cava (D). Therefore, it is the umbilical arteries that are responsible for returning deoxygenated blood to the maternal circulation via the placenta.

Question 4

Which nerve is responsible for mediating the gag reflex?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The gag reflex (or pharyngeal reflex) involves two key cranial nerves. The afferent (sensory) limb of the reflex is mediated by the 9th cranial nerve (glossopharyngeal nerve). When the posterior pharynx, tonsils, or base of the tongue is touched, sensory signals are transmitted via the glossopharyngeal nerve to the brainstem. The efferent (motor) limb, which causes the contraction of the pharyngeal muscles, is primarily mediated by the 10th cranial nerve (vagus nerve). Since the question asks which nerve is responsible for the reflex, and the 9th nerve is the primary sensory trigger, it is the correct answer among the choices. The 6th (abducens) and 11th (accessory) nerves are purely motor, and the 7th (facial) nerve has different sensory and motor functions.

Question 5

A patient undergoes coronary artery bypass grafting using the great saphenous vein and later develops neuralgia along the medial aspect of the leg and foot. Which nerve is most likely to have been injured during the procedure?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The great saphenous vein is commonly harvested for use as a conduit in CABG surgery. The saphenous nerve runs in close proximity to this vein along the medial aspect of the leg and foot. During the surgical harvesting of the vein, the saphenous nerve is highly susceptible to stretching, transection, or entrapment. Injury to this nerve results in sensory disturbances, such as pain (neuralgia), numbness, or tingling, in its distribution area, which is the medial aspect of the leg and foot. This is a well-recognized complication of great saphenous vein harvesting. The sural nerve (A) supplies the lateral aspect of the foot, while the tibial (B) and common peroneal (D) nerves have different and more extensive sensory and motor distributions.

Question 6

Through which secondary messenger system do β₂-adrenergic receptors exert their effects?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Beta-2 adrenergic receptors are a type of G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR). Specifically, they are coupled to a stimulatory G-protein (Gs). When a ligand like epinephrine binds to the beta-2 receptor, it activates the Gs protein. This activated protein, in turn, stimulates the membrane-bound enzyme adenylate cyclase. Adenylate cyclase then catalyzes the conversion of ATP into the second messenger, cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP). The increased intracellular levels of cAMP activate Protein Kinase A (PKA), which then phosphorylates various downstream targets to produce the final physiological effect, such as bronchodilation or vasodilation. Tyrosine kinase receptors (B) are a different class of receptors used by insulin and growth factors.

Question 7

A child with dehydration is treated with oral rehydration solution (ORS). Which receptor or transporter in the gastrointestinal tract facilitates glucose absorption in this process?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The effectiveness of Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS) is based on the coupled transport of sodium and glucose in the small intestine. The absorption of glucose from the intestinal lumen into the enterocytes is primarily mediated by the Sodium-Glucose Linked Transporter 1 (SGLT1). This transporter is located on the apical membrane of intestinal epithelial cells. It functions by using the electrochemical gradient of sodium to co-transport one molecule of sodium along with one molecule of glucose into the cell. This process is a form of secondary active transport. The absorption of sodium and glucose creates an osmotic gradient that drives the passive absorption of water, effectively rehydrating the patient. SGLT2 (B) is found in the kidneys, while GLUT transporters (C, D) are involved in facilitated diffusion of glucose.

Question 9

A patients body fluid compartment analysis shows the following ion concentrations : sodium 10 mEq/L, potassium 140 mEq/L, and chloride 15 mEq/L. Based on these electrolyte values, which fluid compartment is being represented?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The bodys fluid compartments have distinct ionic compositions. The intracellular fluid (ICF), which is the fluid inside the cells, is characterized by a high concentration of potassium (K+) and a low concentration of sodium (Na+) and chloride (Cl-). The values provided (K+ 140 mEq/L, Na+ 10 mEq/L) are classic for the intracellular environment. In contrast, the extracellular fluid (ECF), which includes plasma and interstitial fluid, is characterized by a high concentration of sodium (around 140 mEq/L) and chloride, and a very low concentration of potassium (around 4 mEq/L). The given ion profile is the inverse of the ECF and is definitively representative of the intracellular fluid compartment.

Question 11

A young patient presents with muscle spasms, tingling in the hands and feet, seizures, and episodes of breathing difficulty due to laryngospasm, and laboratory investigations show an electrolyte abnormality. What is the most likely underlying cause of these symptoms?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The patients symptoms of muscle spasms (tetany), perioral and extremity numbness (paresthesias), seizures, and laryngospasm are all signs of acute hypocalcemia. Specifically, they are caused by a decrease in the level of ionized calcium in the blood. Acute respiratory alkalosis, often caused by hyperventilation, is a common cause of these symptoms. The increase in blood pH (alkalosis) causes more calcium to bind to albumin, which decreases the physiologically active ionized calcium fraction. This reduction in ionized calcium increases neuromuscular excitability, leading to tetany and the other neurological symptoms described. While other electrolyte imbalances can cause some of these symptoms, the full constellation in an acute setting is highly suggestive of symptomatic hypocalcemia secondary to respiratory alkalosis.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

BenzhexolThe patient has developed a drug-induced acute dystonic reaction, a type of extrapyramidal symptom (EPS). Question 14

A patient presents with dry eyes, a gritty or foreign-body sensation, and evidence of corneal softening. What is the most probable underlying cause?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The ocular manifestations described are classic signs of severe Vitamin A deficiency, collectively known as xerophthalmia. The initial symptom is often night blindness, followed by xerosis, which is an abnormal dryness of the conjunctiva and cornea, leading to a gritty sensation. As the deficiency worsens, it can lead to keratomalacia, which is a softening, melting, and liquefaction of the cornea. The combination of dry eyes and corneal softening is highly specific for advanced Vitamin A deficiency. Riboflavin deficiency can cause ocular symptoms, but not corneal softening.

Question 16

Corneal transparency is primarily maintained by which glycosaminoglycan (GAG)?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The transparency of the cornea is a remarkable feature that depends on the highly organized and uniform arrangement of its collagen fibrils within the stroma. The specific glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) present in the corneal stroma play a crucial role in maintaining this precise architecture and hydration level. The predominant GAGs in the cornea are keratan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate. Keratan sulfate, in particular, is essential for regulating the spacing between collagen fibrils and controlling stromal hydration. This precise organization minimizes light scattering and allows light to pass through unimpeded, thus maintaining corneal transparency. Defects in keratan sulfate metabolism, as seen in macular corneal dystrophy, lead to corneal clouding.

Question 18

A patient eats dinner at 8 PM and undergoes a blood glucose test at 7 AM the next morning. What is the primary source of glucose in the blood at this time?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The blood test is performed after an 11-hour overnight fast. During the post-absorptive or early fasting state (from about 4 to 12-16 hours after a meal), the primary mechanism for maintaining blood glucose homeostasis is the breakdown of stored glycogen in the liver, a process called hepatic glycogenolysis. The liver stores enough glycogen to supply the bodys glucose needs for this period. As the fast progresses beyond 12 hours, liver glycogen stores become depleted, and gluconeogenesis (C), the synthesis of new glucose primarily in the liver, becomes the predominant source. Muscle glycogen (A) cannot be released into the bloodstream to maintain blood glucose because muscle lacks the enzyme glucose-6-phosphatase. Dietary carbohydrates (D) are exhausted within a few hours of the meal.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The history of urine turning black upon standing in an otherwise healthy child is the pathognomonic sign of alkaptonuria. This is a rare autosomal recessive disorder of tyrosine metabolism caused by a deficiency of the enzyme homogentisate oxidase (also known as homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase). Question 25

Which of the following factors is considered a poor prognostic marker in multiple myeloma?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Beta-2 microglobulin (β2M) is a small protein found on the surface of most nucleated cells and is shed into the blood. In multiple myeloma, its serum level correlates directly with the tumor burden and disease activity. A high serum β2M level indicates a large number of myeloma cells and is one of the most powerful independent poor prognostic markers. It is a key component of the International Staging System (ISS) and the Revised-ISS for multiple myeloma, where higher levels are associated with more advanced stages and shorter survival. While elevated serum creatinine (A) and hypercalcemia (B) are common complications and indicate end-organ damage, β2M is a more direct and powerful measure of tumor load and prognosis.

Question 33

While recording evidence in a court of law, a lawyer asked the witness, Were you present when A killed B?, and the witness answered yes. This type of leading questioning is permitted in :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

A leading question is one that suggests the desired answer or contains the information the questioner is looking to have confirmed (e.g., You were at the scene, werent you?). According to the rules of evidence, leading questions are generally prohibited during direct examination (or examination-in-chief) (A, B) and re-direct examination (C), where the lawyer is questioning their own witness. The purpose of these examinations is to have the witness provide their testimony in their own words. However, leading questions are permitted during cross-examination, where a lawyer is questioning a witness called by the opposing party. The goal of cross-examination is to challenge the witnesss testimony and test their credibility.

Question 37

A patient is being treated with metronidazole for an anaerobic infection. Which substance or medication should be avoided during this treatment period?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Patients taking metronidazole must be strictly advised to avoid consuming alcohol during treatment and for at least 48-72 hours after the last dose. Metronidazole can cause a disulfiram-like reaction when taken with alcohol. It inhibits the enzyme aldehyde dehydrogenase, which is responsible for breaking down acetaldehyde, a toxic metabolite of alcohol. The accumulation of acetaldehyde in the body leads to a variety of unpleasant symptoms, including severe flushing, throbbing headache, nausea, vomiting, sweating, tachycardia, and hypotension. This reaction can be severe and distressing, so complete avoidance of all alcohol-containing products (including certain medications and mouthwashes) is essential.

The infection causes inflammation and filling of the alveoli with foamy proteinaceous material, impairing gas exchange.On imaging, chest X-ray may show bilateral diffuse interstitial infiltrates, though in some cases focal consolidation may occur. Because the patient’s CD4 count is far below 200, PCP becomes the most common and clinically important cause of pneumonia in such individuals.Therefore, the most likely pathogen responsible for this presentation is Pneumocystis jirovecii.

Question 43

Which parasite produces microfilariae that are sheathed and lack nuclei at the tail end?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The microscopic differentiation of various microfilariae species is based on key features, including the presence or absence of a sheath and the arrangement of nuclei in the tail. The microfilaria of Wuchereria bancrofti is characterized by being sheathed and having a tail tip that is devoid of nuclei. In contrast, Brugia malayi (B) is also sheathed but has two distinct, terminal nuclei at the tip of its tail. Loa loa (C) is sheathed, and its nuclei extend continuously to the tip of the tail. Onchocerca volvulus (D) is unsheathed and has no nuclei in its tail tip. The combination of a sheath and an anucleated tail tip is the specific identifying feature for W. bancrofti.

Question 44

A patient develops food poisoning after consuming pastries. Which organism is the most likely causative agent?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Food poisoning characterized by a rapid onset of nausea and vomiting (typically within 1-6 hours) after consuming contaminated food is classic for Staphylococcus aureus intoxication. This bacterium produces a heat-stable enterotoxin. Foods that are rich in protein and are handled and then left at room temperature, such as cream-filled pastries, custards, potato salad, and processed meats, are common vehicles for transmission. The toxin is pre-formed in the food, which is why the symptoms appear so quickly after ingestion. The emetic (vomiting) form of Bacillus cereus food poisoning (B) has a similar presentation and is associated with reheated rice, but S. aureus is classically linked to foods like pastries.

Question 45

A female engineer works 12–14 hours daily and subsists mainly on fast food with little to no intake of fruits or vegetables. Her hemoglobin level is 9 g/dL, mean corpuscular volume is 120 fL, and peripheral smear shows macrocytosis. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The patient presents with macrocytic anemia, as indicated by a low hemoglobin level (9 g/dL) and a high mean corpuscular volume (MCV of 120 fL). The key to the diagnosis is the dietary history. Folic acid (folate) is a B vitamin that is abundant in fresh fruits and leafy green vegetables. A diet completely devoid of these items, consisting only of fast food, is a classic cause of folic acid deficiency. Folate is essential for DNA synthesis, and its deficiency impairs the maturation of red blood cell precursors in the bone marrow, leading to the production of large, abnormal red cells (macrocytes). While vitamin B12 deficiency also causes macrocytic anemia, it is more commonly associated with vegan diets or malabsorption issues.

Question 46

Which of the following methods is most appropriate for the proper management and cleanup of a blood spill?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

For the disinfection of surfaces contaminated with blood or other potentially infectious body fluids, the most recommended and widely used disinfectant is sodium hypochlorite (household bleach). It is a broad-spectrum, fast-acting disinfectant that is effective against viruses (including HIV and Hepatitis B), bacteria, and fungi. For cleaning blood spills, a 1 :10 dilution of bleach (approximately a 0.5% solution) is typically recommended. The area should be cleaned of visible organic matter first, then flooded with the disinfectant solution and allowed a sufficient contact time (e.g., 10 minutes) before being wiped clean. Alcohols are less effective in the presence of organic matter, and formaldehyde is a high-level disinfectant/sterilant not used for routine surface cleaning.

Question 47

A truck driver presents with a painless, well-defined ulcer on the penis along with inguinal lymphadenopathy. What is the best technique to demonstrate the motility of the most likely causative organism?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The clinical presentation of a painless ulcer (chancre) on the genitals accompanied by regional lymphadenopathy is classic for primary syphilis, caused by the spirochete Treponema pallidum. This organism is a thin, spiral-shaped bacterium that is too narrow to be seen with a standard light microscope. The definitive method for visualizing the live spirochete directly from an ulcer exudate is dark-field microscopy. This technique uses a special condenser to illuminate the specimen from the side, so that only light scattered by the organism enters the objective lens. This makes the spirochetes appear as bright, motile spirals against a dark background, allowing for identification based on their characteristic corkscrew motility.

Question 48

Swab is discarded in which color bin

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

According to biomedical waste management guidelines, waste is segregated into different colored bins based on its nature and disposal method. Any item contaminated with human blood or body fluids, such as used cotton swabs, dressings, and plaster casts, is considered infectious or soiled waste. This category of waste must be discarded in the yellow bag. Waste collected in yellow bags is destined for incineration or deep burial to ensure the complete destruction of potentially infectious pathogens. Red bags are for recyclable contaminated waste like plastics (syringes, catheters), white bags are for sharps (needles), and blue bags are for glass waste.

Question 49

A patient is suspected of having brucellosis, and a serum sample is tested using the standard agglutination test. The test is initially negative but turns positive after dilution of the serum. What is the most likely explanation for the initial negative result?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

This scenario describes the prozone phenomenon. This is a serological effect that occurs in agglutination tests when the concentration of antibodies in the patients serum is excessively high. This antibody excess interferes with the formation of the large antigen-antibody lattice networks that are necessary for visible agglutination to occur, leading to a false-negative result in undiluted or low-dilution samples. By serially diluting the serum, the antibody concentration is reduced to an optimal level, allowing for proper cross-linking and lattice formation, which then results in a positive test. This phenomenon is a well-known issue in agglutination tests for diseases like brucellosis and syphilis, necessitating the testing of multiple dilutions.

Question 50

A patient has been diagnosed with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG). On taking history, it is noted that the patient is a known case of bronchial asthma. Which medication is the drug of choice for managing POAG in this patient?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The management of Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma (POAG) in a patient with bronchial asthma requires careful consideration of medication side effects. Topical beta-blockers, such as timolol, are a common first-line treatment for POAG but are relatively contraindicated in patients with asthma because they can be absorbed systemically and cause bronchoconstriction by blocking beta-2 receptors in the lungs. Therefore, an alternative first-line agent is necessary. Prostaglandin analogs, such as Latanoprost, are considered the drug of choice in this scenario. Latanoprost effectively lowers intraocular pressure by increasing the uveoscleral outflow of aqueous humor. Crucially, it has minimal systemic side effects and does not affect the respiratory system, making it a safe and highly effective option for glaucoma patients with co-existing asthma. The other options are also prostaglandins but are used for different indications (e.g., obstetrics, erectile dysfunction) and are not formulated as eye drops for glaucoma.
