Question 3

Which muscle functions to tense the vocal folds?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The pitch of the voice is primarily controlled by altering the tension and length of the vocal cords. The muscle solely responsible for tensing and elongating the vocal cords is the cricothyroid muscle. It is the only intrinsic laryngeal muscle located on the external surface of the larynx and is uniquely innervated by the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve. When the cricothyroid muscle contracts, it pulls the thyroid cartilage anteriorly and inferiorly, tilting it forward on the cricoid cartilage. This action increases the distance between the thyroid and arytenoid cartilages, thereby stretching and tensing the vocal ligaments. Tenser vocal cords vibrate at a higher frequency, resulting in the production of a higher-pitched sound. The thyroarytenoid muscles (C), particularly the vocalis part, have the opposite effect; they shorten and relax the vocal cords, lowering the pitch. The lateral cricoarytenoid (B) adducts the cords, and the posterior cricoarytenoid (D) abducts them. Hence, for vocal cord tension and high pitch, the cricothyroid is the key muscle.

Question 5

Which of the following statements about Weber’s syndrome is incorrect?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Weber's syndrome is a midbrain stroke syndrome caused by an infarction in the territory of the posterior cerebral artery, specifically affecting the anterior cerebral peduncle (D). This area contains two crucial structures : the descending corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts, and the exiting fascicles of the oculomotor nerve (CN III). Therefore, a lesion here produces a characteristic pattern of deficits. It causes an ipsilateral oculomotor nerve palsy (B), leading to a down and out eye position, ptosis, and a dilated pupil on the same side as the lesion. It also affects the corticospinal tract before it decussates, resulting in contralateral hemiplegia or hemiparesis (A), affecting the opposite side of the body. Contralateral Parkinsonism (C) — characterized by tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia — is caused by damage to the substantia nigra, which is located more dorsally in the midbrain tegmentum. A lesion affecting the substantia nigra along with the red nucleus and CN III causes Benedikt's syndrome, not Weber's. Therefore, contralateral Parkinsonism is not a feature of classic Weber's syndrome.

Question 16

What happens when the ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) ratio becomes infinite?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) ratio describes the relationship between the amount of air reaching the alveoli (ventilation, V) and the amount of blood flow through the pulmonary capillaries (perfusion, Q). A V/Q ratio of infinity () mathematically implies that ventilation is occurring (V \> 0) but perfusion is absent (Q = 0). This condition is known as alveolar dead space. A classic example is a pulmonary embolism blocking a pulmonary artery. In this scenario, air is moving in and out of the alveoli, but because there is no blood flow to these alveoli, no gas exchange can occur (B). The air within these dead space alveoli will not give up any oxygen to the blood, nor will it receive any carbon dioxide from the blood. As a result, the composition of the alveolar gas in this unit will become identical to that of the inspired air : the partial pressure of O2 will be approximately 150 mmHg, and the partial pressure of CO2 will be effectively zero (C).

Question 18

Alpha waves are observed in which physiological state?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Electroencephalography (EEG) records the electrical activity of the brain using different frequency bands. Alpha waves, which have a frequency of 8-13 Hz, are the dominant brain wave pattern in a normal adult who is in a quiet, relaxed, and wakeful state with their eyes closed. They are most prominent over the occipital and parietal regions of the brain. When the person opens their eyes or begins to concentrate or perform a mental task (an active state, D), the alpha rhythm is blocked or attenuated and is replaced by faster, lower amplitude beta waves (\>13 Hz). As a person becomes drowsy and falls asleep (A), the alpha waves disappear and are replaced by the slower theta (4-7 Hz) and delta (\<4 Hz) waves characteristic of deeper sleep stages. REM sleep (B) has an EEG pattern that resembles the awake state, with fast, low-voltage activity, but not typically a prominent alpha rhythm. Thus, the classic state for alpha waves is relaxed wakefulness with eyes closed.

In hypovolemic shock, there is a significant reduction in circulating blood volume due to causes such as hemorrhage, dehydration, or severe fluid loss. Question 21

By which mechanism is glucose absorbed in the intestine?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The absorption of glucose and galactose from the lumen of the small intestine into the enterocytes occurs primarily via secondary active transport. This process is mediated by the Sodium-Glucose Linked Transporter 1 (SGLT1), which is located on the apical (luminal) membrane of the intestinal epithelial cells. This transporter couples the movement of one molecule of glucose (or galactose) with the movement of two sodium ions into the cell. The transport of sodium down its steep electrochemical gradient (which is maintained by the Na+/K+-ATPase pump on the basolateral membrane, a form of primary active transport) provides the energy to move glucose against its concentration gradient into the cell. Once inside the cell, glucose then exits across the basolateral membrane into the bloodstream via facilitated diffusion (B) through the GLUT2 transporter. Simple diffusion (C) and primary active transport (D) are not the primary mechanisms for luminal glucose uptake.

Question 26

Lesch–Nyhan syndrome arises due to a deficiency of which enzyme?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Hypoxanthine-guanine phosphori bosyltrans ferase (HGPRT)Lesch-Nyhan syndrome is a rare, X-linked recessive disorder of purine metabolism. It is caused by a severe deficiency of the enzyme hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT). This enzyme is a key component of the purine salvage pathway, responsible for recycling the purine bases hypoxanthine and guanine back into the purine nucleotide pool (forming IMP and GMP, respectively). When HGPRT is deficient, these purine bases cannot be salvaged and are instead degraded into uric acid. Furthermore, the lack of salvage leads to a feedback-driven increase in de novo purine synthesis. The combination of these effects results in a massive overproduction and accumulation of uric acid (hyperuricemia). This leads to the clinical manifestations of the disease, which include severe gouty arthritis, kidney stones, and the pathognomonic neurological features of cognitive impairment, dystonia, and compulsive self-injurious behavior.

DNA polymerase I is an important enzyme involved in DNA replication in prokaryotic organisms. Question 31

What do chaperones assist in:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Chaperones, also known as heat shock proteins (HSPs), are a functionally related group of proteins whose primary role is to assist in the correct folding of other proteins. As a newly synthesized polypeptide chain emerges from the ribosome, it must fold into a specific, stable three-dimensional conformation to become biologically active. This process is complex and prone to errors, such as misfolding and aggregation. Chaperones bind to unfolded or partially folded polypeptide chains, preventing them from aggregating and facilitating their correct folding into the proper functional structure. They do not form part of the final protein structure but act as catalysts for the folding process. They are also involved in refolding proteins that have been denatured by stress (like heat shock) and in assembling multi-protein complexes. They are not primarily involved in cleavage (A), degradation (C), or post-translational modification (D) of proteins.

This anchor is used to attach dozens of proteins to the cell surface. In PNH, affected blood cells lack all GPI-anchored proteins. CD59 is a crucial protein that normally protects cells from damage by the complement system by preventing the formation of the terminal membrane attack complex (MAC). The absence of CD59 on PNH red blood cells makes them exquisitely sensitive to destruction by complement, leading to chronic hemolysis. The diagnosis of PNH is confirmed by using flow cytometry to demonstrate the absence of CD59 and CD55 on blood cells.

Question 43

Which of the following is an opsonin?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Opsonization is a critical process in the immune system where pathogens or particles are coated with proteins called opsonins. This coating enhances the ability of phagocytic cells, such as macrophages and neutrophils, to recognize, engulf, and destroy the pathogen. The complement system is a major source of opsonins. When the complement cascade is activated, the central component, C3, is cleaved into two fragments : C3a and C3b. The smaller fragment, C3a, is an anaphylatoxin that promotes inflammation. The larger fragment, C3b, is the most important and abundant opsonin produced by the complement system. It covalently binds to the surface of pathogens. Phagocytic cells have specific complement receptors (like CR1) that recognize and bind to the deposited C3b, triggering efficient phagocytosis. While IgG antibodies are also major opsonins, C3b is the key opsonin from the complement pathway. C5a (C) is also an anaphylatoxin and a powerful chemoattractant for neutrophils.

Question 44

Bernard–Soulier syndrome occurs due to a deficiency of which factor?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Bernard-Soulier syndrome is a rare, inherited bleeding disorder characterized by a defect in primary hemostasis (platelet plug formation). It is an autosomal recessive condition caused by a quantitative or qualitative deficiency of the glycoprotein Ib-IX-V complex (GpIb-IX-V) on the surface of platelets. The GpIb component of this complex serves as the primary receptor for von Willebrand factor (vWF). This GpIb-vWF interaction is essential for the initial step of platelet adhesion to the exposed subendothelium at a site of vascular injury, especially under conditions of high shear stress.

Question 46

Which of the following best explains the Flipping effect?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is an enzyme that exists as five different isoenzymes (LDH1 to LDH5), which have different tissue distributions. In normal serum, the concentration of the isoenzymes follows the pattern LDH2 \> LDH1 \> LDH3 \> LDH4 \> LDH5. The heart muscle is particularly rich in LDH1, while red blood cells are also rich in LDH1 and LDH2. In the setting of an acute myocardial infarction (MI), damaged cardiac muscle cells release their contents, including large amounts of LDH1, into the bloodstream. This causes the serum level of LDH1 to rise significantly, to the point where its concentration exceeds that of LDH2. This reversal of the normal ratio, where LDH1 \> LDH2, is known as the flipped pattern or flipping effect. While troponins are now the preferred cardiac markers, the LDH flip was historically a key and relatively specific indicator of myocardial infarction, typically appearing 12-24 hours after the onset of chest pain.

Question 50

PAS-positive macrophages are characteristically observed in which of the following conditions?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Whipple’s diseasePAS-positive macrophages are a hallmark histopathological feature of Whipple’s disease, a rare chronic systemic infection caused by the bacterium Tropheryma whipplei. In this condition, large numbers of foamy macrophages accumulate in the lamina propria of the small intestine, particularly in the duodenum and jejunum. These macrophages contain glycoprotein-rich bacterial remnants that stain strongly with Periodic Acid–Schiff (PAS), giving them a distinctive magenta appearance under the microscope. The disease interferes with normal intestinal absorption, leading to symptoms such as chronic diarrhea, weight loss, malnutrition, abdominal pain, and sometimes joint pain or neurological manifestations. Although PAS staining can highlight certain organisms or cellular components in other diseases, the presence of abundant PAS-positive macrophages in intestinal biopsies is considered highly characteristic of Whipple’s disease. Crohn’s disease and AIDS do not typically show this specific pathological finding, making option A the correct answer.
