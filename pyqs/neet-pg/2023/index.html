Question 1

A patient presents with progressively increasing pain associated with a swelling located below the ear lobule. Involvement of which of the following anatomical structures is responsible for this pain?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The swelling described is located in the parotid gland region. The gland is enclosed by a tough, inelastic capsule formed by the investing layer of the deep cervical fascia. When the gland swells, this capsule stretches, causing intense pain. The sensory innervation for the parotid capsule and the overlying skin is provided by the great auricular nerve, a branch of the cervical plexus (C2, C3). Therefore, it is the nerve responsible for transmitting the pain sensation. The auriculotemporal nerve (B) carries secretomotor fibers to the gland. The facial nerve (A) provides motor supply to the muscles of facial expression and passes through the gland but does not convey pain sensation from it. While the deep cervical fascia (D) is the structure being stretched, the question implicitly asks for the neurological structure responsible for perceiving the pain, making the great auricular nerve the most accurate answer.

Question 3

In a patient of liver cirrhosis anastomotic blood flow increase in

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Liver cirrhosis leads to portal hypertension, which forces blood from the high-pressure portal system into the low-pressure systemic venous system through sites of portosystemic anastomosis. One of the most critical of these sites is at the gastroesophageal junction. Here, blood from the left gastric vein (a tributary of the portal vein) is shunted into the esophageal veins (which drain into the systemic azygos vein). The resulting massive increase in blood flow through this connection causes the veins to dilate, forming esophageal varices. These varices are prone to rupture and can cause life-threatening upper gastrointestinal bleeding, a major complication of cirrhosis. The other options do not represent major portosystemic anastomoses; they are either connections within the portal system or entirely within the systemic circulation.

Question 4

In a 35-year-old patient with intravascular hemolysis, the serum potassium level rises to 5.5 mEq/L. What effect will this have on the resting membrane potential in this patient?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The resting membrane potential (RMP) is primarily established by the potassium (K+) concentration gradient across the cell membrane, with a higher concentration inside the cell. The RMP is negative due to the constant efflux of positive K+ ions. Intravascular hemolysis releases K+ from red blood cells, causing hyperkalemia (increased extracellular K+). This reduces the concentration gradient between the intracellular and extracellular compartments. According to the Nernst and Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equations, a smaller K+ gradient reduces the driving force for K+ to exit the cell. With less positive charge leaving the cell, the interior becomes less negative, meaning the RMP shifts to a more positive value (e.g., from -70mV to -60mV). This depolarization moves the RMP closer to the threshold for firing an action potential, initially increasing excitability.

Question 5

In cases of infant respiratory distress syndrome (IRDS), there is a deficiency of which specific lipid?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Infant respiratory distress syndrome (IRDS) is caused by a deficiency of pulmonary surfactant in the lungs of premature newborns. Surfactant is a complex that reduces alveolar surface tension, preventing the air sacs from collapsing at the end of expiration. The most crucial component of surfactant is a phospholipid called dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC). DPPC is a specific type of lecithin, which is a general term for any phosphatidylcholine. The maturity of fetal lungs is often assessed by the lecithin-to-sphingomyelin (L/S) ratio in amniotic fluid; a ratio above 2 indicates sufficient surfactant production and lung maturity. In IRDS, the production of lecithin is inadequate, leading to respiratory failure. The other lipids listed are not the primary deficient components.

Question 8

A family that predominantly consumes polished rice presents with signs of a vitamin deficiency. Which vitamin deficiency is likely, and which investigation is used to detect it?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Polished rice is rice that has had its outer bran and germ layers removed. These layers are the primary dietary source of Thiamine (Vitamin B1). A diet heavily reliant on polished rice is a classic cause of thiamine deficiency, which leads to the condition known as beriberi. The functional status of thiamine in the body is biochemically assessed by measuring the activity of the enzyme transketolase in red blood cells. Transketolase, an enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway, requires thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) as a cofactor. In a thiamine-deficient state, the enzymes baseline activity is low, but it increases significantly when TPP is added to the test tube, confirming the deficiency.

Question 9

When the insulin-to-glucagon ratio is low, which of the following enzymes is stimulated?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

A low insulin-to-glucagon ratio is the hormonal signal of the fasting state, indicating a need to mobilize stored energy. In adipose tissue, glucagon and low insulin levels lead to the activation of Hormone-Sensitive Lipase (HSL). HSL is the key intracellular enzyme that catalyzes the hydrolysis of stored triglycerides into free fatty acids and glycerol. These products are then released into the bloodstream to be used as fuel by other tissues like the liver and muscle, through processes like beta-oxidation and gluconeogenesis. In contrast, insulin (a high insulin:glucagon ratio) stimulates enzymes involved in storage, such as Lipoprotein lipase (A) for fat uptake, Phosphofructokinase I (C) for glycolysis, and Glycogen synthase (D) for glycogen synthesis.

Question 10

A 4-year-old child presents with easy fatigability. The mother reports that the child becomes very hungry between meals but recovers after eating. Liver examination shows no glycogen storage. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The clinical presentation of fasting hypoglycemia (fatigue and hunger between meals) that is relieved by feeding, combined with the definitive finding of an absence of glycogen in the liver, points to a defect in glycogen synthesis. The key enzyme responsible for creating glycogen from glucose is glycogen synthase. A deficiency in this enzyme (Glycogen Storage Disease Type 0) means the liver cannot store glucose as glycogen after a meal. As a result, during a fast, the liver has no glycogen stores to break down (glycogenolysis) to maintain blood glucose levels, leading to hypoglycemia. Deficiencies in branching (A) or debranching (B) enzymes lead to abnormal glycogen structures, not absence. Glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency (C) leads to a massive accumulation of normal glycogen.

Question 11

A 4-year-old patient presents with lymphadenopathy. On examination, generalized lymphadenopathy is noted without organomegaly. Peripheral blood smear shows lymphocytosis with smudge cells. What is the next appropriate step in the diagnostic workup?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

This clinical presentation is highly characteristic of Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL), a malignancy of mature B-lymphocytes. Key features include an older patient, generalized lymphadenopathy, significant lymphocytosis, and the presence of smudge cells (fragile neoplastic lymphocytes) on the peripheral smear. The gold standard and essential next step to confirm the diagnosis is immunophenotyping by flow cytometry on the peripheral blood. This test identifies a monoclonal population of B-cells expressing a unique pattern of surface markers (typically CD19, CD20-dim, CD5, and CD23). This confirms the clonality and specific cell type, establishing the diagnosis of CLL without initially needing more invasive procedures like a bone marrow (BM) examination or lymph node biopsy, which are reserved for atypical cases or staging.

Question 14

Which of the following statements are true regarding the relative risk associated with Mendelian patterns of inheritance?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

This question asks for the correct recurrence risk for an affected child in a typical Mendelian inheritance scenario. For an autosomal recessive disorder, an affected child (genotype aa) is most commonly born to two heterozygous carrier parents (genotype Aa). In such a cross (Aa x Aa), the probability for each pregnancy is as follows: a 1 in 4 chance of having an affected child (aa), a 2 in 4 (or 1/2) chance of having an unaffected carrier child (Aa), and a 1 in 4 chance of having an unaffected, non-carrier child (AA). Therefore, the risk of having another child affected with the same autosomal recessive condition is 1/4 or 25%. The other options are incorrect for standard Mendelian patterns.

Question 16

Correct answer: C

The autopsy method that involves removing the thoracic, cervical, and abdominal organs as a single, large unit is known as the Letulle technique. This is also referred to as the en masse or en bloc method. By keeping all the organ systems connected, this technique allows for excellent examination of anatomical relationships, which is particularly useful for tracing the pathways of trauma, metastatic cancer, or complex congenital anomalies. The Virchow technique (B) involves removing organs one by one. The Rokitansky technique (A) is an in situ dissection. Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The clinical syndrome described is classic for envenomation by a viper snake (such as a Russells viper or saw-scaled viper), which are common in agricultural areas. Question 20

The Letulle technique is an autopsy method in which all thoracic, abdominal, and pelvic organs are removed together as a single organ block and then dissected outside the body. Question 22

A patient with elevated uric acid levels is receiving treatment for gout. Which of the following drugs acts by inhibiting the synthesis of uric acid?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Uric acid is the final product of purine metabolism. The enzyme xanthine oxidase catalyzes the last two steps of this pathway. Allopurinol is a xanthine oxidase inhibitor. By blocking this enzyme, allopurinol decreases the production of uric acid, thereby lowering serum uric acid levels and preventing gout attacks. It is a first-line medication for the chronic management of gout. In contrast, probenecid (A) is a uricosuric agent that works by increasing the excretion of uric acid by the kidneys. Colchicine (C) and diclofenac (D) are anti-inflammatory drugs used to treat the pain and inflammation of an acute gout flare, but they do not alter uric acid levels.

Question 26

A patient with organophosphate poisoning was treated with atropine infusion and pralidoxime. Two hours later, the patient developed a high-grade fever. What is the most likely cause of this fever?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

In the treatment of organophosphate (OP) poisoning, high doses of atropine are administered to block the muscarinic effects of excess acetylcholine. A significant anticholinergic side effect of atropine is the inhibition of sweating (anhidrosis) by blocking muscarinic receptors on sweat glands. The inability to sweat severely impairs the bodys ability to dissipate heat, which can lead to a rise in core body temperature, a condition known as atropine fever. This is a direct pharmacological consequence of the high-dose atropine therapy required in this setting. The fever is not a typical effect of pralidoxime (A) or the OP poisoning itself (C), and increased secretions (D) would cause heat loss, not fever.

Question 30

Which drug is the treatment of choice for dialysis disequilibrium syndrome?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Dialysis Disequilibrium Syndrome (DDS) is a neurological complication resulting from cerebral edema. It occurs when hemodialysis removes urea from the blood much faster than from the brain, creating an osmotic gradient that pulls water into the brain cells. The treatment aims to counteract this osmotic shift. Mannitol is an osmotic diuretic. When administered intravenously, it increases the osmolarity of the blood, creating a reverse osmotic gradient that pulls fluid out of the brain tissue and into the vasculature, thereby reducing cerebral edema and intracranial pressure. Loop diuretics like ethacrynic acid (A) and bumetanide (D) do not address the central osmotic imbalance. Aspirin (C) has no role in this condition.

Question 33

What is the mechanism of action of t PA?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

t-PA stands for tissue Plasminogen Activator. It is a proteolytic enzyme that plays a crucial role in dissolving blood clots. Its mechanism of action is fibrinolytic. t-PA functions by binding to fibrin within an existing thrombus (blood clot). This binding activates t-PA, enabling it to convert plasminogen, an inactive proenzyme that is also present in the clot, into its active form, plasmin. Plasmin is a potent enzyme that directly degrades the fibrin meshwork which forms the structural integrity of the clot. This enzymatic breakdown of fibrin leads to the dissolution of the clot. t-PA does not prevent clot formation (C) or have direct anti-platelet (D) or anti-thrombin (A) effects; it actively breaks down a pre-formed clot.

Question 41

A patient presented with hepatosplenomegaly and was found to have pallor on examination. A rapid antigen test was positive for HRP-2 antigen. Which species of Plasmodium is responsible for this infection?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) for malaria work by detecting specific antigens produced by the Plasmodium parasites. The Histidine-Rich Protein 2 (HRP-2) antigen is produced exclusively by Plasmodium falciparum. Therefore, a positive result for HRP-2 is diagnostic for an infection with P. falciparum. Question 48

A -year-old child presented with developmental delay (mental retardation) and was found to have a cherry-red spot on retinal examination. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The finding of a cherry-red spot on the retina is a classic ophthalmological sign seen in several lysosomal storage diseases. It occurs when lipids or other substances accumulate in the ganglion cells of the retina, making the surrounding macula appear pale or white. This accentuates the normal red color of the underlying choroid at the fovea, which is devoid of ganglion cells. In a child presenting with progressive neurodegeneration (mental retardation) and a cherry-red spot, the most likely diagnosis is Tay-Sachs disease, which is caused by a deficiency of the enzyme hexosaminidase A. Niemann-Pick disease (D) can also present with a cherry-red spot, but Tay-Sachs is the classic answer for this combination. Hurler and Hunter syndromes are mucopolysaccharidoses and are not typically associated with this retinal finding.
