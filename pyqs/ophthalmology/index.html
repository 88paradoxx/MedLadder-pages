Question 2

Which of the following agents leads to melanosis coli?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Melanosis coli is a benign condition characterized by a dark brown to black pigmentation of the colonic mucosa. It is not true melanin but rather a lipofuscin pigment deposited within macrophages in the lamina propria. This condition is strongly associated with the chronic use of a specific class of laxatives known as anthraquinone laxatives. This class includes senna, cascara, aloe, and rhubarb. These compounds are thought to induce apoptosis (programmed cell death) of the colonic epithelial cells. The apoptotic bodies, which contain lipofuscin, are then phagocytosed by macrophages. Over time, the accumulation of these pigment-laden macrophages creates the characteristic dark discoloration of the colon, which is visible on colonoscopy. Other types of laxatives, such as the osmotic laxatives sorbitol and magnesium sulfate or the stimulant laxative bisacodyl (a diphenylmethane derivative), are not associated with this condition.

Question 4

Mycoplasma is resistant to:

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Mycoplasma species are a unique type of bacteria that are intrinsically resistant to a major class of antibiotics. The defining characteristic of Mycoplasma is that they completely lack a cell wall. Antibiotics that target cell wall synthesis for their mechanism of action are therefore completely ineffective against them. This includes all beta-lactam antibiotics, such as penicillins and cephalosporins. Ceftriaxone is a third-generation cephalosporin and acts by inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis. Since Mycoplasma has no cell wall to target, it is naturally resistant to ceftriaxone. In contrast, Mycoplasma infections are treated with antibiotics that target other bacterial processes. These include tetracyclines (e.g., doxycycline) and macrolides (e.g., azithromycin), which inhibit protein synthesis by acting on the ribosome, and fluoroquinolones (e.g., levofloxacin), which inhibit DNA synthesis. Aminoglycosides also inhibit protein synthesis.

Question 9

Which special stain is employed to identify Cryptococcus?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Cryptococcus neoformans is an encapsulated yeast that is a major cause of meningitis in immuno compromised patients. A key and defining feature of this organism is its thick polysaccharide capsule. While a rapid diagnosis can often be made by visualizing this capsule with an India ink stain of the CSF (where the capsule appears as a clear halo around the yeast), a more specific histopathological stain can be used on tissue samples. The Mucicarmine stain is a special stain that has a strong affinity for the mucopolysaccharides that make up the cryptococcal capsule. It stains the capsule a bright rose-red or magenta color, making the organism stand out clearly against the background tissue. This staining reaction is highly characteristic and is considered a definitive method for identifying Cryptococcus in tissue biopsies, such as from the brain or lungs.

Correct answer: A

Horner syndrome is caused by a disruption of the sympathetic nerve supply to the eye and face. One of the classic signs is a mild ptosis (drooping of the upper eyelid), typically 1-2 mm. Correct answer: A

Question 21

A patient presents with a history of penetrating ocular trauma and is diagnosed with sympathetic ophthalmitis. Which of the following findings is most likely to be seen?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Sympathetic ophthalmia is a rare, bilateral, diffuse granulomatous inflammation that occurs after a penetrating injury or intraocular surgery to one eye (the exciting eye). A few weeks to many years after the initial trauma, an autoimmune response is triggered against exposed retinal antigens (like melanin-associated proteins), leading to inflammation in both the injured eye and the previously healthy, uninjured sympathizing eye. The inflammation characteristically affects the entire uveal tract—the iris, ciliary body, and choroid. Inflammation of all parts of the uveal tract is known as panuveitis. Clinically, this presents with signs of both anterior uveitis (pain, photophobia, keratic precipitates) and posterior uveitis (vitritis, choroidal lesions like Dalen-Fuchs nodules, and optic disc edema). It is a severe, sight-threatening condition requiring aggressive immunosuppressive therapy.

Question 27

A 3-year-old woman presents with loss of vision on the right side in both eyes, consistent with right homonymous hemianopia. What is the most likely underlying cause?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

A right homonymous hemianopia is a visual field defect where the patient loses vision in the right half of the visual field of both eyes. To understand its cause, one must trace the visual pathway. Light from the right visual field falls on the left half (nasal part of the right retina and temporal part of the left retina) of each eye. The fibers from the nasal retina of the right eye cross at the optic chiasm to join the fibers from the temporal retina of the left eye. These fibers then travel together in the left optic tract. Therefore, any lesion that damages the left optic tract, left optic radiation, or left visual cortex will cause a right homonymous hemianopia. A lesion of the right occipital lobe (B) would cause a left homonymous hemianopia.

Question 32

Incongruous homonymous hemianopia accompanied by Wernicke’s hemianopic pupil indicates a lesion in which anatomical structure?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

A lesion of the optic tract produces a contralateral homonymous hemianopia. Because the fibers from corresponding retinal points are not yet perfectly aligned in the optic tract, the visual field defects in each eye are typically incongruous (not identical in shape and size). Furthermore, the optic tract contains pupillary afferent fibers that are traveling towards the pretectal nucleus. A lesion in the optic tract will interrupt these fibers. Lesions in the optic radiation or cortex are post-geniculate; they cause congruous hemianopias and do not affect the pupillary light reflex.

Question 34

What condition must be present for sympathetic ophthalmia to develop?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Sympathetic ophthalmia is a rare, devastating, bilateral granulomatous panuveitis that occurs after trauma to one eye. The essential prerequisite for this condition is a penetrating (or perforating) injury to one eye, which is then called the exciting eye. The injury exposes intraocular antigens (such as retinal S-antigen) to the systemic immune system, which are normally sequestered and recognized as self. This breaks immune tolerance and triggers a delayed-type (Type IV) hypersensitivity reaction. Question 36

Which corneal layer is responsible for maintaining hydration of the corneal stroma?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The corneal stroma has a natural tendency to absorb fluid from the aqueous humor and swell, which would cause it to become hazy and lose its transparency. The maintenance of a relatively dehydrated state (deturgescence), which is essential for corneal clarity, is primarily the function of the corneal endothelium. The endothelium is a single layer of hexagonal cells lining the posterior surface of the cornea. These cells contain a high density of active Na+/K+-ATPase pumps. This metabolic pump actively transports ions out of the stroma and back into the aqueous humor. This creates an osmotic gradient that continuously draws excess fluid out of the stroma, counteracting its tendency to swell. Damage or dysfunction of the endothelium (e.g., in Fuchs' dystrophy or after eye surgery) leads to a failure of this pump function, resulting in corneal edema and loss of vision.

Question 41

A 65-year-old patient with a history of atrial flutter is planned for long-term prophylaxis to prevent recurrence. For rate control and prevention of rapid ventricular response, which calcium channel blocker is the most appropriate choice?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

For rate control in atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter, the goal is to slow conduction through the atrioventricular (AV) node. The calcium channel blockers that have this effect are the non-dihydropyridine agents. This class includes diltiazem and verapamil. They act on the L-type calcium channels in the AV nodal tissue, slowing the conduction of atrial impulses to the ventricles and thereby controlling the ventricular rate. In contrast, the dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, such as amlodipine (A), nifedipine (B), and felodipine (D), are potent peripheral vasodilators and have minimal to no effect on the AV node. In fact, by causing vasodilation and a reflex tachycardia, they can sometimes worsen the ventricular rate in atrial flutter or fibrillation. Therefore, for rate control, a non-dihydropyridine agent like diltiazem is the appropriate choice.

A diabetic patient currently treated with insulin glargine and metformin continues to have poor glycemic control (HbA1c 8.3%) and shows features of heart failure. Which medication would be the most appropriate addition to the treatment regimen?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The patient has inadequately controlled type 2 diabetes and concurrent heart failure. The choice of a third agent must consider both glycemic control and cardiovascular effects. The Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, such as empagliflozin, have emerged as a preferred class in this scenario. In contrast, pioglitazone (B), a thiazolidinedione, is contraindicated in patients with heart failure as it can cause fluid retention and worsen the condition. A sulfonylurea like gliclazide (D) or a DPP-4 inhibitor like sitagliptin (A) would help with glucose control but do not offer the specific proven benefit in heart failure that an SGLT2 inhibitor does.

Question 44

Which of the following functions or findings is reduced in Retinitis Pigmentosa?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP) is a group of inherited retinal dystrophies characterized by the progressive loss of photoreceptor cells (rods and cones), leading to night blindness and a gradual constriction of the visual fields. The outer segments of photoreceptor cells are highly specialized structures that are exceptionally rich in the omega-3 long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acid, Docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). DHA is a critical structural component of the photoreceptor membranes and plays a vital role in their function, including phototransduction and cell survival. Studies in patients with various forms of RP have shown that there is often a systemic deficiency or a metabolic defect leading to decreased plasma levels of DHA. While the exact role is still being investigated, this deficiency is thought to contribute to the progressive photoreceptor degeneration. This has led to clinical trials exploring the potential therapeutic benefit of DHA supplementation in slowing the progression of the disease.

Question 45

What is against-the-rule correction in astigmatism?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Astigmatism is a refractive error where the eye's cornea or lens has different curvatures in different meridians. In against-the-rule (ATR) astigmatism, the horizontal meridian (at 180 degrees) is steeper (has more focusing power) than the vertical meridian (at 90 degrees). To correct this with a spectacle lens, a minus cylinder is used to neutralize the excess power of the steep meridian. The axis of a minus cylinder lens is placed 90 degrees away from the meridian it is intended to correct. Therefore, to correct the steep horizontal (180-degree) meridian in ATR astigmatism, the axis of the minus cylinder must be placed vertically, at or near 90 degrees. A prescription written as -1.25 cyl 90° signifies a minus cylinder with its axis at 90 degrees, which is the correct form for correcting against-the-rule astigmatism. Conversely, a minus cylinder with its axis at 180 degrees would be used to correct with-the-rule astigmatism, where the vertical meridian is steeper.
