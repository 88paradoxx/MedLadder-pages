Question 1

Scissor gait is typically observed in which condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

A scissor gait is a characteristic type of spastic gait that is a hallmark of spastic cerebral palsy, particularly the spastic diplegia subtype. Cerebral palsy is a group of permanent movement disorders that appear in early childhood. In spastic diplegia, there is damage to the upper motor neurons that control the lower limbs, leading to increased muscle tone (spasticity), particularly in the hip adductor and hamstring muscles. This spasticity of the hip adductors is so pronounced that it causes the thighs and knees to be pulled tightly together and even cross over each other in a scissor-like fashion during walking. The patient walks on their toes with their knees and hips partially flexed. This abnormal gait pattern is highly characteristic of the upper motor neuron damage seen in spastic cerebral palsy.

Question 2

Which limb position or attitude is typically observed in a patient with a posterior hip dislocation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

A posterior dislocation of the hip is the most common type of hip dislocation, accounting for approximately 90% of cases. It typically results from a high-energy traumatic force, such as a dashboard injury in a motor vehicle accident. The injury forces the femoral head out of the acetabulum in a posterior and superior direction. This displacement, combined with the unopposed pull of the surrounding muscles (particularly the gluteus medius/minimus and iliopsoas), results in a highly characteristic and pathognomonic deformity or attitude of the limb. The affected leg is held in a position of flexion, adduction, and internal rotation. The limb will also appear shortened. This specific posture is a key clinical sign that allows for immediate diagnosis upon inspection. In contrast, an anterior dislocation of the hip results in the limb being held in abduction and external rotation.

Question 4

Which region of the scaphoid bone is most prone to developing avascular necrosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The scaphoid bone of the wrist has a tenuous and unique blood supply. The majority of its blood supply (70-80%) enters through small foramina in the dorsal ridge, located in its distal half, and then flows in a retrograde fashion to supply the proximal part of the bone. The proximal pole (proximal 1/3rd) has a very limited or non-existent direct blood supply. When the scaphoid is fractured, particularly through its waist (the middle 1/3rd), this retrograde blood supply to the proximal fragment can be completely disrupted. This interruption of blood flow makes the proximal pole highly susceptible to developing avascular necrosis (AVN), a condition where the bone tissue dies due to a lack of blood supply. The risk of AVN increases significantly with more proximal fractures. This complication can lead to non-union of the fracture, collapse of the bone, and subsequent degenerative arthritis of the wrist.

Question 9

Which is the most common joint involved in septic arthritis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Septic arthritis is an infection within a joint space, which is a rheumatologic emergency requiring prompt diagnosis and treatment to prevent irreversible joint destruction. The infection can occur in any joint but has a strong predilection for large, weight-bearing joints. In adults, the knee is the most commonly affected joint, accounting for approximately 50% of all cases of septic arthritis. The large size of the knee joint, its extensive synovial membrane, and its susceptibility to trauma and bacteremia from other sources contribute to its high incidence. Following the knee, the next most commonly involved joints are the hip, shoulder, ankle, and wrist. The most common causative organism is Staphylococcus aureus. The classic presentation is an acute onset of a single hot, swollen, and exquisitely painful joint with a restricted range of motion, often accompanied by systemic signs like fever. Diagnosis is confirmed by aspiration of the synovial fluid.

Question 12

What is meant by Perilunate dislocation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Perilunate dislocation is a severe and unstable wrist injury that results from high-energy trauma, typically a fall onto an outstretched, hyperextended hand. The injury involves the disruption of the ligaments that connect the carpal bones. The key to understanding this injury is the relationship between the radius, lunate, and capitate on a lateral wrist radiograph. Normally, these three bones are co-linear, forming a straight line. In a perilunate dislocation, the ligaments holding the capitate to the lunate are torn, and the capitate (along with the rest of the carpus) dislocates dorsally relative to the lunate. The lunate, however, maintains its normal alignment with the distal radius. Therefore, the lower radius and lunate remain in alignment, while the capitate alone is out of the plane (dislocated dorsally). If the injury progresses, the lunate can also dislocate, rotating volarly into the carpal tunnel, which is then called a lunate dislocation.

Question 14

Tom Smith’s septic arthritis refers to which condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Tom Smith's arthritis is an eponym for a particularly destructive form of septic arthritis of the hip joint that occurs in infancy. It was described by the surgeon Thomas Smith in 1874. The condition is characterized by a rapid and often complete destruction of the femoral head. In infants, the blood supply to the femoral head epiphysis passes through the joint capsule. An infection in the hip joint can cause a septic effusion that rapidly increases the intra-articular pressure. This pressure can compress the blood vessels, leading to avascular necrosis (death) of the femoral head. The infection can also directly destroy the cartilage. Because the femoral head in infants is largely cartilaginous, it can be completely destroyed by this process, leading to a pathological dislocation of the hip and severe long-term disability. This condition is an absolute surgical emergency requiring immediate drainage of the joint.

Question 20

A woman in her first trimester of pregnancy has a history of toxoplasmosis and is IgG positive. What advice should be given regarding continuation of the pregnancy?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The serological finding of a positive Toxoplasma IgG antibody and a negative IgM antibody (implied) in a woman before or in early pregnancy indicates a past, chronic infection. The presence of IgG signifies that the mother has immunity to toxoplasmosis. The greatest risk of congenital toxoplasmosis, which can cause severe fetal damage, occurs when a mother acquires a primary (new) infection during her pregnancy. In a woman who is already immune from a previous infection, the risk of reactivating the infection and transmitting it to the fetus is extremely low, almost negligible, unless the mother is severely immuno compromised (e.g., with advanced AIDS). Therefore, for an immunocompetent woman with evidence of a past infection, the advice is to reassure her and continue the pregnancy, as there is no significant risk of congenital transmission.

Question 28

Day 1 of 8-day cycle. Which hormone profile is correct?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Day 21 of a standard 28-day menstrual cycle falls in the mid-luteal phase. This phase occurs after ovulation (which happens around day 14). After ovulation, the ruptured follicle transforms into the corpus luteum. The corpus luteum is a temporary endocrine gland that produces large amounts of progesterone (P) and, to a lesser extent, estrogen (E). Therefore, in the mid-luteal phase, both progesterone and estrogen levels are elevated. Progesterone is the dominant hormone in this phase, responsible for preparing the endometrium for implantation. The high levels of both estrogen and progesterone exert a strong negative feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary gland, which suppresses the release of gonadotropins. At how many weeks of gestation can nuchal translucency be assessed on ultrasonography?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The nuchal translucency (NT) measurement is a key component of the first-trimester screening for fetal aneuploidies, particularly Down syndrome (Trisomy 21). The NT is the sonographic appearance of a collection of fluid under the skin behind the fetal neck. This measurement is performed using a transabdominal or transvaginal ultrasound during a very specific and narrow gestational window. The optimal time to measure the NT is between 11 weeks and 13 weeks + 6 days of gestation. During this period, the fetal crown-rump length (CRL) should be between 45 mm and 84 mm. An increased NT thickness during this specific timeframe is associated with an increased risk of chromosomal abnormalities, congenital heart defects, and other genetic syndromes. The measurement is not valid or interpretable outside of this specific gestational window.

Question 126

What is the preferred first-line treatment for mastitis in a breastfeeding mother?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Lactational mastitis is an infection of the breast tissue that most commonly occurs in breast feeding mothers. The most frequent causative organism is Staphylococcus aureus, which enters the breast tissue through a crack or fissure in the nipple. The treatment of mastitis involves both supportive measures (effective milk removal, warm compresses) and antibiotic therapy. The choice of antibiotic must provide coverage for S. aureus. Dicloxacillin or cloxacillin (in regions where it is available) are anti-staphylococcal penicillins and are considered the first-line treatment for uncomplicated lactational mastitis. They are effective against methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA) and are safe for the breastfeeding infant. Cephalexin is another good first-line option. If community-acquired MRSA is a concern, clindamycin or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole would be used. Ceftriaxone and ampicillin do not provide reliable coverage for penicillinase-producing S. aureus.
