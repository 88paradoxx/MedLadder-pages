Question 1

What is the most frequent reason for performing liver transplantation in pediatric patients?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The indications for liver transplantation in children differ from those in adults. In the pediatric population, the single most common indication for liver transplantation worldwide is biliary atresia. Biliary atresia is a rare neonatal liver disease characterized by the progressive, idiopathic fibrotic obliteration of the extrahepatic bile ducts. This leads to a complete obstruction of bile flow, resulting in cholestatic jaundice, cirrhosis, and ultimately end-stage liver failure. While the initial surgical management is a Kasai portoenterostomy to attempt to restore bile flow, this procedure is not curative for many patients. The progressive liver damage and development of portal hypertension often continue, and liver transplantation is ultimately required for long-term survival. Biliary atresia accounts for approximately half of all pediatric liver transplants.

Question 2

Omphalocele is caused by :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

An omphalocele is a congenital ventral abdominal wall defect characterized by the herniation of abdominal viscera through the umbilical ring. The herniated organs are covered by a protective sac composed of the amnion, Wharton's jelly, and the peritoneum. The underlying embryological cause of an omphalocele is the failure of the midgut to return to the abdominal cavity after its normal physiological herniation. During early fetal development (around the 6th week of gestation), the midgut grows rapidly and herniates out into the umbilical cord. This is a normal process. The gut then rotates and should return completely to the abdominal cavity by the 10th-12th week. An omphalocele occurs when this return fails to happen, leaving some of the abdominal organs (most commonly the liver and intestines) outside the abdominal cavity, contained within the umbilical cord sac.

Question 4

What is the most frequent cause of chronic pancreatitis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Chronic pancreatitis is a progressive inflammatory disease of the pancreas characterized by irreversible destruction of the pancreatic parenchyma, fibrosis, and loss of both exocrine and endocrine function. While there are numerous causes, the most common etiology by a significant margin in the Western world and many other parts of the globe is chronic and heavy alcohol consumption. Alcohol abuse is responsible for approximately 70-80% of all cases of chronic pancreatitis. Alcohol is believed to be directly toxic to pancreatic acinar cells and to alter the composition of pancreatic secretions, leading to the formation of protein plugs that obstruct small pancreatic ducts. This obstruction and direct toxicity initiate a cycle of inflammation and fibrosis that eventually destroys the gland. Gallstones are the most common cause of acute pancreatitis, but not chronic. Pancreatic calculi are a consequence, not a primary cause. Pancreas divisum is a congenital anomaly that is a less common cause.

Question 7

Cushing's ulcer is seen in cases of :

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

A Cushing's ulcer is a specific type of acute stress ulcer that occurs in the stomach or duodenum of patients who have sustained a severe intracranial injury, such as a traumatic head injury, brain tumor, or intracranial hemorrhage. The pathophysiology is thought to be related to direct stimulation of the vagal nuclei in the brainstem due to increased intracranial pressure. This vagal stimulation leads to hypersecretion of gastric acid (hyperchlorhydria), which then overwhelms the protective mucosal defenses and causes deep, often perforating ulcers. These ulcers are typically single and are most common in the stomach, duodenum, or esophagus. This is in contrast to a Curling's ulcer, which is another type of stress ulcer that is seen in patients with severe burns (A) and is caused by hypovolemia and mucosal ischemia.

Question 12

In which condition is the Ankle–Brachial Pressure Index (ABPI) falsely elevated? MEDINK CHAPTER 12_SURGERY

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The Ankle-Brachial Pressure Index (ABPI or ABI) is a simple, non-invasive test used to assess for peripheral artery disease (PAD). It is calculated by dividing the systolic blood pressure at the ankle by the systolic blood pressure in the arm. A normal ABI is between 1.0 and 1.4. An ABI < 0.9 is diagnostic of PAD, with the value correlating to disease severity (intermittent claudication, ischemic ulcers). However, in some patients, particularly those with long-standing diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or advanced age, the arteries of the lower leg (tibial arteries) can become heavily calcified and hardened due to medial arterial calcification (Mönckeberg's sclerosis). These calcified arteries are non-compressible. When a blood pressure cuff is inflated around the ankle, it cannot fully compress the rigid artery, leading to a falsely and artificially elevated ankle pressure reading. This can result in a misleadingly normal or even abnormally high ABI (e.g., > 1.4), masking the presence of severe underlying PAD.

Question 14

When a mother donates a kidney to her son, this type of transplant is referred to as what?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Transplants are classified based on the genetic relationship between the donor and the recipient. An allograft, or allogeneic transplant, is the transplantation of cells, tissues, or organs between two genetically non-identical individuals of the same species. A kidney transplant from a mother to her son falls into this category. Since the mother and son are not genetically identical (the son inherits half of his genes from his mother and half from his father), their human leukocyte antigens (HLA) will be different. This genetic disparity means the son's immune system will recognize the mother's kidney as foreign and will mount an immune response to reject it. Therefore, allograft transplantation requires lifelong immunosuppressive medication to prevent rejection. An isograft (A) is between identical twins. An autograft (C) is tissue from the same individual. A xenograft (D) is a transplant between different species.

Question 15

Which of the following statements about Barrett’s esophagus is not correct?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Barrett's esophagus is a condition where the normal stratified squamous epithelium of the distal esophagus is replaced by a specialized columnar epithelium containing goblet cells. This is an example of metaplasia (D), which occurs as a protective adaptation to chronic injury from gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). This metaplastic tissue is prone to developing complications. It can become eroded by acid, leading to the formation of a Barrett's ulcer, a type of peptic ulcer (B). Question 16

Which tissue layers are incised during a fasciotomy?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

A fasciotomy is an emergency surgical procedure performed to treat acute compartment syndrome. Compartment syndrome occurs when increased pressure within a confined fascial compartment compromises the circulation and function of the tissues within that space, potentially leading to muscle and nerve necrosis. The goal of the procedure is to relieve this pressure by decompressing the affected compartment(s). To achieve this, the surgeon must make an incision through all the layers that are confining the swollen muscle. This involves making a long incision through the skin, followed by the underlying subcutaneous tissue and the superficial fascia. The most critical step is then to incise the tough, inelastic deep fascia that encases the muscle compartment. This final incision allows the swollen muscle to bulge out, immediately relieving the intra compartmental pressure and restoring blood flow. Merely incising the skin and subcutaneous layers would be insufficient to decompress the muscle compartment, which is the primary goal of the procedure.

Question 35

At what age does the brain undergo its maximum growth?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The human brain undergoes its most rapid period of growth during the first few years of life. At birth, the brain is about 25% of its adult weight. This increases dramatically, reaching about 75% of its adult weight by the age of 2 years. By age 5, it has reached approximately 90% of its adult size. This postnatal growth is not due to an increase in the number of neurons (which is largely complete at birth) but rather to an explosive increase in myelination, synaptogenesis (the formation of connections between neurons), and the proliferation of glial cells. This early period is a critical window for cognitive, motor, and social-emotional development, and it highlights the importance of proper nutrition and environmental stimulation during infancy and toddlerhood for optimal brain development.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The combination of episodic icterus (jaundice) and pallor (from anemia) in a child suggests a chronic hemolytic anemia. One of the most common inherited hemolytic anemias is Hereditary Spherocytosis (HS). This is a disorder of the red blood cell membrane cytoskeleton, which causes the cells to become spherical rather than biconcave discs. These spherocytes are less deformable and are prematurely destroyed in the spleen, leading to chronic hemolysis, anemia, jaundice, and often splenomegaly. The classic diagnostic laboratory test for HS is the osmotic fragility test. This test demonstrates that the spherocytic red cells have increased fragility and will lyse more readily when placed in hypotonic saline solutions compared to normal red blood cells.

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

McArdle disease is Glycogen Storage Disease Type V. It is a metabolic myopathy caused by a deficiency of the enzyme myophosphorylase, which is the muscle isoform of glycogen phosphorylase. This enzyme is responsible for the first step of glycogenolysis (the breakdown of glycogen) in skeletal muscle. In McArdle disease, the muscles are unable to break down their glycogen stores to produce glucose for energy. This leads to the classic symptoms of exercise intolerance, severe muscle fatigue, cramping, and pain that occur shortly after starting strenuous activity. Patients often experience a second wind phenomenon if they rest and allow blood-borne fuels like glucose and free fatty acids to reach the muscles. A failure of serum lactate to rise after ischemic forearm exercise is a characteristic diagnostic finding. Glucose-6-phosphatase (A) is deficient in Von Gierke disease. Question 45

A mother of a newborn is concerned because her baby has not passed urine on the first postnatal day. What advice should be given to her?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

It is quite common for a healthy, full-term newborn not to pass urine for the first 12 to 24 hours of life. Approximately 99% of newborns will void within the first 48 hours. During the first few days, the mother produces colostrum, which is low in volume but rich in nutrients and antibodies. The infant's fluid intake is minimal until the mother's milk comes in around day 3-5. As long as the baby is otherwise well, feeding effectively, and has no other signs of illness or congenital anomalies (like posterior urethral valves), the failure to pass urine on Day 1 is not typically a cause for alarm. The appropriate advice is to reassure the mother, encourage her to continue breastfeeding on demand to ensure adequate hydration, and observe the infant. Intervention with formula (A), IV fluids (C), or NICU admission (D) is not indicated unless there are other concerning signs or the anuria persists beyond 48 hours.
