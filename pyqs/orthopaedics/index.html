Question 1

In a child whose height-for-age Z-score is less than − standard deviations, what is the most likely underlying cause?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Anthropometric indices are used to assess the nutritional status of children. A height-for-age Z-score that is less than -2 standard deviations below the WHO Child Growth Standards median is the definition of stunting. Stunting is an indicator of chronic malnutrition. It reflects a cumulative, long-term process of inadequate nutrition and/or recurrent infections that has hindered a childs linear growth potential over an extended period. In contrast, acute malnutrition is defined by wasting (low weight-for-height) or nutritional edema, which reflects a more recent and severe nutritional deficit. Therefore, a low height-for-age Z-score is the specific marker for chronic malnutrition.

Question 3

Correct answer: A

The combination of physical features described—short stature, a webbed neck (pterygium colli), and a low posterior hairline—is the classic clinical triad for Turner syndrome. Turner syndrome is a chromosomal disorder affecting females, in which all or part of one of the X chromosomes is missing (monosomy X, 45,X0). Other associated features can include a broad chest with widely spaced nipples, cubitus valgus, and congenital heart defects (most commonly coarctation of the aorta or a bicuspid aortic valve). The diagnosis is confirmed by karyotyping. The other syndromes listed are autosomal trisomies with different and more severe clinical phenotypes.

Question 5

A 0-year-old boy presented with complaints of recurrent bleeding and a past history of joint pains. There was no history of mucosal bleeding. Deficiency of which coagulation factor is most likely responsible for this condition?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The clinical presentation of recurrent bleeding into joints (hemarthrosis), leading to joint pain, in a male patient is the classic picture of hemophilia. The absence of significant mucosal bleeding makes von Willebrand disease (vWD) less likely, as mucocutaneous bleeding is its hallmark. Hemophilia A is an X-linked recessive disorder caused by a deficiency of Factor VIII, while Hemophilia B is an X-linked recessive disorder caused by a deficiency of Factor IX. Both factors are critical components of the intrinsic coagulation cascade. Their deficiency impairs clot formation, leading to the characteristic deep tissue and joint hemorrhages. A lupus anticoagulant (D) is typically associated with thrombosis, not a bleeding disorder.

Question 6

Chloride level is tested in which disease?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The measurement of chloride concentration in sweat is the gold standard diagnostic test for Cystic Fibrosis (CF). CF is a genetic disorder caused by mutations in the CFTR gene, which codes for a chloride ion channel. A defective channel impairs chloride transport across epithelial cells. In the sweat ducts, this defect prevents the reabsorption of chloride from sweat, resulting in an abnormally high sweat chloride concentration. A level greater than 60 mmol/L is considered diagnostic. The other listed conditions are not diagnosed by a chloride test; Gauchers disease is diagnosed via enzyme assay, Osteogenesis Imperfecta by genetic testing or clinical findings, and Liddle Syndrome by genetic testing and clinical response to specific diuretics.

Question 10

Which chromosomal karyotype is typically associated with true hermaphroditism?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

True hermaphroditism, now more appropriately termed ovotesticular disorder of sex development (DSD), is a rare condition defined by the histological presence of both ovarian tissue (containing follicles) and testicular tissue (containing seminiferous tubules) in the same individual. These tissues may be combined in a single gonad, known as an ovotestis, or the person may have a separate ovary and testis. While several different karyotypes can be associated with this condition, the most common karyotype found in individuals with ovotesticular DSD is 46,XX, accounting for approximately 60% of cases. The development of testicular tissue in a 46,XX individual is often due to the translocation of the SRY (sex-determining region Y) gene onto an X chromosome or an autosome, or mutations in other genes involved in the sex determination pathway. 45,XO (A) is the karyotype for Turner syndrome.

Correct answer: C

The APGAR score is a rapid assessment tool used to evaluate the clinical status of a newborn infant at 1 and 5 minutes after birth. It scores five parameters (Appearance, Pulse, Grimace, Activity, Respiration) on a scale of 0 to 2 each, for a maximum total score of 10. The score is used to guide the need for resuscitation. The infant may require some assistance, such as stimulation and supplemental oxygen.0-3 : Severely depressed. The infant is in critical condition and requires immediate and intensive resuscitation, including positive pressure ventilation and possibly chest compressions and medications.An APGAR score of 3 at 1 minute clearly indicates that the neonate is severely depressed and in urgent need of life-saving resuscitation measures to support their breathing and circulation.

Question 13

Which fontanelle is the last to close?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Fontanelles are the soft, membranous gaps between the cranial bones in an infant's skull, which allow for the growth of the brain and the molding of the head during birth. There are six fontanelles at birth. The posterior fontanelle is triangular and typically closes early, by about 2 to 3 months of age. The anterolateral (sphenoidal) fontanelles on the sides of the skull usually close by about 6 months of age. The posterolateral (mastoid) fontanelles close later, between 6 and 18 months. The anterior fontanelle is the largest, diamond-shaped fontanelle, commonly known as the soft spot. It is the last one to close, with the process of ossification typically being completed between 18 and 24 months of age. Its persistence beyond this age may indicate an underlying medical condition. Because it remains open for the longest period, the anterior fontanelle is a crucial landmark for clinical assessment in infants.

Question 15

According to WHO criteria, how is severe acute malnutrition (SAM) defined?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) in children aged 6 to 59 months by the presence of at least one of three criteria. One of the key anthropometric criteria is severe wasting. Wasting is a measure of acute weight loss and is assessed by the weight-for-height index. A child is diagnosed with SAM if their weight-for-height is less than -3 standard deviations (-3 SD) below the WHO Child Growth Standards median. This indicates a severe and life-threatening degree of acute malnutrition. The other two independent criteria for diagnosing SAM are the presence of bilateral pitting edema of nutritional origin, or a mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) of less than 11.5 cm. Weight-for-age is an indicator of being underweight but is not used to define SAM.

Question 22

Which vaccine has to be given every year?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The influenza vaccine is unique among routine vaccines in that it is recommended to be administered every year. There are two main reasons for this. First, the immunity conferred by the influenza vaccine wanes over time, and annual revaccination is needed to maintain protection. Second, and more importantly, the influenza virus is constantly evolving through processes called antigenic drift and antigenic shift. Antigenic drift involves small, gradual changes in the virus's surface antigens (hemagglutinin and neuraminidase). These changes result in new viral strains emerging each year that the immune system may not recognize from previous infections or vaccinations. Therefore, the composition of the influenza vaccine must be updated annually to match the strains that are predicted to be circulating in the upcoming flu season. This requirement for an annual update is specific to the influenza vaccine.

Question 25

A 7-year-old patient presents with chronic low back pain that improves with activity and is associated with early morning stiffness, along with a past history of anterior uveitis. X-ray imaging of the sacroiliac joints is normal. What is the most appropriate next step in evaluation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

This young patient's clinical presentation is highly suggestive of an inflammatory spondyloarthropathy, with ankylosing spondylitis (AS) being the most likely diagnosis. The key features are the inflammatory nature of the back pain (improves with activity, worse with rest, morning stiffness) and the history of an extra-articular manifestation, acute anterior uveitis. In the early stages of the disease, plain X-rays of the sacroiliac (SI) joints are often normal, as it can take several years for the characteristic changes of sacroiliitis (erosions and sclerosis) to become visible. The most sensitive imaging modality for detecting early inflammatory changes is a Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the sacroiliac joints. An MRI with STIR (Short Tau Inversion Recovery) sequences can detect active inflammation in the form of bone marrow edema in the subchondral bone of the SI joints, a finding known as sacroiliitis, long before any changes are visible on an X-ray.

Correct answer: B

Question 27

A patient presents with sensory loss over the thenar eminence and the lateral three and a half fingers. At what anatomical site is the injury most likely located?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The sensory distribution described (lateral 3 1/2 fingers) is classic for the median nerve. However, the involvement of the thenar eminence (the fleshy part at the base of the thumb) is the key to localizing the lesion. The palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve, which supplies sensation to the thenar eminence, arises from the median nerve in the forearm, proximal to the carpal tunnel. It then travels superficial to the transverse carpal ligament to supply the skin. In Carpal Tunnel Syndrome (A), the main median nerve is compressed within the tunnel, but this superficial palmar cutaneous branch is spared. Therefore, sensation over the thenar eminence is typically preserved. The presence of sensory loss in both the fingers and the thenar eminence indicates a more proximal lesion of the median nerve at a higher level, such as in the forearm or at the elbow.

Question 29

A woman reports nocturnal leg pain accompanied by a crawling or uncomfortable sensation and has a clinical diagnosis of restless leg syndrome. What is the drug of choice for her treatment?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

Restless Legs Syndrome (RLS), also known as Willis-Ekbom disease, is a neurological sensorimotor disorder. Its pathophysiology is strongly linked to dysfunction in the central dopaminergic pathways, as well as impaired iron metabolism in the brain. For patients with persistent, moderate-to-severe RLS that is not caused by a correctable underlying condition (like iron deficiency), the first-line pharmacological treatment is a low dose of a dopamine agonist. Pramipexole and ropinirole are non-ergot dopamine agonists that are highly effective in controlling the symptoms of RLS and are considered drugs of choice for initiating therapy. They are typically taken 1-3 hours before bedtime to manage the characteristic nocturnal symptoms. While alpha-2-delta ligands like gabapentin (B) are also effective and are considered an alternative first-line or second-line option, dopamine agonists are the classic first-line treatment. Correcting any underlying iron deficiency (D) is also a crucial part of management.

Question 31

Fracture of which part of a bone leads to disturbance of its linear growth?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The epiphyseal plate, also known as the physis or growth plate, is the area of growing cartilage located between the epiphysis and metaphysis of long bones in children and adolescents. Linear (longitudinal) growth of the bone occurs at this plate through a process of endochondral ossification, where cartilage cells proliferate and are progressively replaced by bone. A fracture that extends into or across the epiphyseal plate (classified by the Salter-Harris system) can damage the delicate columns of proliferating chondrocytes. This damage can disrupt the normal growth process, potentially leading to premature fusion of the plate and subsequent growth arrest. This can result in a limb length discrepancy or an angular deformity as the bone grows asymmetrically. Fractures confined to the diaphysis, metaphysis, or epiphysis that do not involve the growth plate do not affect linear growth.

Question 33

Most common complication of untreated radial head dislocation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

An untreated or missed dislocation of the radial head in a child, especially when associated with a fracture of the ulna (a Monteggia fracture-dislocation), can lead to long-term deformity and functional impairment. The dislocation disrupts the normal mechanics of the elbow joint and can affect the growth of the radius. Over time, this disruption can lead to a progressive cubitus valgus deformity. This is a condition where the extended forearm is angled away from the body, resulting in an increased carrying angle. The valgus deformity develops due to relative overgrowth of the radius or undergrowth of the lateral humeral condyle. Cubitus varus (A), or a gunstock deformity, is typically a complication of a malunited supracondylar humerus fracture, not a radial head dislocation. Nerve damage and myositis ossificans are possible but cubitus valgus is the most characteristic late deformity.
