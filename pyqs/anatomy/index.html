Question 1

What are the contents of Umbilical cord?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The umbilical cord is the lifeline connecting the fetus to the placenta. In a normal, fully developed umbilical cord, there are three blood vessels encased in a gelatinous substance called Wharton's jelly. These vessels are two umbilical arteries and one umbilical vein. The two umbilical arteries carry deoxygenated blood and waste products away from the fetus to the placenta. The single umbilical vein carries oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood from the placenta to the fetus. Initially, there are two umbilical veins, but the right umbilical vein typically obliterates early in fetal development, leaving only the left one to persist. The presence of only a single umbilical artery instead of two can sometimes be associated with other congenital anomalies.

Question 3

A girl presents with a foot injury following a history of forceful eversion of the foot. Which structure is most likely to be affected?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Ankle sprains are common injuries, but the ligaments affected depend on the mechanism of injury. The vast majority of ankle sprains (over 85%) are inversion injuries, where the foot rolls inward, stressing the ligaments on the lateral (outer) side of the ankle. These are the anterior talofibular ligament (A), the posterior talofibular ligament (C), and the calcaneofibular ligament (D). In contrast, a forceful eversion injury, where the foot is twisted outward, puts stress on the ligaments on the medial (inner) side of the ankle. The primary stabilizer on the medial side is the deltoid ligament. This is a very strong, fan-shaped ligament complex that connects the medial malleolus of the tibia to the talus, calcaneus, and navicular bones. A significant eversion force is required to sprain or tear the deltoid ligament.

Question 4

A patient presents with muscle wasting, hypotonia, and weakness of the left hand, with impaired fine motor skills such as kneading dough. Which anatomical structure is most likely affected?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The patient's symptoms—muscle wasting (atrophy), reduced tone (hypotonia/flaccidity), and weakness—are the classic signs of a lower motor neuron (LMN) lesion. LMNs are the final common pathway connecting the central nervous system to the muscles. The cell bodies of the LMNs that innervate the limbs are located in the anterior (ventral) horn of the spinal cord. Since the symptoms are confined to the left hand, the lesion must be affecting the anterior horn cells in the cervical spinal cord that supply the muscles of that hand. The corticospinal tracts decussate (cross over) in the medulla. Therefore, a lesion in the right cerebral hemisphere would cause deficits on the left side of the body. While a pyramidal or internal capsule lesion (B, C, D) would cause left-sided weakness, these are all upper motor neuron lesions, which would result in spasticity and hyperreflexia, not atrophy and reduced tone.

Question 5

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve has a clinically important and vulnerable course. After emerging from the parotid gland, it runs forward, deep to the platysma muscle, along or just below the inferior border of the mandible. It has a crucial relationship with the submandibular gland and the posterior belly of the digastric muscle. Question 6

What is the condition or stage of the oocyte after ovulation?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

During fetal life, primary oocytes begin meiosis but are arrested in prophase I. This state persists until puberty. Just prior to ovulation, a surge of Luteinizing Hormone (LH) triggers the completion of meiosis I, resulting in an unequal division that produces a large secondary oocyte and a small first polar body. The secondary oocyte then immediately enters meiosis II but is arrested in metaphase II. It is this secondary oocyte, halted at metaphase II, that is expelled from the ovary during ovulation. The meiotic process will only resume and complete if the oocyte is successfully fertilized by a sperm. Therefore, the oocyte available for fertilization post-ovulation is a secondary oocyte arrested in metaphase II. Option A describes the pre-pubertal state, while options B and C are incorrect stages of meiotic arrest.

Question 7

Fracture at which site affects the longitudinal growth of a bone?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The longitudinal growth of long bones in children and adolescents occurs at the epiphyseal plate, also known as the growth plate or physis. This cartilaginous plate is situated between the epiphysis (the end of the bone) and the metaphysis. Growth occurs through a process of endochondral ossification, where cartilage cells within the plate proliferate, mature, and are eventually replaced by bone, thereby elongating the bone shaft (diaphysis). A fracture that involves the epiphyseal plate (a Salter-Harris fracture) can disrupt this delicate process. Damage to the proliferating chondrocytes can lead to premature cessation of growth, resulting in limb length discrepancy or angular deformities. Fractures of the diaphysis (Option B), epiphysis (Option C), or metaphysis (Option D) that do not involve the growth plate do not typically interfere with the bone's longitudinal growth potential.

Question 10

Correct answer: A

Horner syndrome results from a disruption of the oculosympathetic pathway, leading to a triad of miosis, ptosis, and anhidrosis. The ptosis seen in this syndrome is typically mild (1-2 mm). Question 12

A singer has difficulty producing high-pitched sounds, and examination reveals bowing of the right vocal cord. Injury to which muscle is most likely responsible for this finding?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The pitch of the voice is determined by the tension and length of the vocal cords. To produce high-pitched sounds, the vocal cords must be tensed and elongated. This action is the primary function of the cricothyroid muscle. It is the only intrinsic laryngeal muscle innervated by the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve. Contraction of the cricothyroid muscle tilts the thyroid cartilage forward, thereby stretching the vocal cords. Paralysis of this muscle, as suggested by the bowing of the right vocal cord and the inability to reach high pitches, indicates a lesion of the right external superior laryngeal nerve. The posterior cricoarytenoid (Option A) abducts (opens) the cords, the lateral cricoarytenoid (Option B) adducts (closes) them, and the thyroarytenoid (Option D) shortens and relaxes the cords, lowering the pitch.

Question 14

The membrane covering an omphalocele is formed from which embryological layer or tissue?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

An omphalocele is characterized by the herniation of abdominal viscera through a midline defect at the umbilicus, with the contents being covered by a protective sac. This sac is a bilaminar structure. The outer layer of the sac is composed of amnion, which is the innermost membrane of the placenta that encloses the embryo. The inner layer of the sac is the peritoneum. Between these two layers is Wharton's jelly. This covering distinguishes it from gastroschisis, where the herniated viscera are exposed directly to the amniotic fluid without a sac. The chorion (Option B) is the outermost fetal membrane and is not involved. Mesoderm (Option C) and endoderm (Option D) are embryonic germ layers that give rise to the organs themselves but not the specific outer covering of the omphalocele sac.

Question 15

In a patient who has fecal matter discharging from the umbilicus, what is the most probable diagnosis?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The discharge of fecal matter from the umbilicus indicates a patent connection between the intestine and the umbilicus, known as a vitelline fistula or omphalomesenteric duct fistula. This duct normally connects the midgut to the yolk sac in the embryo and obliterates by the seventh week of gestation. A persistent remnant of this duct on the intestinal side is known as Meckel's diverticulum, which is a type of ileal diverticulum. When the entire duct remains patent, it forms a fistula. Therefore, the underlying pathology for a vitelline fistula is a persistent ileal diverticulum connected to the umbilicus. A urachal fistula (Option A) connects the bladder to the umbilicus and would present with urine discharge. Omphalocele (Option C) and gastroschisis (Option D) are congenital abdominal wall defects, not fistulas presenting with discharge in this manner.

Sciatica occurs due to irritation or compression of the sciatic nerve, which commonly causes pain along the posterior thigh and may affect the hamstring muscles. Question 27

Which blood vessel is responsible for carrying deoxygenated blood from the fetus back to the placenta?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

In fetal circulation, the roles of the umbilical arteries and vein are reversed compared to systemic circulation in an adult. The umbilical artery, of which there are two, carries deoxygenated blood and waste products from the fetus to the placenta for oxygenation and removal. Conversely, the single umbilical vein (B) carries highly oxygenated and nutrient-rich blood from the placenta to the fetus. Question 29

Which nerve is responsible for mediating the gag reflex?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The gag reflex (or pharyngeal reflex) involves two key cranial nerves. The afferent (sensory) limb of the reflex is mediated by the 9th cranial nerve (glossopharyngeal nerve). When the posterior pharynx, tonsils, or base of the tongue is touched, sensory signals are transmitted via the glossopharyngeal nerve to the brainstem. Question 32

The elongated path of the left recurrent laryngeal nerve is a result of the persistence of which embryonic aortic arch?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: D

The asymmetrical course of the right and left recurrent laryngeal nerves is a direct consequence of the embryological development of the aortic arches. Both nerves initially hook around the 6th aortic arch on their respective sides. On the right side, the distal portion of the 6th arch and the entire 5th arch artery degenerate. This allows the right recurrent laryngeal nerve to ascend and hook underneath the next persistent structure, which is the 4th aortic arch artery (forming the right subclavian artery). On the left side, however, the distal part of the 6th aortic arch persists to form the ductus arteriosus (which later becomes the ligamentum arteriosum). Because this structure remains, the left recurrent laryngeal nerve is trapped and cannot ascend. It is held down in the thorax, where it loops under the arch of the aorta (formed by the left 4th arch) adjacent to the ligamentum arteriosum. This explains its longer, recurrent course from the chest back up to the larynx.

Question 35

Which of the following structures in females is a remnant of the Wolffian duct?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

In embryonic development, males and females initially have both Wolffian (mesonephric) and Müllerian (paramesonephric) ducts. Question 36

Claudication resulting from popliteal-femoral incompetence primarily affects which area?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

Claudication refers to muscle pain or cramping that is induced by exercise and relieved by rest. It is a hallmark symptom of peripheral artery disease (PAD), caused by insufficient blood flow to meet the metabolic demands of the exercising muscle. The location of the claudication pain directly corresponds to the muscle group distal to the arterial stenosis or occlusion. The femoropopliteal arterial segment, comprising the superficial femoral artery and the popliteal artery, is the most common site of atherosclerotic disease in the lower limbs. Question 37

A patient shows hyperextension at the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints and flexion at the interphalangeal (IP) joints. Which muscle is most likely paralyzed?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The posture described is a classic claw hand deformity. This deformity arises from an imbalance between the extrinsic and intrinsic muscles of the hand. The intrinsic hand muscles, specifically the lumbricals and interossei, are responsible for flexing the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joints and extending the interphalangeal (IP) joints. When these intrinsic muscles are paralyzed (typically due to a combined ulnar and median nerve injury), their actions are lost. This allows the unopposed action of the extrinsic muscles to dominate. The long extensor, extensor digitorum, pulls the MCP joints into hyperextension. Simultaneously, the long flexors, flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus, pull the IP joints into flexion. This combination of MCP hyperextension and IP flexion creates the characteristic claw-like appearance. Therefore, paralysis of the interossei and lumbricals is the direct cause of this deformity.

Question 41

In fetal circulation, which location has the highest oxygen concentration?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

In fetal circulation, oxygenation occurs at the placenta, not the lungs. Highly oxygenated and nutrient-rich blood travels from the placenta to the fetus via the single umbilical vein. The umbilical vein enters the abdomen and sends a major portion of its blood through a shunt called the ductus venosus, which bypasses the liver and drains directly into the inferior vena cava (IVC). This freshly oxygenated blood from the placenta mixes with the deoxygenated blood returning from the lower body of the fetus within the IVC. Despite this mixing, the segment of the IVC just before it enters the right atrium contains the most highly oxygenated blood found anywhere in the fetal body. This stream is preferentially shunted across the foramen ovale into the left atrium to supply the fetal brain and heart. The superior vena cava (A) carries only deoxygenated blood from the upper body.

Question 43

In which organ is the Space of Disse located?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The Space of Disse, or the perisinusoidal space, is a unique anatomical location found within the liver. It is the microscopic space situated between the basolateral surface of the hepatocytes (liver cells) and the fenestrated endothelial lining of the liver sinusoids (specialized capillaries). This space is filled with blood plasma that filters through the fenestrations of the sinusoidal endothelium. The Space of Disse is a critical site for the metabolic exchange between the blood and the hepatocytes; plasma solutes can directly contact the microvilli of the hepatocytes, allowing for efficient uptake of nutrients and secretion of proteins like albumin. It also contains the hepatic stellate cells (also known as Ito cells), which are responsible for storing Vitamin A and are involved in the pathogenesis of liver fibrosis. This perisinusoidal space is a feature unique to the liver's microarchitecture.

Question 50

The ducts of Bellini are found in which organ?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

The ducts of Bellini, also known as the papillary ducts, are anatomical structures found within the kidney. They represent the most distal part of the collecting duct system. The collecting ducts from multiple nephrons progressively merge in the renal medulla to form larger and larger ducts. The final, large-caliber ducts that are formed just before the collecting system opens into the minor calyx at the tip of the renal papilla are called the ducts of Bellini. Question 53

Which nerve supplies the submandibular gland?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: B

The secretomotor innervation of the submandibular and sublingual salivary glands follows a complex parasympathetic pathway. The preganglionic parasympathetic fibers originate from the superior salivatory nucleus in the brainstem and travel with the facial nerve (CN VII). These fibers then branch off as the chorda tympani nerve, which joins the lingual nerve (a branch of the mandibular nerve, CN V3) in the infratemporal fossa. Question 58

Which muscle functions to tense the vocal folds?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: A

The pitch of the voice is primarily controlled by altering the tension and length of the vocal cords. The muscle solely responsible for tensing and elongating the vocal cords is the cricothyroid muscle. It is the only intrinsic laryngeal muscle located on the external surface of the larynx and is uniquely innervated by the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve. When the cricothyroid muscle contracts, it pulls the thyroid cartilage anteriorly and inferiorly, tilting it forward on the cricoid cartilage. This action increases the distance between the thyroid and arytenoid cartilages, thereby stretching and tensing the vocal ligaments. Tenser vocal cords vibrate at a higher frequency, resulting in the production of a higher-pitched sound. The thyroarytenoid muscles (C), particularly the vocalis part, have the opposite effect; they shorten and relax the vocal cords, lowering the pitch. The lateral cricoarytenoid (B) adducts the cords, and the posterior cricoarytenoid (D) abducts them. Hence, for vocal cord tension and high pitch, the cricothyroid is the key muscle.

Question 60

Which of the following statements about Weber’s syndrome is incorrect?

Show answer and explanation

Correct answer: C

Weber's syndrome is a midbrain stroke syndrome caused by an infarction in the territory of the posterior cerebral artery, specifically affecting the anterior cerebral peduncle (D). This area contains two crucial structures : the descending corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts, and the exiting fascicles of the oculomotor nerve (CN III).
